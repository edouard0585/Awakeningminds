History and traditions of meditation

Where the techniques you practice come from

A timeline in seven landmarks

Meditation belongs to no particular tradition: contemplative forms exist in almost every culture. The techniques in this library descend mainly from Indian and Buddhist lineages, reformulated in a fully secular way since the 1980s — notably by the MBSR program (Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction), which opened the way to their scientific validation and their use in educational, professional and institutional settings.

What are the main meditation traditions?

Four great families shaped most of the techniques you practice today. In India, the Vedic and yogic traditions developed mantra, breath control and concentration. Buddhism, born in northern India about 2,500 years ago, then spread across Asia and took many forms: Theravada in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia, Chan in China (which became Zen in Japan), and the Tibetan schools with their rich visualizations.

In China, Taoism cultivated stillness and slow, deep breathing. The great monotheistic religions have their own contemplative paths too: silent prayer in Christian monasteries, meditation in the Jewish Kabbalah, and dhikr in Sufism, the mystical branch of Islam, where the names of God are repeated again and again. Their goals differ — liberation, union with the divine, inner peace — but all of them train the same tool: attention.

What did the Buddha teach about meditation?

He taught two qualities to develop together: calm (samatha), which steadies the mind, and insight (vipassana), which sees clearly how every experience arises and passes. His teachings on mindfulness of breathing (Anapanasati), on the four foundations of mindfulness (Satipatthana) and on loving-kindness (Metta) remain the backbone of today's mindfulness. He also described meditation in four postures — sitting, walking, standing and lying down — which is why walking meditation is just as traditional as sitting.

How did meditation reach the West?

Through teachers and books from Asia, mostly from the end of the 19th century. In 1893, the Indian monk Swami Vivekananda spoke at the Parliament of the World's Religions in Chicago and sparked wide interest in yoga and Indian philosophy. In the mid-20th century, the books of the Japanese scholar D.T. Suzuki made Zen known to a broad Western public.

Then came the boom of the 1960s and 1970s: Transcendental Meditation became famous when the Beatles studied with Maharishi Mahesh Yogi, Zen centers opened in the United States, and vipassana retreats spread across Europe and America. The Vietnamese Zen monk Thich Nhat Hanh, exiled in France, taught mindfulness in everyday life: washing the dishes, walking, breathing. In 1979, Jon Kabat-Zinn created MBSR at the University of Massachusetts.

When did scientists start studying meditation?

In earnest in the 1960s and 1970s. Researchers measured the breathing, heart rate and brain waves of yogis, Zen monks and people practicing Transcendental Meditation. In 1975, the Harvard cardiologist Herbert Benson popularized the “relaxation response,” the body's calming reaction that meditation can trigger. MBSR then gave researchers a standard eight-week program to test in controlled studies. Since the 2000s, brain imaging has shown how attention and stress circuits change with practice, and meditation has moved into mobile apps, the latest landmark of the timeline.

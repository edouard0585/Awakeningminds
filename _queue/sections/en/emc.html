Altered states of consciousness

Trance, astral travel, past lives, guides — understanding, experiencing, staying lucid

What is an altered state of consciousness (ASC)?

An altered state of consciousness is any state that departs from ordinary waking. Everyday examples: daydreaming, the drowsy reverie just before sleep (hypnagogia), the “flow” of an artist absorbed in their craft; deeper ones: dreams, hypnosis, trance, deep meditation. You already cross several every day without naming them. Traditions around the world have developed techniques to enter them voluntarily — and that's what this chapter explores.

What are altered states good for?

  • Radical relaxation: deep states rest the nervous system far beyond ordinary relaxation.
  • Creativity and problem-solving: the free imagery of the theta zone brings up associations, images and solutions inaccessible to the analytical mind (Dalí and Edison deliberately used hypnagogia).
  • Self-exploration: dialogue with your depths — memories, symbols, parts of yourself — in the form of inner images and stories.
  • Spiritual dimension: a sense of unity, meaning, connection — at the heart of all contemplative traditions.
  • Transformation: used in a structured professional setting (guided hypnosis), these states facilitate changes that willpower alone cannot achieve.
🔬 The two lenses — our compass for this whole chapterEvery practice in this chapter can be read two ways. Traditional lens: there are subtle planes, guides, memories of other lives, that these techniques allow us to contact. Psychological lens: these experiences are real and meaningful productions of your deep psyche — imagery, symbols, memory, active imagination — with no demonstrated external existence. Science documents the states (they are measurable) but not the metaphysical interpretations. Good news: the techniques and their benefits are the same under both readings. You don't have to pick a camp to practice — only stay honest with yourself.
Exploration 01

What is a meditative trance?

Intermediate⏱ 15–30 minTheta state · Absorption

Definition: a state of deep absorption where attention narrows or lets itself be carried, the critical mind steps back, and inner imagery becomes vivid and autonomous. It's the foundational state of this chapter: inner journey, meeting the guide, regression — everything begins with knowing how to enter a light trance.

How does the brain enter a trance?

The brain enters trance when given a repetitive, predictable stimulus (rhythm, repetition, swaying) or an intense, narrow focus (fixating on a point). The analytical mind, deprived of novelty to process, “disengages” — and makes room for deeper layers. That's why all cultures converged on the same tools: drum, repetitive chant, dance, flame gazing.

Step by step — self-induction of a light trance (safe method)

  1. Frame: quiet room, seated comfortably (not lying down: you want trance, not sleep), timer for 20 minutes, intention stated in one sentence: “I let myself descend into deep relaxation, and I return when I decide.”
  2. Breath descent: 10 breaths in 4-6, letting each exhale release one zone (jaw, shoulders, belly, hands).
  3. Fixation: place your gaze on a low point (or a candle 1 m away). Soft gaze, without exaggerated blinking. Let vision naturally blur.
  4. Countdown: eyes closed, count mentally from 10 to 1, going down “a floor” with each number — imagine a staircase, a light gently dimming.
  5. Signs you're there: heavy or floating body, distorted time, images that come on their own, distant sounds. Let it happen, observe without commenting.
  6. Stay: remain in this receptive state. You can run a visualization here (safe place, meeting the guide) or simply float.
  7. Coming back — always careful: count from 1 to 5 going up the staircase, move fingers and toes, stretch, open eyes, name 3 objects in the room aloud. Drink a glass of water.
💡 The golden rule of tranceA practice is judged by the quality of its return. A good session ends grounded, present, functional. If you feel “floaty” long after: shorten sessions and strengthen the return step (walking, food, physical contact with objects).
Exploration 02

What is astral travel, and is it real?

Advanced⏱ 30–60 min · at the threshold of sleepHypnagogia · Imagery

Traditional definition: astral travel, or an out-of-body experience, is the experience of leaving your physical body to move in a subtle form (the “astral body”), described in Western esotericism, in yoga (subtle bodies) and in many accounts across cultures.

What science says: out-of-body experiences (OBEs) are real as experiences — about one person in ten has one spontaneously, often at the threshold of sleep or during sleep paralysis. Neuroscience links them to the temporoparietal junction, the area that builds the sense of being “located” in the body: they can even be triggered in the lab. No verifiable perception at a distance has been demonstrated under controlled conditions, however. The experience is authentic and often striking; its interpretation remains open — our two lenses apply fully.

Why do people try astral travel?

Practitioners describe intense feelings of freedom and lightness, a lasting loss of the fear of death, a transformed relationship with the body, and an imaginal exploration of unmatched vividness. Under the psychological lens, it's an immersive experience of your own inner space; under the traditional lens, an exploration of subtle planes. In either case: an inner adventure, not an escape from reality.

Step by step — the classical method (relaxation → hypnagogia → separation)

  1. Ideal moment: early morning after 5–6 h of sleep (wake up, stay up 20 minutes, go back to bed) — the body falls back asleep quickly, the mind stays clear. This is the window in which spontaneous OBEs occur.
  2. Lying on your back, total stillness. Deep relaxation like yoga nidra: descending scan + 4-6 breaths, until complete heaviness.
  3. Let the body fall asleep, the mind awake: stay a passive observer. Hypnagogic signs appear: fleeting images, sounds, vibrations, sensations of floating or tipping. Don't react — excitement wakes you up, fear makes you tense. Observe like a spectator.
  4. At the heart of the vibrations, use a separation technique in imagination: the rope (feel yourself climbing a rope above you, hand over hand), the roll (roll gently out of the body as at the edge of a bed), or floating (let yourself rise like a cork). It's the sensory intention that counts, not effort.
  5. If the experience triggers, stay calm, explore gently, and remember: you can return at any moment by thinking of your hand or wanting to move a finger.
  6. Return and integration: move slowly, write everything down immediately (details fade like a dream), then ground yourself (light, water, movement).
⚠️ What you must know1 · Along the way, you may encounter sleep paralysis: body locked, sometimes oppressive presence sensations. It's a normal mechanism (the body is in sleep mode, the mind awake), harmless, ending on its own in seconds — breathe, try to move a toe, or let it pass. Knowing this ahead of time defuses 90% of the fear. 2 · These practices fragment sleep: keep them occasional (1–2 times/week maximum). 3 · Not recommended during periods of high anxiety or raw grief, or if you often feel cut off from yourself or from what's around you — the waking/sleeping boundary must remain a playground, not a refuge.
Exploration 03

What is past-life regression?

Advanced⏱ 30–45 min · in tranceRegression · Symbolic

Traditional definition: “past-life regression” is a technique — generally guided by a practitioner, under hypnosis — that seeks to explore memories of earlier lives. It comes from worldviews based on reincarnation (Hinduism, Buddhism, spiritism…), in which the soul goes through several existences and some memories are said to remain accessible in altered states.

What science says: no past-life memory has been validated under controlled conditions, and hypnosis is precisely the state in which the brain most easily fabricates strikingly realistic false memories (cryptomnesia: elements from forgotten films, readings or stories reassemble into a “memory”). This is THE point of vigilance for this whole chapter: live the experience if it draws you, but never take its content as a biographical or historical fact.

What can a past-life exploration bring you?

Mostly self-knowledge. Read symbolically, a “past life” is a story your depths stage — like a very structured dream. And like a dream, it speaks about you: the unexplained fear, the recurring pattern, the untapped talent take the form of a story. Many practitioners gain real insights (“that character who never dares to leave, that's me”) — the value is in the mirror, not the archaeology.

Step by step — solo symbolic exploration

  1. Ask a real-life question before starting: “where does my fear of water come from?”, “what keeps replaying in my relationships?”. That's your guiding thread.
  2. Enter a light trance (Trance card method: breath descent + 10 to 1 staircase).
  3. The corridor of doors: visualize a benevolent corridor lined with doors. One of them “answers” your question — let yourself be drawn without choosing mentally.
  4. Pass through the door and describe: look at your feet (shoes? floor?), your clothes, the place, the era. Let the story unfold in scenes, as observer: who is this character, what do they live, what do they fear, what do they love?
  5. Look for the message, not the dates: ask inwardly “what does this story have to tell me, here and now?”. That's the only question that counts.
  6. Close the door with thanks, go back down the corridor, count from 1 to 5, return fully. Write down the story and above all its message.
⚠️ Be especially carefulIf you see a practitioner: walk away from anyone who asserts the historical authenticity of what emerges, links your current difficulties to “faults” from past lives, or makes you dependent on repeated sessions. Deeply painful memories may surface in disguised form — if they overwhelm you, continue with a mental health professional, not with more regressions.
Exploration 04

What is an inner guide?

Intermediate⏱ 15–25 minActive imagination · Dialogue

Traditional definition: benevolent presences — spirit guides, shamanic power animals, angels, ancestors, inner teachers — said to accompany each of us and to be reachable in altered states for guidance and protection.

Psychological lens: the figure of the guide is a personification of your deep wisdom — what Jung explored through “active imagination”: dialoguing with autonomous inner figures to access what you know without knowing you know. The guide “answers” things that surprise you, because they come from non-verbal layers of your psyche. Once again, both lenses lead to the same gestures — and the same benefits: clarity, comfort, more aligned decisions.

Step by step — meeting the guide

  1. Enter a light trance, then set up your safe place (Visualization card) — that's the starting point of any inner journey.
  2. State the invitation: “I invite the wisest and most benevolent part of me to take form and join me.” Then wait, receptive, without forcing.
  3. Welcome what comes: a person, an animal, a light, a voice, a simple felt presence. Don't correct the form — the psyche chooses its language. If nothing comes: an empty bench where you sit “next to” the presence is enough.
  4. Check the quality: a real inner guide is warm, patient, never threatening or authoritarian. Any figure that demands, frightens or flatters is not your wisdom — simply dismiss it and re-invite.
  5. Dialogue: ask ONE real, important question. Listen to the answer in all its forms: words, images, sensations, sudden certainty. You can ask: “show me what I need to see.”
  6. Thank, ask for a sign of reconnection (a word, an inner gesture to find this contact more quickly next time), then come back up and write the dialogue down.
💡 The next-day testThe value of an inner dialogue is judged later, with a clear head: is the “guidance” received wise, benevolent and realistic when reread the next day? Keep what passes this test, let go of the rest. Your waking discernment always remains the final decider — an inner guide advises, it doesn't govern.
Exploration 05

Objects that support your practice

All levelsRitual · Frame

The real power of objects: no object meditates for you — but the brain loves rituals. A dedicated object, always the same, becomes a conditioned trigger: seeing it, touching it or hearing it signals “we're entering practice,” and the body engages relaxation before the first breath. It's the same mechanism as Proust's madeleine — put in service of your practice. Regardless of the lens (energy of traditions or psychological conditioning): well used, objects work.

ObjectWhat it isHow to use it
MalaNecklace of 108 beads from Indian and Tibetan traditionsOne bead per mantra repetition, threaded between thumb and middle finger: the hand counts, the mind stays free for the sound. One turn = one complete session.
Singing bowlMetal (“Tibetan”) or crystal bowl, rubbed/struckThree strikes to open the session, one to close. In sound meditation: follow the sound of the strike until its complete extinction — a remarkable attention exercise.
CandleFlame as fixation point (tratak)1 m away, eye level. Soft gaze on the flame for 1–3 min, then eyes closed: contemplate the afterimage. Induces calm and concentration — and a light trance if prolonged.
Drum · rhythmic musicSteady rhythm ~3-4 beats/secondClassic support for shamanic inner journey: lying down, let the rhythm “carry” the imagery. Dedicated recordings exist (15–30 min with return signal).
Incense · oilsOlfactory signalSmell is the sense most directly wired to emotional memory: a scent reserved for practice becomes a surprisingly powerful state-switch.
Crystals · stonesSymbolic and focus objectsNo demonstrated intrinsic effect — but perfectly usable as anchor objects: a smooth pebble in the hand gives touch a point of return, as the breath gives one to attention.
The dedicated spacePractice corner: cushion, blanket, shelfThe most powerful of all “objects.” Always the same corner, the same opening gestures: the place ends up carrying half the practice.
💡 Keep it simpleA beginner needs a place and ten minutes — everything else is optional. Add an object when the practice is established, one at a time, and because it truly helps you. The accessory collection is a classic trap: equipping the practice you're not doing.
Essential framework

Are altered states of consciousness safe?

For most people, yes, as long as you keep a clear frame; some people should first talk to a health professional (see rule 5). Here are the rules this library asks you to keep, whatever your lens.

The 6 rules of a good explorer

  1. Foundation before depth: first build 4 to 8 weeks of classical meditation (sections 1 to 5). ASCs are explored from a stable mind — never in place of stability.
  2. Intention before, integration after: a sentence of intention at entry, written notes and a real return to earth on exit (movement, water, food, light). An unintegrated experience is worth nothing.
  3. Reality remains the judge: your explorations must enrich your waking life — relationships, work, mood. If they replace it, impoverish it, or detach you from it, that's the stop signal.
  4. Discernment about content: inner images, guides and stories are meaningful, not literal. No important decision (health, money, relationships) is made on the sole basis of trance content.
  5. Health first: if you have epilepsy, avoid rhythmic sound and flickering light. If you have ever lost touch with reality, often feel cut off from yourself, carry painful past events you haven't yet worked through with help, or are going through a crisis, avoid deep explorations unless a health professional supports you. When in doubt, ask first.
  6. Choose practitioners carefully: a good practitioner (hypnosis, sophrology, spiritual guidance) is trained, transparent about their method, promises nothing extraordinary, doesn't create dependence, and refers you to a professional when that's what's needed. Opaque prices, manufactured urgency, unquestionable authority: leave.
⚠️ When to get help without delayExperiences that impose themselves outside practice (voices, presences, feeling cut off from yourself), a lasting feeling of unreality, growing anxiety, isolation, or being unable to “come back down”: talk to a doctor or a psychologist quickly. It's not a failure, and it's not a lost cause — it's the right reflex, and it takes nothing away from your inner path.

What are the signs of astral projection?

The sensations people describe most

The signs people report most often are strong vibrations and a sudden lightness. Many also feel they float or roll, or see the room from outside their body. These signs usually come at the edge of sleep, in deep relaxation or just after waking. None of them, on its own, proves that anything left your body.

Accounts of astral projection are strikingly alike, from Robert Monroe's first book in 1971 to today's online forums. Here are the sensations that come up again and again.

  • Vibrations: a buzzing or a wave of energy that spreads through the body, sometimes with a roaring sound.
  • Heaviness, then lightness: the body feels like lead, then suddenly weightless.
  • Movement without moving: floating, sinking, rocking or rolling to one side while your real body stays still.
  • A new point of view: the room seen from the ceiling, from a corner or from beside the bed.
  • Seeing your own body: lying below you, often a startling sight.
  • Moving by thought: you think of a place, and you seem to glide there.
  • A sudden return: a jolt, as if you had dropped back into bed.

What happens just before you leave your body?

Vibrations, sounds and the edge of sleep

Most people describe a short transition. The body grows heavy and numb, then vibrations or sounds rise, sometimes with a brief paralysis. Practitioners see it as the doorway out of the body. Sleep science calls it the border between waking and sleep.

As you drift off, the brain starts producing dreamlike images and sounds while you are still partly awake. This state is called hypnagogia. You may hear buzzing, rushing wind, voices or music. You may feel that you are falling or swaying. These sensations are common and harmless, even when they are intense.

Sometimes the body stays asleep while the mind wakes up. For a few seconds you cannot move, and you may feel a presence in the room. This is sleep paralysis. Many astral projection reports begin right here. Some practitioners even use it as a doorway: they relax into it instead of fighting it.

💡 Are vibrations required?No. Many people feel strong vibrations and never leave their body. Others describe a smooth exit with no vibrations at all. See them as a sign of deep relaxation, not as a ticket out.

How do you know you are outside your body?

The key shift: where you seem to be

The clearest marker is a change of location. You no longer feel that you are inside your head, but somewhere else in the room. Researchers see this as the heart of an out-of-body experience.

Neuroscientist Olaf Blanke and his colleagues describe three parts. You feel located outside your body. You perceive the world from that outside position. And sometimes, you see your own body from there. A 2025 study comparing these experiences with lucid dreams went further. Its authors suggest treating the feeling of existing without a body as the central feature of an out-of-body experience.

Practitioners add a few clues of their own:

  • Your mind feels lucid and alert, without the haze of a dream.
  • You travel by wanting to, not by walking.
  • You sense your physical body far away, or feel you are in two places at once.
  • The next morning, you remember it like a real event, not like a fading dream.

Was it astral projection or a dream?

Three honest questions

Often you cannot be sure, and that is fine. The two experiences can start in the same place and share the same sensations. Three questions help you sort out what happened, without forcing an answer.

  1. Where did it start? From waking, through deep relaxation, as in classic astral projection? Or from inside a dream, as in a lucid dream?
  2. Did you know you were dreaming? If the thought “This is a dream” crossed your mind, it was probably a lucid dream.
  3. Did you feel outside your body? If your point of view clearly left your body, it looks more like an out-of-body experience.

Many experiences fall somewhere in between, and some people call them astral dreams. Keep an open mind: the label matters less than what you felt and what you learned.

Does seeing your room prove you left your body?

Experience versus proof

No. A room you know well is easy for the mind to rebuild in detail, just as dreams rebuild familiar places. Many people also notice small errors: a lamp in the wrong place, a door that does not exist. Such errors suggest that the scene is partly built by your brain.

To show that perception really leaves the body, you would need to see something you could not otherwise know. And it would have to work again and again, under fair conditions. In 1968, psychologist Charles Tart reported that a young woman correctly named a five-digit number hidden on a high shelf. It was a single case, the setup was criticized, and no study has convincingly repeated it.

This does not make your experience worthless. An out-of-body experience can be moving, beautiful and even life-changing. It simply is not, by itself, proof.

How can you test your experience fairly?

A simple target test

Use a hidden target, and write down what you perceive before you check it. This one rule protects you from fooling yourself.

  1. Prepare a target. Ask someone to place a card with a random word or number on a high shelf, and keep it secret. Or shuffle a deck and put one card face up there without looking.
  2. Practice as usual. Relax, use your favorite technique and let the experience come.
  3. If you feel you are out, look. Try to read the target calmly, without straining.
  4. Write before you check. Note everything you perceived, even vague impressions.
  5. Compare honestly. A hit counts only if it is precise. “Something red” does not count for the queen of hearts.
  6. Keep score over time. One lucky hit means little. Ten attempts tell you much more.

What should you do after a possible astral projection?

Ground yourself, write it down, wait

Ground yourself first, then write everything down before you interpret it. Conclusions can wait.

  • Come back fully. Move your fingers and toes, feel your weight, sit up slowly and drink a glass of water.
  • Write it down right away. Note the time, your position, how well you slept, any paralysis or vibrations, and what you saw.
  • Describe before you explain. Write “I saw the room from the ceiling,” not “I left my body.” Interpretations can come later.
  • Look back after a few weeks. Patterns appear: the best time of night, the sensations that come first, what helps and what gets in the way.

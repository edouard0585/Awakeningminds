A meditation practised with the heart

Metta, or training kindness

Most meditations train attention. This one trains something else: the disposition you bring to living beings — yourself included. It's called metta in its original tradition, "loving-kindness" in English.

The principle is simple, almost naive: you silently form a few sincere wishes, first for yourself, then for ever-widening circles of people. What you're working on isn't the feeling — that can't be ordered — but the intention. The feeling follows, in time.

2

The circles, nearest to furthest

The order matters
  1. Yourself. The hardest one for many — and that's exactly why it comes first: you can't give what you won't grant yourself.
  2. A loved one. Someone for whom affection is easy and uncomplicated. Here the heart finds its tone.
  3. A neutral person. The baker, someone on the train, anyone you know nothing about. This is the circle that genuinely widens.
  4. A difficult person. Start with mild irritation, never with the deepest wound.
  5. All beings. The circle opens without limit — the city, the country, everything alive.

The phrases

Four classic wishes, repeated slowly for each circle: May you be safe. May you be at peace. May you be healthy. May your life be easy. Adapt them to your own words — phrases that ring true beat phrases that are correct.

3

When it resists

Which it often does

"I don't feel anything"

Normal. You don't manufacture a feeling on demand: you repeat an intention. The words are enough — warmth comes later, sometimes after several weeks.

"For myself, it's impossible"

Very common. Go around it: start with the loved one, let the tone settle, then turn back toward yourself, wishing yourself what you just wished them.

"The difficult person makes me angry"

Then they're too difficult for today. Step back one notch — mild irritation — and return later. This practice never asks you to forgive or excuse: only to wish for peace, which is a different thing.

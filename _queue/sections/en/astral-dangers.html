Is astral projection dangerous?

The short answer

For most people, no. Practiced as meditation, astral projection is deep relaxation combined with imagination. The real risks are ordinary ones: lost sleep, fear and, for a few people, feeling cut off from themselves.

The scary stories online are another matter. Some people fear getting trapped outside the body, a silver cord that snaps or spirits that take their place. None of this has ever been shown. These stories grow out of old esoteric books, horror films and the vivid images at the edge of sleep. Knowing where they come from takes away much of their power.

So the useful question is less “Is it dangerous?” than “How do I practice wisely?” The answer comes down to a few simple habits, which you will find below.

Can you get stuck outside your body?

The most common fear

No. There is no recorded case of anyone being unable to come back. Every out-of-body experience ends on its own, usually within seconds or minutes, and you simply find yourself in bed again.

Practitioners say the way back is a single thought. Think of your physical body, try to move a finger, and the experience ends. Fear works the same way. A burst of panic usually brings you straight back, sometimes with a jolt.

What can feel like being stuck is sleep paralysis. You are awake, but your body is still in the relaxed state of dreaming sleep. So you cannot move for a few seconds, sometimes a little longer. It is unpleasant, but it always passes.

  • Slow your breathing: it is one of the few things you can still control.
  • Put all your attention into one small movement: a finger, a toe, your tongue.
  • Tell yourself what it is: a short overlap of sleep and waking.

What about the silver cord, entities and possession?

Where the frightening stories come from

These fears come from old books and from real sensations at the edge of sleep, not from evidence. Each one has a calmer explanation.

Can the silver cord break?

The silver cord is an image borrowed from Ecclesiastes, a book of the Bible. Esoteric writers spread it in the early twentieth century. Many practitioners never see it at all. No account shows that it can snap, or that anyone has come to harm this way.

Are there entities waiting on the astral plane?

Many frightening encounters happen during sleep paralysis. The dreaming brain is still partly active, and it often adds a sense of presence. It may be a shadow in the doorway, or a weight on the chest. Researchers find this intruder feeling in many cultures, under many names.

Whatever you believe about other realms, you never have to engage. You can end the session, open your eyes and turn on the light. Astral · Encounter Protocol, a 16-minute session in the app, teaches a calm approach. You describe what you sense before you interpret it, and you keep the distance you choose.

Can something take over your body?

There is no evidence that anything can take over your body while you practice. Feeling watched or touched at the edge of sleep is a known effect of moving in and out of dreaming. If such fears keep you awake, take a break from astral practice.

What are the real risks of astral projection?

Ordinary, but worth taking seriously

The real risks are lost sleep, fear that lingers and, for some people, feeling detached from themselves or the world. A few habits can add more.

  • Lost sleep. Night alarms and long attempts eat into rest, and tiredness makes strange sensations more likely.
  • Lingering fear. One frightening night can make bedtime tense for weeks.
  • Feeling unreal. Exercises that pull your sense of self away from your body may not suit people who already feel detached.
  • Forced breathing. Breathing too fast can bring tingling and dizziness. It proves nothing and can turn unpleasant.
  • Mistaking images for facts. Making real decisions based on what you “saw” during an experience can lead you astray.
  • Costly promises. Be wary of courses or devices that guarantee an exit, often for a high price.

Who should avoid astral projection practice?

When grounding is the better choice

If any of the points below sounds like you, choose grounding practices instead, at least for now.

  • You regularly feel that you, or the world around you, are unreal or far away.
  • You are grieving, very anxious or under heavy stress.
  • Your sleep is fragile, or you already have frequent sleep paralysis.
  • Frightening night experiences keep coming back.
  • You have been drinking alcohol or taking drugs. Never mix them with these practices.

Grounding practices bring you back into the body instead of out of it. Think of a body scan, slow breathing or a walk outside. In the evening, a sleep meditation is a gentler choice. Drifting into Sleep, for example, lasts 16 minutes and has no wake-up at the end.

How do you practice astral projection safely?

Seven simple precautions

Practice rested, keep it short, breathe naturally and always ground yourself afterward. These seven habits make the biggest difference.

  1. Practice when rested. Never on a night when sleep is precious, and never with a sleep debt.
  2. Keep it short. Twenty to thirty minutes is plenty at first.
  3. Breathe naturally. No breath-holding, no forced breathing.
  4. Choose your way back before you start. A finger to move, a word to say, your eyes to open.
  5. Stay curious, not determined. The harder you push to get out, the more alert you become.
  6. Ground yourself afterward. Feel your weight, touch the floor, drink water, take a few steps.
  7. Write it down, then rest. One or two attempts a week are enough.

A guiding voice helps too. Each guided astral projection meditation in the app leads you through deep relaxation, then a single technique, then back. If you are new to this, start with the Micro-Detachment exercises. They train small, gentle shifts before any full exit.

What should you do if a session frightens you?

Come back, ground yourself, talk about it

Open your eyes, move, turn on a light and anchor yourself in the room. Fear fades fastest when you give your senses something real to hold on to.

  1. Name five things you can see. Then four you can touch, and three you can hear.
  2. Breathe out slowly, a little longer than you breathe in, for a minute or two.
  3. Get up and do something simple: wash your face, make a warm drink, look out the window.
  4. Talk about it with someone you trust, or write it down.

Then take a break for a few days or weeks. When you return, try in daylight, with a guided session, and for a shorter time.

⚠️ When to ask for helpIf fear, a feeling of unreality or bad nights last after you stop practicing, ask a professional for advice. These practices are for well-being, never a substitute for professional care.

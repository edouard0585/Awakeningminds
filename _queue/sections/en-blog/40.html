1Living in Your Head: Great for Solving an Equation, Much Less So for a Quiet Evening

You’re sitting on a couch.

Physically, everything is fine.

But mentally, you are simultaneously:

  • in yesterday’s conversation;
  • in tomorrow’s appointment;
  • in a disaster that could theoretically happen in November;
  • and in an imaginary argument where, as always, you come up with the perfect comeback about eight hours too late.

Welcome to “living in your head” mode.

Thinking is obviously not the problem.

The ability to anticipate, reflect, imagine and plan is one of humanity’s great strengths.

The problem starts when mental activity becomes so dominant that the body and the present environment almost vanish from awareness.

That is exactly what grounding techniques aim to correct.

Grounding means deliberately redirecting your attention toward immediate, concrete information:

  • your feet;
  • your breath;
  • temperature;
  • sounds;
  • an object;
  • a movement;
  • the pressure of your body.

In some energy practices, people talk about rooting, the root chakra, connecting to the earth or bringing energy back down into the lower body.

In psychology, the term grounding is more often used to describe techniques for returning to present-moment sensations.

The languages differ.

The basic gesture is often very similar:

temporarily leaving the mental script to get back to your senses.

2Technique #1 — 5-4-3-2-1: Using Your Five Senses as a Lifeline

Look around you.

Name:

5 things you can see.

A window.

A mug.

A shadow.

A door handle.

A book.

Then:

4 things you can touch.

Your pants.

The seat.

The table.

Your hands.

Then:

3 sounds you can hear.

A car.

The refrigerator.

Your breathing.

Then:

2 smells.

Finally:

1 taste or sensation in your mouth.

Why does this technique work so well for so many people?

Because it gives the brain a task that is very different from rumination.

Instead of processing:

“What if tomorrow…”

you ask it:

“What’s the third blue thing in this room?”

The question is a lot less existential.

And that is exactly what you sometimes need.

3Technique #2 — Pressing Your Feet Into the Floor

Sit down.

Place your feet on the floor.

For ten seconds, press down lightly.

Release.

Repeat.

Then alternate:

  • heels;
  • balls of the feet;
  • toes.

You can also stand up and slowly shift your body weight from one foot to the other.

This practice strongly reactivates proprioception, the information that lets your brain know where your body is.

When mental activity runs too high, sensations of pressure and weight are especially useful because they are simple, immediate and hard to mistake for a thought.

Ask yourself:

“Where is the weight right now?”

The answer is literally under your feet.

4Technique #3 — The Two-Minute Mini Body Scan

Close your eyes if that feels right.

Then move through:

  • feet;
  • calves;
  • thighs;
  • pelvis;
  • belly;
  • chest;
  • shoulders;
  • jaw;
  • face.

At each area, simply ask:

“What do I feel here?”

Not:

“What should I be feeling?”

A two-week trial of the body scan observed improvements in various measures of interoceptive awareness, although some effects were also seen with comparison interventions, which calls for caution about how specific the mechanism is. (PubMed)

More broadly, a recent meta-analysis of 29 randomized trials found that mindfulness interventions produced small to moderate improvements in self-reported measures of interoception. (PubMed)

That is exactly the goal of body-based grounding:

turning the body back into a source of information you can actually hear.

5Technique #4 — Walking Without Your Phone for Ten Minutes

Here is a very sophisticated meditation practice.

Put your phone away.

Walk.

That’s it.

More precisely, for ten minutes, notice:

  • the contact of your foot;
  • the movement of your hips;
  • the temperature of the air;
  • colors;
  • sounds;
  • your breathing.

Every time you drift off into a thought, pick a single thing outside you.

A tree.

A building’s facade.

A sound.

The feeling of the wind.

Then keep going.

Walking has a tremendous advantage over seated meditation: the body is constantly producing new information.

One leg moves forward.

The weight shifts.

The light changes.

The surroundings move.

For people who tend to ruminate when they stay still, mindful walking can therefore be more accessible than classic meditation.

And contrary to a stubborn idea, you don’t need to walk barefoot through a forest as the moon rises.

Shoes and a sidewalk work too.

The planet won’t hold it against you.

6Technique #5 — Cold, Texture and Clear-Cut Sensations

Pick up an object that gives a clear sensation:

  • a warm mug;
  • an ice cube wrapped in a cloth;
  • a stone;
  • a textured ball;
  • a rough fabric.

Notice:

  • temperature;
  • pressure;
  • texture;
  • weight.

For thirty seconds, mentally describe each piece of information.

This technique is especially useful when your mind is spinning very fast, because a clear physical sensation competes effectively with abstraction.

The important rule is to stay gentle.

Grounding is not supposed to cause pain.

Don’t confuse:

“I’m looking for a clear sensation”

with:

“I have to hurt myself to feel my body.”

Those are two completely different things.

7Technique #6 — Grounding Through the Exhale

Sit down and breathe in naturally.

Then slightly lengthen your exhale.

For example:

  • 4 seconds in;
  • 6 seconds out.

Or:

  • 4 seconds;
  • 5 seconds.

The exact count matters less than comfort.

With each exhale, feel at the same time:

  • the weight of your pelvis;
  • your feet;
  • your shoulders dropping.

Slow breathing influences heart rate variability and the interplay between breathing and autonomic regulation; it can therefore be an excellent complement to purely sensory grounding techniques.

But don’t turn it into a performance.

If you feel dizzy, go back to natural breathing right away.

The goal is to come back into your body.

Not to drift off into space.

8What About Walking Barefoot on the Grass?

Grounding is sometimes used in another sense: putting your body in direct electrical contact with the earth, along with claims about the earth’s electrons, inflammation, sleep or other physiological effects.

This theory needs to be distinguished from psychological grounding.

Walking barefoot on the grass can be extremely pleasant and a very good sensory practice:

  • temperature;
  • moisture;
  • texture;
  • pressure;
  • attention to your feet.

But you don’t need to sign up for controversial electrophysiological claims to enjoy the experience.

Sometimes touching grass does you good simply because you are… touching grass.

Not everything needs a cosmic power plant to become interesting.

9Grounding and Dissociation: An Important Nuance

The term dissociation is often used very loosely online.

In a clinical sense, it can refer to different phenomena, including a feeling of detachment from yourself, your body, your emotions or your surroundings.

Some people describe derealization, with the impression that the world is strange or unreal.

Others experience depersonalization, as if they were detached from themselves.

Sensory grounding exercises can sometimes help a person come back to present-moment information.

But when these experiences are repeated, very intense or genuinely disrupt daily life, a few grounding exercises are not necessarily a sufficient response.

In that case, it helps to talk to a healthcare professional.

The same goes if anxiety becomes very significant, if panic attacks are frequent or if the symptoms appear in a context of trauma.

Grounding is a tool.

It is not an obligation to handle everything on your own.

10The Three-Minute Emergency Protocol

When you feel yourself drifting completely into your thoughts, try this.

First minute: look.

Find three objects.

Describe their color and shape.

Second minute: feel.

Feet.

Hands.

Back.

Breath.

Third minute: move.

Stand up.

Walk slowly.

Take five steps in one direction and five in the other.

Then ask yourself:

“What is the next concrete thing I actually need to do?”

Drink some water?

Send a message?

Get back to work?

Take a shower?

Go for a walk?

Sleep?

Grounding isn’t only there to calm you down.

It rebuilds a bridge between your inner state and real action.

11The Real Definition of Grounding: Becoming Available Again to What Exists

Being grounded doesn’t mean being calm all the time.

You can be grounded and sad.

Grounded and angry.

Grounded and anxious.

Grounding means something more like:

“I know where my body is, what is happening around me and what I am feeling well enough not to be completely swallowed by my mental script.”

That is why grounding techniques complement meditation so well.

Meditation teaches you to observe the mind.

Grounding reminds you that there is also:

  • a body;
  • a room;
  • sounds;
  • a floor;
  • a world.

Your brain may want to solve your entire life at 11:47 p.m.

Your feet have a much more reasonable ambition.

They just want to touch the ground.

Sometimes, start with them.

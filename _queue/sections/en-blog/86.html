1Sometimes the problem starts when sleep becomes a mission

There’s something deeply unfair about insomnia: the more you want to sleep, the further away sleep sometimes seems. You’re lying there, eyes closed, fully aware that you have to get up in six hours, then five hours and forty-seven minutes, then five hours and twenty-nine minutes, and you start monitoring your body like an engineer in charge of a space launch: “Is my breathing slowing down? Am I relaxed enough? Am I starting to drift off? Why am I still awake?”

Sleep then becomes a performance.

And that’s exactly the problem.

Unlike walking, reading or cooking a meal, falling asleep isn’t something we can trigger directly through willpower. We can create favorable conditions, but we can’t order the brain: “shutdown in thirty seconds.”

The more important falling asleep becomes, the more we develop a kind of sleep monitoring, in which every sensation is read as a sign of success or failure. The person is no longer simply resting in bed: they’re checking whether they’re asleep.

This hypervigilance can fuel the insomnia itself.

So the useful question becomes less:

“How can I force my brain to sleep?”

than:

“How can I stop turning temporary wakefulness into a threat?”

This is one of the central insights of cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, or CBT-I, now considered a first-line treatment for chronic insomnia in many clinical guidelines.

2Your bed shouldn’t become an office where you work very hard at falling asleep

Imagine spending two hours in bed every night:

  • thinking;
  • checking the time;
  • worrying;
  • reading the news;
  • answering emails;
  • trying to sleep;
  • getting annoyed because you’re not sleeping.

Gradually, the brain can learn an association very different from the one you want:

bed = wakefulness + frustration + monitoring.

Stimulus control, used in CBT-I, aims precisely to rebuild a more stable association between bed and sleep.

The general idea isn’t to stay in bed indefinitely, battling insomnia.

When you feel that you’re truly awake, frustrated and caught up in a mental fight, you can leave the bed for a while, go somewhere quiet with dim light and do something low-key, then come back when sleepiness returns.

Watch out for a very common oversimplification: there’s no need to watch the clock and tell yourself you absolutely have to get up after exactly 20 minutes. That can create a new kind of monitoring.

The principle is more about behavior than about the stopwatch:

if you’re clearly awake and your bed is becoming a place of frustration, call off the fight.

Calm reading.

Soft music.

Quiet breathing.

Dim light.

Then back to bed when your eyelids get heavy.

It seems counterintuitive when you’re exhausted, but spending two hours fighting in bed can strengthen exactly the association you’re trying to undo.

3Paradoxical intention: trying to stay awake so you stop wanting to sleep

Some approaches use a technique called paradoxical intention, which is particularly interesting for people whose insomnia is strongly fueled by the fear of not sleeping.

The principle is deliberately strange.

Instead of telling yourself:

“I have to sleep now,”

you temporarily adopt the attitude:

“I’m going to stay quietly awake without trying to bring on sleep.”

You don’t look at your phone.

You don’t stimulate yourself.

You don’t actively force yourself to fight off drowsiness.

You simply drop the effort.

Picture the difference.

The classic version:

“Close your eyes. Breathe. Relax. Why isn’t this working? I have to sleep. I absolutely have to sleep.”

The paradoxical version:

“I can just stay here. If sleep comes, great. If not, I don’t have anything to accomplish right now.”

The goal is to reduce sleep performance anxiety.

This technique is obviously not a universal solution, but it illustrates an essential principle: when wanting to sleep has become one of the things keeping you awake, learning to ease off the effort can work better than adding yet another control technique.

That’s also why some bedtime meditations work better when they say:

“You don’t need to fall asleep”

rather than:

“In five minutes, you’ll be fast asleep.”

The first takes pressure off.

The second sets a deadline.

4Why insomnia loves next-day disasters

At 2 a.m., the mind often calculates the future with dramatic precision:

“Tomorrow is going to be a disaster.”

“I won’t be able to work.”

“I’m going to make a mistake.”

“If I don’t sleep now, my week is ruined.”

These thoughts generate emotional arousal at exactly the moment you want to dial down alertness.

Two things need to be distinguished here.

Yes, lack of sleep can affect:

  • attention;
  • mood;
  • memory;
  • performance;
  • health, when it becomes chronic.

But one bad night on its own isn’t automatically a disaster.

You’ve probably already lived through less-than-perfect days after sleeping badly.

You were tired.

Maybe you drank too much coffee.

You didn’t function as well.

But you survived.

A more realistic way to reframe it is to think:

“Tomorrow may be harder, but I can function imperfectly for one day.”

This sentence doesn’t deny the tiredness.

It reduces the threat.

And reducing the threat can lower precisely the arousal that keeps you awake.

In CBT-I, working on beliefs about sleep is often an important part of treatment, especially when someone develops rigid rules like:

“I absolutely have to sleep eight hours.”

“If I wake up, my night is ruined.”

“A good night has to be unbroken.”

Human sleep is naturally variable.

The goal isn’t perfection.

5What’s better done during the day than at night

The best time to deal with your insomnia isn’t always 3 a.m.

During the day, work instead on the things that actually structure sleep:

  • a fairly regular wake-up time;
  • natural light, especially early in the day;
  • physical activity;
  • managing caffeine;
  • enough time awake;
  • cutting down on long naps if they disrupt sleep pressure;
  • a steady evening routine.

A regular wake-up time is particularly important, because it helps stabilize circadian rhythms and avoids constantly shifting your biological schedule after every bad night.

The instinctive reaction after a difficult night is often:

“I’ll stay in bed three hours longer tomorrow.”

Understandable.

But when this becomes a habit, you risk lowering your evening sleep pressure and keeping the mismatch going.

The goal, obviously, isn’t to deprive yourself of sleep in some haphazard way.

The time-in-bed restriction or compression protocols used in CBT-I are structured and need to be tailored, particularly for some people with specific medical or psychiatric conditions.

Chronic insomnia deserves better than a collection of tips picked at random off the internet.

6When giving up trying finally becomes a serious strategy

If you can’t fall asleep tonight, try something radically simple:

stop chasing sleep for a while.

Tell yourself:

“I’m just going to rest.”

Feel the mattress.

Leave your breathing alone.

If you become truly awake and frustrated, get out of bed for a bit.

Don’t check the time every five minutes.

Don’t start calculating your sleep deficit like an anxious accountant.

Then come back when sleepiness returns.

If insomnia lasts several weeks, happens several nights a week, comes with heavy daytime fatigue or seriously affects how you function, it’s best to talk to a professional and consider CBT-I, while possibly looking into contributing factors such as sleep apnea, restless legs syndrome, medication, pain, anxiety or depression.

The sleep paradox is actually rather elegant:

  • you can prepare the ground;
  • you can protect your schedule;
  • you can calm your environment;
  • but at the decisive moment, the last step is often to stop trying.

Sleep doesn’t like being summoned.

It generally prefers to slip in when no one is watching the door.

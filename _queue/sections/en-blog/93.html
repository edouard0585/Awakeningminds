1Fifteen stones, almost nothing else… and yet millions of people stay in front of them for minutes on end

The stone garden of Ryōan-ji, in Kyoto, has a quality that is extremely rare in our era saturated with images: it contains very little and nevertheless manages to hold the eye.

No spectacular waterfall.

No miniature forest.

No gigantic statue.

No lotus floating in a perfectly photogenic mist.

A rectangle of light gravel.

Fifteen stones.

Some moss.

Walls.

And several centuries of questions.

UNESCO describes the famous Ryōan-ji dry garden as a rectangle of approximately 250 m², composed of fifteen stones distributed in five groups on white gravel, all enclosed by earth walls on three sides; it constitutes a major example of a Japanese dry garden, or karesansui. (UNESCO World Heritage Centre)

The apparent simplicity is precisely what makes the place so powerful.

Faced with an extremely busy landscape, the eye knows what to do:

explore.

In front of Ryōan-ji, there is almost nothing to explore.

So you are forced to look longer.

And the more you look, the more important the relationships between the stones become:

  • distance;
  • emptiness;
  • mass;
  • orientation;
  • balance.

The garden acts almost as a visual meditation.

Not because it necessarily hides a secret esoteric code, but because it reduces the number of objects until it makes perceptible what we usually ignore:

the space between things.

2Why fifteen stones, and above all why can’t we see them all at once?

The most famous detail of the garden is probably this fascinating idea: from the usual viewing positions, at least one stone remains hidden by another, so that the fifteen are not all clearly visible simultaneously from a single ordinary point.

This phenomenon has naturally fueled an impressive number of interpretations.

An oft-repeated modern legend states that only a person who has achieved enlightenment could symbolically “see the fifteen stones,” but one must be careful: popular interpretations of this type are much easier to repeat than their historical origin is to establish.

What is certain, however, is that the fifteen stones really exist and that they are arranged in five groups. (UNESCO World Heritage Centre)

Why fifteen?

There is no definitively established historical answer that would allow us to say:

“this is exactly what the author meant.”

And that’s probably good news.

Because, faced with a minimalist work, we are very tempted to immediately look for the hidden instructions:

stones = islands?

Tigress and cubs?

Mountains protruding from the clouds?

Constellations?

The garden has received all kinds of readings.

But a contemplative work can be strong precisely because it cannot be reduced to a single translation.

You are forced to stay with ambiguity.

And staying with a question without immediately burying it under an answer is already a very Zen practice, even if we avoid pretending that each grain of gravel holds a secret doctrine.

3The karesansui: representing a landscape with almost nothing

The Ryōan-ji garden belongs to the world of karesansui, literally dry landscape, a form of Japanese garden in which rocks, gravel and other elements help to compose a landscape without necessarily using real water.

Gravel can evoke water.

A stone can become a mountain.

A group of stones can suggest an island or a landform.

But we must avoid transforming this visual language into a rigid dictionary:

gravel = always ocean; stone = always mountain.

The interest lies more in the capacity of the garden to condense an immense landscape into a few elements.

It’s almost the visual equivalent of a haiku.

Instead of literally reproducing nature, it extracts a few relationships from it.

This reduction has an astonishing psychological effect.

When almost everything has been removed, every detail takes on enormous weight.

The angle of a stone.

A tuft of moss.

The regular movement of gravel.

The aging wall.

The void becomes an integral part of the composition.

In Japanese aesthetic culture, the relationship between form and empty space appears in many areas, and the concept of ma, often loosely translated as interval or relational space, helps to understand why “nothing” is not necessarily the absence of content.

The space between two objects organizes their relationship.

Same thing in meditation.

The silence between two thoughts is not necessarily a hole to fill.

It can become something that we notice.

4The real mystery of the garden: we absolutely want it to mean something specific

Imagine that a guide tells you:

“The fifteen stones represent fifteen tigers crossing the sea.”

Suddenly the way you look changes.

You start looking for tigers.

Then another guide says:

“No, they are islands.”

You look for islands.

A third states:

“It’s a cosmological representation.”

And now your meditation has become a treasure hunt.

The difficulty lies in accepting that an old garden may have accumulated several layers of reading without us being able to recover with certainty the exact intention behind each formal choice.

UNESCO describes the site as a mid-15th-century stone garden and a remarkable example of karesansui, but this historical recognition does not provide us with a complete legend explaining each stone. (UNESCO World Heritage Centre)

This is precisely what makes the place fascinating.

The human brain hates ambiguity.

It wants to solve.

To name.

To classify.

Ryōan-ji almost answers it:

“Look again.”

And it can become a great mindfulness exercise.

Every time you think:

“I understood,”

observe what you actually understood.

A shape?

An interpretation?

A preference?

The difference is important.

We often confuse what we see with the story we immediately tell about what we see.

The garden has fifteen stones.

Everything else is already beginning to become interpretation.

5How to meditate in front of Ryōan-ji — or recreate the exercise at home

If you have the chance to visit the garden, resist the urge to arrive, take eight photos and leave for the next temple.

Sit down.

Choose a stone.

For two minutes, look only at:

  • shape;
  • texture;
  • orientation;
  • shadow.

Then widen your gaze to the group.

Then to the empty space between this group and the next.

Finally to the whole composition.

You have just practiced a deliberate change of attentional scale.

This is an excellent form of visual meditation.

You can then ask a simple question:

“What do I notice when I stop looking for meaning?”

Maybe the height differences.

Maybe the wall.

Maybe the rhythm of the groups.

Maybe your own impatience.

This last observation is often the most interesting.

You can reproduce the exercise at home with three objects on a table.

Place them intentionally.

Sit down.

Observe not only the objects, but the spaces.

Then move a single element.

Look how everything changes.

This micro-practice transforms an aesthetic idea into a bodily experience:

balance also depends on what is not occupied.

A pretty useful lesson in an apartment, a schedule and probably a life.

6What fifteen stones can teach us about our obsession with filling everything

We fill almost everything.

Walls.

Schedules.

Phones.

Moments of waiting.

Silences in a conversation.

Ten seconds in an elevator.

Even a walk easily becomes a podcast.

Ryōan-ji suggests exactly the opposite:

less.

And this “less” is not empty of meaning.

It increases sensitivity.

When twenty objects compete for your attention, you see each one vaguely.

When there are almost none left, you start to see the connections.

This is probably one of the reasons why dry gardens continue to influence our contemporary idea of Zen meditation, even if we must avoid reducing the whole history of Japanese Zen to a minimalist aesthetic intended for decoration magazines.

Ryōan-ji is a specific historical place, part of a tradition and a real architectural context.

But its effect can go beyond its history.

You arrive in front of fifteen stones.

You look for an explanation.

Then, perhaps, you discover that the most interesting question is not:

“What do they represent?”

but:

“Why do I need them to mean something so much?”

And at that moment, the garden ceases to be only in front of you.

It begins to work on the way you look.

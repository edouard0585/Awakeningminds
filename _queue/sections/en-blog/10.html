1Day 1: nothing mystical happens

Day one.

Ten minutes.

I close my eyes.

I’m supposed to be watching my breath.

Instead, I discover that my brain contains:

  • a shopping list;
  • a song from 2007;
  • three imaginary conversations;
  • an extremely urgent itch on my nose;
  • and a worry I didn’t know existed thirty seconds ago.

First discovery of a 30-day meditation challenge:

the brain is not quiet.

Second discovery:

that’s not a failure.

The work consists precisely in noticing that your attention has wandered off, then coming back.

Not in reaching the cosmic void by Wednesday.

2Week 1: learning to come back

The first few days are mostly mechanical.

Breath.

Thought.

Back.

Sound.

Back.

Itch.

Back.

After a few sessions, a tiny change appears: I catch my distractions a little faster.

It’s subtle.

I haven’t become smarter.

I haven’t opened my third eye.

But I’m starting to recognize the exact moment a thought carries me away.

This skill spills over slightly into everyday life.

Something annoying happens.

Before I respond on the spot, there is sometimes an extra half-second of distance.

Not always.

But sometimes.

And sometimes is enough to start.

3Week 2: on day 8, I really don’t feel like it

This is where the pretty Instagram stories get less pretty.

Day 8:

zero motivation.

Day 9:

“I’ll do it later.”

Day 10:

my brain mysteriously suggests cleaning out a drawer rather than sitting still for ten minutes.

Welcome to what building a habit really looks like.

Daily meditation becomes less new.

So less exciting.

And that is precisely where it starts to become a practice rather than an experiment.

A randomized study with participants from many countries looked at a 30-day digital mindfulness program and observed improvements in several measures of psychological health among those who followed the intervention, though with a high dropout rate — a useful reminder that sticking with the program is already part of the challenge. (PubMed)

4Week 3: day 19, welcome to the plateau

The quick gains sometimes seem to have vanished.

I sit down.

My mind is still chattering.

It’s frustrating.

Then I realize something:

I had quietly started to believe that “making progress in meditation” meant “having fewer and fewer thoughts.”

Wrong.

The change lies more in your relationship with your thoughts.

A worry can be there without necessarily turning into a 45-minute investigation.

An unpleasant emotion can exist without me having to fix it right away.

It feels less like:

“I control my brain.”

And more like:

“Look, my brain is putting on its show again.”

The movie is still playing.

But I don’t have to buy the popcorn.

5Week 4: the visible changes are surprisingly ordinary

After about a month, the most interesting changes aren’t spectacular.

They look like this:

  • I notice tension in my shoulders sooner;
  • I take a breath before certain replies;
  • I spot some of my rumination;
  • I can sit with a few moments of silence more easily;
  • I’m more aware of how tired I am;
  • I can deliberately return to a physical sensation.

Studies of daily mindfulness programs do find improvements in stress and well-being in some groups. A recent trial of daily digital meditation, for example, found a substantial drop in perceived stress after eight weeks. (PubMed)

But it’s important to also look at what didn’t change.

I still procrastinate.

Some people still get on my nerves.

A bad night is still a bad night.

Sadly, the bills have not developed a spiritual practice and keep arriving.

Meditation improves certain skills.

It doesn’t replace life.

6What I take away after 30 days

The main benefit may not be calm.

It’s detection.

Detecting:

“I’m tense.”

“I’m ruminating.”

“I’m reacting.”

“I’m tired.”

“My attention has scattered.”

Then having one more option:

coming back.

To try the experiment yourself:

Days 1 to 7: 5 minutes.

Breath only.

Days 8 to 14: 8 minutes.

Breath + body.

Days 15 to 21: 10 minutes.

Add observing your thoughts.

Days 22 to 30: 10 to 15 minutes.

Freely choose breath, body scan or open awareness meditation.

After each session, just write down:

How was I before?

How am I now?

What did I notice?

Three lines.

That way you’ll have a far more interesting record than a vague memory.

And on day 30, don’t ask:

“Have I become zen?”

Ask:

“Do I notice myself more?”

That is probably where the real change is hiding.

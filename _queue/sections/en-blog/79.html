1The basic trap of fear: waiting to feel ready before you act

Imagine you’re afraid of speaking in front of a group and you make a decision that seems perfectly logical: “I’ll give a presentation when I feel confident enough.” The problem is that your brain rarely learns to see a situation as safe just because you’ve thought about it from your couch; if you systematically avoid presentations, you almost never give it the experience that could contradict its catastrophic script, so the fear can stay intact for years despite thousands of hours spent hoping to become mysteriously braver.

This is precisely the principle behind exposure therapies, used in particular to treat various phobias and anxiety disorders: the person gradually encounters, in a suitable setting, the situations, sensations, memories or objects they avoid — not because “forcing yourself” is morally superior, but because avoidance prevents certain corrective learning from taking place.

Take a fear of elevators.

You step in.

Anxiety rises.

You step straight back out.

Spectacular relief.

What has the brain just learned?

“Getting out saved me.”

Next time, the elevator looks even more threatening.

So the immediate relief has reinforced avoidance in the long run.

This is one of the most important paradoxes of anxiety: what very effectively reduces fear over the next thirty seconds can sometimes help keep it going for the next thirty months.

Modern approaches to exposure rely in particular on inhibitory learning: rather than only trying to bring fear down to zero during each exercise, the work consists of creating new learning that can compete with earlier threatening associations. The work of Michelle Craske and her colleagues has contributed greatly to developing this perspective. (PubMed)

2Exposure doesn’t mean throwing yourself straight into your worst terror

The word exposure can sound brutal, because we immediately picture someone with a fear of spiders locked in a room full of tarantulas while a therapist blocks the door — an excellent way to turn a nuanced clinical technique into the plot of a bad movie.

In many cases, gradual exposure starts much more modestly.

Suppose you’re afraid of public speaking.

Build a ladder from 0 to 10:

2/10 — read a paragraph out loud on your own.

3/10 — record yourself.

4/10 — send the recording to a friend.

5/10 — tell a story for two minutes to three people.

6/10 — ask a question in a meeting.

7/10 — give a five-minute presentation.

9/10 — give an important presentation in front of thirty people.

The aim isn’t necessarily to always start at level 2 and only move up once all fear has disappeared; contemporary models put more emphasis on creating new experiences and violating catastrophic predictions than on a simple mechanical drop in anxiety.

Before the exercise, write something like:

“I think there’s an 80% chance I’ll completely fall apart and people will think I’m ridiculous.”

Afterward:

“I was very anxious, I hesitated twice, but I finished; nobody laughed.”

You’re giving your brain new data.

So the goal isn’t:

“I must stop feeling fear.”

But:

“I can discover what really happens when I move forward despite it.”

3Habituation or learning: you don’t need to wait for your fear to drop to zero

For a long time, exposure was often explained mainly through habituation: staying in the situation long enough would allow anxiety to rise and then fall, and that fall itself would be the heart of the learning.

This idea remains intuitive, but modern research shows the picture is more complex: the drop in fear during a session doesn’t necessarily predict long-term therapeutic success perfectly, and a person can learn something important even if they still finish the exercise feeling anxious. (PubMed)

Imagine you ride an elevator up ten floors.

Fear before:

8/10.

Fear after:

6/10.

You might conclude:

“Failure, I’m still anxious.”

But your initial prediction was:

“I’m going to panic, lose control and need to be rescued.”

What actually happened?

You felt strong anxiety.

You went up.

You came down.

Nothing catastrophic happened.

So the key learning may be:

“I can be anxious in an elevator without the predicted catastrophe happening.”

This perspective is far more powerful than the idea that an exposure only succeeds when you feel completely relaxed.

Sometimes, courage consists precisely in finishing the exercise with sweaty palms and realizing:

“I was scared, and I did it anyway.”

4Safety behaviors: when your psychological umbrella stops you from checking whether it’s really raining

Anxious people often use safety behaviors meant to prevent the imagined catastrophe.

Fear of taking the subway:

always standing near the door.

Fear of a panic attack:

checking your heart rate every thirty seconds.

Social fear:

mentally rehearsing every sentence before speaking.

Fear of driving:

only taking the same route and calling someone for the entire journey.

Not all of these strategies are bad in every situation, and the question of dropping them needs to be tailored to the individual; nevertheless, some can block learning because after a successful exposure you conclude:

“Nothing happened only because I had my safety behavior.”

Instead of learning:

“I can handle the situation,”

you learn:

“I can handle the situation only if my crutch is there.”

The literature on inhibitory learning looks specifically at how safety signals and behaviors can influence exposure, and current recommendations are more nuanced than a simple “drop them all immediately.” (PubMed)

The aim is rather to gradually identify which ones genuinely block new learning and which ones are reasonable or necessary.

A seatbelt in a car is a safety device.

Refusing to drive without calling your sister for the whole trip is something else.

Psychology, unfortunately, loves details.

5How to build a reasonable personal exposure plan — and when not to do it alone

For a moderate, uncomplicated fear, you can start by defining a concrete goal — not “never be afraid again,” but something like:

  • “being able to take the elevator up to the fifth floor”;
  • “asking a question during a meeting”;
  • “calling an organization without putting it off for three days.”

Next, write down several situations of increasing difficulty, choose a step that produces real but manageable anxiety, identify your catastrophic prediction before the exercise, carry out the action, then compare what you predicted with what actually happened.

Repeat the experience in several contexts.

This matters because very specific learning can stay tied to one particular situation.

You want to gradually learn:

“I can handle this in different circumstances,”

and not just:

“I can only do it on Tuesdays at 2 p.m. with the same person sitting next to me.”

Varying exposures is precisely one of the strategies used in inhibitory learning models to promote generalization. (PubMed)

On the other hand, some situations call for more caution.

A severe phobia.

Obsessive-compulsive disorder.

Post-traumatic stress disorder.

Significant panic attacks.

An eating disorder.

Dissociative symptoms.

A fear linked to a real danger.

In these cases, “face your fear on your own” can become far too simplistic advice, because clinical exposure therapy is structured according to the specific disorder, and poorly designed exposure can reinforce avoidance, become excessive or target the wrong thing.

Someone with OCD, for example, doesn’t use exactly the same protocol as someone who is afraid of dogs, and working on trauma certainly doesn’t mean deliberately throwing yourself into the most triggering situation in the hope of “toughening up.”

6Meditation and exposure: learning to let fear come along for the ride

Meditation can be an excellent complement when it’s used not to make fear disappear before exposure, but to learn to recognize anxious sensations without obeying them immediately.

You’re about to speak.

Your heart speeds up.

Your stomach tightens.

The thought appears:

“I’m going to look ridiculous.”

A mindfulness approach might be:

“racing heart.”

“tension in the stomach.”

“catastrophe thought.”

Then:

action.

You move forward.

This distinction is essential, because if you systematically use breathing or meditation as a mandatory condition before each exposure to guarantee that your anxiety drops, the technique itself can become a safety behavior.

The message then becomes:

“I can only face this situation once I’ve calmed down.”

Yet the most powerful learning can sometimes be:

“I can do something important while feeling anxious.”

Fear then gradually stops being a red light.

It becomes one piece of information among others.

And that is often what overcoming a fear really means: not reaching an existence in which your brain never sounds the alarm again, but becoming free enough that you no longer organize your whole life around avoiding that alarm.

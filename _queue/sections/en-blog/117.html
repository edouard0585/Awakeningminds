1Inspiration or appropriation: the line isn’t simply “am I allowed to love this tradition?”

Taking an interest in another culture is obviously not wrong in itself. Humans have been exchanging stories, music, food, technologies and religious practices for millennia. The problem of cultural appropriation arises when that borrowing happens within a particular power dynamic: a dominant group takes elements from a historically marginalized population, transforms them, sells them or claims authority over them while ignoring the people who passed them on.

In contemporary spirituality, the subject is especially sensitive.

Some Indigenous traditions were banned, suppressed or ridiculed by colonial states before becoming, a few generations later, appealing products sold by people from outside those communities.

The contrast is hard to miss.

Lisa Aldred analyzed it in a particularly influential way in her 2000 article “Plastic Shamans and Astroturf Sun Dances,” devoted to the New Age commercialization of Native American spiritualities. In it, she describes in particular the rise of non-Native people presenting themselves as experts in sacred practices, and the mass sale of objects marketed as “Native American.”

So the right question isn’t just:

“Can I use a drum?”

It becomes:

“Where exactly does what I’m using come from, who passed it on to me, how do I present it, and who benefits from selling it?”

This second set of questions is less convenient for putting together a retreat brochure. It’s far more useful for practicing honestly.

2The “plastic shaman”: when spiritual identity becomes a commercial product

The term plastic shaman is used critically to describe people who claim a dubious Indigenous identity or authority in order to sell teachings, initiations, ceremonies or objects.

The problem isn’t simply making money.

An Indigenous teacher can, of course, charge for their work.

The problem arises when someone invents a lineage, mixes several traditions while presenting them as authentic, or sells sacred ceremonies despite the opposition of the communities they come from.

Aldred’s article shows how certain forms of New Age spirituality turned Native American elements into consumer goods cut off from their colonial and political histories.

This creates a strange situation: the actual culture becomes less visible at the very moment its aesthetic becomes everywhere.

A headdress here.

A medicine wheel there.

A “shamanic name” before dessert.

And soon enough, a perfectly “ancestral” ceremony somehow has four different peoples of origin.

A first ethical principle is therefore extremely simple: never invent authority.

If you’ve taken three contemporary workshops, say you’ve taken three contemporary workshops.

Don’t make up a secret initiation with a people whose name nobody can verify.

Here, humility is a far more reliable spiritual technology than feathers.

3Some practices are open; others are tied to communities, responsibilities or initiations

Not all cultural practices have the same status.

A popular song broadcast publicly, a craft technique taught to visitors and a secret ceremony reserved for certain members of a community can’t be treated as identical resources available on the internet.

Respect therefore begins with finding out what the keepers of the tradition say themselves.

Some communities are happy to teach certain practices to outsiders.

Others ask that certain rituals remain within the community.

Some distinguish between what can be shared, observed, photographed or sold.

And these positions can even vary within the same people.

This means there’s no universal form titled “Indigenous permission.”

You have to look at the specific context.

The problem gets worse when a religious element is reduced to its aesthetic. A sacred plant becomes a lifestyle product; a ritual garment becomes a festival accessory; a funeral ceremony becomes a personal development workshop.

In the process, what gets removed is often precisely what made the practice meaningful: obligations to a community, language, mythology, land and reciprocity.

Respecting a tradition therefore doesn’t just mean pronouncing its name correctly.

It means accepting that it may have limits that don’t suit us.

If everything other people hold sacred has to be instantly available for our personal transformation, we may have confused spirituality with an all-you-can-eat buffet.

4How to build a contemporary shamanic practice without usurping a specific tradition

There’s a surprisingly simple solution: say exactly what you’re doing.

If you practice a contemporary visualization accompanied by drumming, call it that.

If you follow Michael Harner’s Core Shamanism, say it’s Core Shamanism, a modern synthesis designed not to reproduce the specific ceremonies of any one people. The Foundation for Shamanic Studies itself states that its system looks for common elements and isn’t tied to a particular culture.

You can also explore your own regional traditions: folklore, stories, places, contemplative practices, music and your relationship with the landscape.

That doesn’t mean inventing an “authentic Gaulish shamanism” whose evidence has miraculously vanished.

It’s simply about avoiding the belief that any deep spirituality must necessarily come from an exoticized population.

When you learn directly from an Indigenous person, find out about their actual role, how they are recognized by their community and exactly what they authorize you to pass on afterward.

Paying teachers fairly, citing your sources and supporting communities are also far more concrete gestures than vaguely thanking “the elders.”

Finally, keep names specific.

A Shipibo-Konibo practice is Shipibo-Konibo.

A Korean ritual is Korean.

An Evenki rite is Evenki.

The word “shamanism” is sometimes useful for comparison. It becomes dangerous when it turns fifty cultures into a single spiritual soup.

5Respect doesn’t mean freezing cultures: exchange, evolution and reciprocity

We should also avoid the opposite extreme: imagining that cultures are completely sealed boxes that no one should ever look into.

Societies are constantly exchanging.

Indigenous people themselves create new forms, teach abroad, use the internet, write books and adapt their practices.

Respect therefore doesn’t mean locking someone into an unchanging “traditional” image.

It means recognizing their power to decide how their culture evolves.

The question of reciprocity then becomes central.

If a tradition brings inspiration, prestige or income, what goes back to the people it comes from?

If you run workshops based on a particular practice, are you supporting teachers or organizations directly connected to that tradition?

If you use a sacred story, do you know where it comes from?

These questions turn appropriation into relationship.

The debate about Western shamanism is ultimately not a war between “openness” and “prohibition.” It’s about the difference between encounter and extraction.

Lisa Aldred showed how the commercialization of Native American spiritualities could reproduce colonial relationships even when presented as admiration or a spiritual quest.

Good intentions, then, aren’t always enough.

Practicing with respect takes a little extra work: history, attribution, consent, reciprocity and honesty about your own place.

It’s not very mystical.

But respect may well be one of the most concrete forms of spirituality there is.

1What does grounding after meditation actually mean?

It means learning to come back gradually to an ordinary awareness oriented toward your surroundings, and it’s a fundamental skill. Spiritual practices talk a great deal about setting off. How do you enter trance? How do you journey? How do you deepen meditation? How do you go beyond your usual state? The return is far less glamorous, yet it deserves just as much attention.

After an immersive experience, some people feel perfectly normal straight away. Others may keep, for a few minutes, a floating feeling, a strong emotion, an unusual sense of their body or simply the wish to stay quiet. After a particularly intense practice, the transition can take longer.

It helps here to distinguish spiritual vocabulary from psychological vocabulary. A practitioner may call the phase following a shamanic visualization the “return from the journey.” A psychologist might speak of reorienting attention. The term grounding is also used in therapeutic settings to describe various strategies meant to bring attention back to the present environment.

A critical review published in 2025, however, showed something very important: grounding techniques used by therapists are frequently recommended when people feel cut off from themselves or their surroundings, but their specific effectiveness has not yet been properly established by solid trials, partly because the term itself lacks a single, precise definition.

In other words, “grounding works scientifically” would be too strong a claim. It’s more accurate to say that it is a common practice among health professionals, consistent with the aim of sensory reorientation, but with surprisingly limited specific evidence.

2How do you ground yourself after meditation?

After a trance or a very absorbing meditation, there’s no need to leap straight onto your phone. Start instead by reconnecting with the simplest bodily information.

Feel the weight of your pelvis on the chair or your back on the floor. Notice your feet, your hands and the temperature of the air. Gently move your fingers, then your toes. Stretch if it feels comfortable.

This strategy follows a simple logic: during some absorbing experiences, attention is strongly turned toward inner images, thoughts or sensations. Coming back consists of gradually widening this attention to present sensory information.

Hammond and Brown’s review of grounding points out precisely that clinical descriptions of these techniques frequently use the five senses and simple cognitive tasks to encourage reorientation toward the present.

So you can look around and silently name a few objects: window, lamp, rug, table. Then listen to nearby sounds, then distant ones. Touch the texture of your clothing or your seat.

There’s nothing mysterious here. And that is exactly the point.

After exploring cosmic dragons, ancestors or the depths of the unconscious, finding a remarkably ordinary chair again can be excellent news.

3How do you get your bearings back after a long meditation?

Place yourself back in a concrete context, because good reorientation isn’t only about the body.

Remind yourself of the date, where you are and what you’re going to do next. If the session was long, check the time. Calmly drink a little water. Walk for a few minutes once you feel steady enough.

It can help to avoid jumping straight into an activity that demands a high level of alertness if you still feel disoriented. Driving right after an experience that has strongly altered your perception is not a great moment to test your inner enlightenment.

There is also an essential difference between a chosen, temporary state and feeling cut off from yourself without wanting to. If you simply need a few minutes to reorient after a meditation, that may be a normal transition. However, recurring episodes in which the world seems unreal or you feel like a stranger to yourself, memory gaps, confusion or a feeling of not being in control of your actions warrant a check-up with a health professional.

Grounding must not become a way of masking a problem that needs to be looked at by a professional.

The 2025 review emphasizes this distinction too: grounding is often mentioned by therapists as a way to interrupt or ease episodes of feeling cut off from yourself, but the current lack of solid experimental evidence rules out treating it as a universal solution.

4How do you integrate a trance experience?

Describe it first and interpret it later, keeping several meanings open. The physical return is only half the work. A trance experience can leave behind very powerful images, emotions or intuitions. The temptation is then to give them a definitive meaning right away.

You saw a wolf? Perhaps it’s your eternal spirit animal.

Or perhaps your brain simply decided a wolf suited the scenery admirably.

The most fruitful attitude is to keep several hypotheses open.

Write down what happened before interpreting it: “I saw a wolf near a river,” rather than “my spirit guide told me I had to move house.”

Only then note your associations: what does this animal represent for you? What emotion was present? Does the image bring back a memory? Does it have a particular cultural meaning?

This distinction between observation and interpretation keeps you from confusing emotional power with factual truth.

A trance can become excellent material for reflection without being treated as the universe’s certified messaging service.

5How do you build a return ritual, and when should you get help?

The simplest way to come back better is to plan your exit before you go in.

Set a duration. Use music that fades out gradually. Keep a few minutes free after the practice. Avoid going straight into a meeting, a difficult journey or an avalanche of notifications.

Your sequence can become the same every time: slow down, feel your feet, open your eyes, name the place, drink some water, walk, then write a few lines. Repetition creates a clear behavioral anchor.

After an emotionally powerful experience, opt for a relatively quiet day. There’s no need to tell fifteen people about your revelation right away or to make a major decision. Let the experience settle.

Above all, some signs go beyond a simple return: persistent hallucinations outside the practice, a prolonged inability to recognize your surroundings, extreme agitation, suicidal thoughts, days of abnormally high mood and energy, loss of contact with reality or signs that may point to a problem in the brain or nerves. In these situations, seek medical or psychological help promptly.

So the art of trance isn’t only about setting off. A mature practice includes a way in, an experience and a way out.

In many traditions, coming back actually carries deep symbolic value: the experience only matters if something can then be brought back into daily life. More presence, a new question, an emotion understood or simply a feeling of calm.

The most impressive journey isn’t necessarily the one that takes you the farthest. Sometimes it’s the one you know how to come back from with both feet on the ground — and, a welcome detail, your house keys still in your pocket.

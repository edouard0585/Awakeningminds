1Khöömii: When a Single Voice Suddenly Seems to Hold Two

The first time you hear a great khöömii singer, the impression can feel almost impossible: a deep, continuous drone seems to carry, at the same time, a small high melody that is clear and sometimes close to a whistle. Yet there is no trickery and no second singer hidden behind the yurt. The phenomenon rests on an extraordinary mastery of vocal production and, above all, of resonance.

UNESCO describes Khoomei or Hooliin Chor as a style in which a performer produces a harmony made up of several perceptible parts, including a continuous low element formed in the throat. The practice is found in several communities, particularly in western Mongolia, in Inner Mongolia in northern China and in the Republic of Tuva in Russia. The Mongolian art of Khoomei was inscribed in 2009 on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

These traditions, however, are not just an acoustic curiosity. UNESCO highlights the historical links between the songs and ritual ceremonies, banquets, competitions, and tributes to ancestors, heroes and the natural world. Other archives devoted to Mongolian khöömii mention the imitation of natural sounds and its traditional transmission from bearer to apprentice.

So we must immediately resist a Western habit: turning an extremely rich cultural practice into a mere “sound meditation technique.”

Khöömii is first and foremost an art and a living heritage.

2How Does a Singer Actually Create the Impression of Two Voices?

Even an ordinary voice never emits a single frequency. When the vocal folds produce a fundamental frequency, the signal also contains a series of harmonics, meaning components located at multiples of that frequency. The shape of the vocal tract — tongue, mouth, lips, pharynx — then filters this signal and reinforces certain frequency regions.

Overtone singing exploits this phenomenon with remarkable precision.

A study published in eLife in 2020 used acoustic analysis, dynamic magnetic resonance imaging and vocal tract modeling to study Tuvan overtone singing. The researchers showed that singers could shape their vocal tract to focus certain harmonics very narrowly, notably by bringing two formants close together, thereby creating the perception of a distinct high sound above the drone.

So it isn’t necessarily a matter of two sets of vocal folds producing two independent melodies. The mechanism can be understood as a combination of a harmonic-rich vocal source and extremely specialized filtering.

Other research on low-pitched forms of throat singing has, however, shown the possible involvement of the ventricular folds, or “false vocal cords.” A study using high-speed imaging observed, in a male singer, vibrations of the ventricular folds at a frequency corresponding to half that of the true vocal folds, producing additional subharmonics.

There are therefore several techniques and several mechanisms. “Overtone singing” is not one single magic position of the throat.

3Nature, Culture and Spirituality: What the Traditions Actually Say

In the West, khöömii is often presented with an extra layer of mysticism: “healing frequencies,” “energy activation,” “cellular harmonization.” The problem isn’t that someone might subjectively experience the singing as spiritual. The problem arises when a recent interpretation is presented as ancestral Mongolian truth or as a scientific conclusion.

Carole Pegg’s ethnomusicological work is particularly useful here. Her fieldwork in western Mongolia studied xöömii in its social context, its theories of origin, its methods of transmission, and its relationship with nature and shamanism. She also highlights the contrast between certain non-Mongolian claims presenting overtone singing as a “magical vocal technique” for healing and far more complex Mongolian conceptions, which have at times considered the very production of xöömii to be physically demanding or potentially harmful.

UNESCO also reports that the songs traditionally express respect and praise for nature, ancestors and heroes. That is enough to show a deep symbolic and cultural dimension without inventing a universal energy theory.

The relationship to the landscape is particularly important in the stories surrounding these practices: wind, water, animals and the resonances of the environment become sound models. The singer isn’t simply producing a note; they are engaged in a cultural dialogue with an acoustic world.

4Does Overtone Singing Really Induce Trance?

This is where we need to carefully separate plausibility, testimony and evidence.

A repetitive drone, prolonged listening, attention closely focused on the harmonics and organized breathing can certainly produce an absorbing experience. A listener may feel relaxation, fascination, chills or a subjective change in the sense of time. An expert singer can also reach a very intense state of concentration.

But these observations don’t show that khöömii has a specific neurophysiological property that “triggers trance.”

Ethnomusicology warns us precisely against this kind of shortcut. Gilbert Rouget, in his comparative analysis of music and trance, rejected the idea that an instrument, a rhythm or a sonic feature universally holds the mechanical power to produce a trance state. Context, learning, ritual, emotion and cultural representation matter enormously.

So writing that “Mongolians have always used overtone singing to enter shamanic trance” would be far too simplistic. There are historical and symbolic relationships between certain repertoires, nature and shamanic contexts, but khöömii also has social, artistic, festive and identity-related functions that can’t be reduced to trance. UNESCO mentions, for example, horse races, archery, wrestling, banquets and ceremonies.

The beauty of the singing doesn’t need fake neuroscience to be fascinating.

5Listening, Learning and Experimenting Without Damaging Your Voice or Distorting the Tradition

If you want to use overtone singing as a contemplative support, the most respectful approach is first to listen to genuine performers, discover the different styles and understand where they come from. A listening session can become a sound meditation: choose a recording, close your eyes if that feels right, and try to follow the low drone and then the high harmonic, alternating between them. This simple, deliberate shift of attention reveals how many perceptual layers a single signal can contain.

If you want to learn to produce the sounds, it’s better to work with a competent teacher than to force your throat based on a thirty-second video. Anatomical research shows that khuumii engages the larynx, pharynx, resonance cavities, breathing and sometimes the ventricular folds in specialized ways. Pain, losing your voice or persistent hoarseness is not a sign that “energy is opening”: it is a signal to stop and, if necessary, to see a voice professional.

Finally, cultural respect is also a matter of words. You can appreciate khöömii as an art, use it as an object of attentive listening and draw inspiration from its ability to transform how we perceive sound, without claiming to be a custodian of a Mongolian tradition.

What Mongolia “knows” here is not an esoteric secret that can be boiled down to a frequency. It is something much richer: generations of singers have developed remarkable vocal techniques, an aesthetic language, ways of passing them on, and a cultural relationship between voice, landscape, community and memory. Science is beginning to explain how the vocal tract accomplishes this acoustic feat. It doesn’t need to erase the tradition; and the tradition doesn’t need to dress up as a brain scan to keep all its power.

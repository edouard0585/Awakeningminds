1Three words we mix up far too easily

In everyday language, the words shaman, healer and medium sometimes seem interchangeable. Someone says they communicate with spirits? Medium. They use a drum? Shaman. They help someone feel better? Healer. At that rate, a friendly percussionist with good intuition could end up with three different business cards.

Anthropology shows, however, that these roles can be told apart, even if the boundaries vary from one culture to another. Michael Winkelman, in particular, compared different ritual specialists across many societies and proposed distinguishing categories such as shamans, shaman/healers, healers and mediums. His study highlights that these profiles sometimes share healing, divination or altered states of consciousness, but differ in how they relate to spiritual entities and in the social context in which they practise.

So the most important point is this: these categories are comparative, not universal. A community may use completely different words, and the specialist we call a "shaman" may carry out functions that our Western vocabulary would split between priest, therapist, diviner, storyteller and healer.

We also need to separate a cultural role from a scientific claim. Saying that a person is recognised as a traditional healer or a medium in a given society is an ethnographic fact. Saying that they objectively cure an illness through the intervention of a spirit is an additional claim that requires medical evidence.

2The shaman: travelling, negotiating and acting between several worlds

In classic anthropological usage, the shaman is often described as a ritual specialist able to enter a particular state of consciousness at will in order to interact with spirits or move symbolically through other realms of the cosmos.

This definition comes mainly from the traditions of Northern Asia and Siberia, from which the word itself spread. In several of these traditions, the practitioner may be seen as travelling to an upper, lower or parallel world to look for the cause of an illness, guide a soul, negotiate with entities or obtain information.

Winkelman rightly points out that one of the features that frequently sets the shaman apart from the medium is this logic of journeying or relational mastery: the shaman is usually presented as going towards the spirits or calling on them, whereas the medium is more often described as welcoming their presence within.

This contrast is not absolute, though. Some so-called shamanic traditions also include possession, inspiration or spirits speaking through people. Real-world practices are more complex than comparison charts.

The shaman can also be a healer. They may diagnose an affliction according to a spiritual cosmology, perform a ritual, sing, drum or use sacred plants and objects. But healing is only one of their possible functions.

Likewise, hearing someone introduce themselves today as a "shaman" tells us nothing automatically about their training. In a traditional society, the role is often recognised by a community and tied to a specific line of transmission. In contemporary spirituality, the title is sometimes self-proclaimed after a very short course.

The two situations are not historically equivalent.

3The healer: the broadest of the three categories

The word healer is much more general. It can refer to anyone considered able to contribute to health through traditional, religious, symbolic or empirical means.

A healer may use plants, massage, prayer, divination, chanting, bodywork or rituals. They may or may not enter a trance. They may or may not talk about spirits.

That is precisely why not all healers are shamans.

In Winkelman's cross-cultural typologies, healers appear as a distinct category, often with greater specialisation, and able to work without the kind of spiritual journey generally associated with the shaman.

There is also a big difference between traditional healing and proven medical effectiveness.

A plant used by a healer may contain a pharmacologically active substance. A ritual may reduce anxiety or strengthen a sense of social support. An intervention may also be ineffective or dangerous.

So we should neither reject everything because it is traditional nor accept everything because it is ancestral.

The World Health Organization has, in fact, long distinguished traditional medicine systems from modern biomedicine and stressed the importance of evaluating them in terms of quality, safety and effectiveness.

A responsible healer should also acknowledge their limits. A fracture, a severe infection, a neurological crisis or cancer should not be treated with a ritual alone.

A healer's symbolic, cultural or complementary role can matter a great deal without having to replace medicine.

4The medium: when the message comes "through" the person

The medium is generally defined by a different relationship with the invisible: they act as an intermediary between the living and entities, often the dead or spirits.

In some traditions, this communication happens through speech, writing, visions or possession.

Winkelman observes that possession mediums form an anthropological category distinct from shamans in several societies: the spirit is described as temporarily taking control or speaking through the medium, whereas the shaman is seen more as the active operator of their own journey or relationship with the spirits.

This distinction is very visible in certain Korean or African traditions, where a specialist may temporarily embody a deity or an ancestor during a ritual.

Once again, we need to separate cultural description from scientific validation.

There are a great many accounts of mediumship and a long history of experimental research on the subject. But science has not established as a consensus fact that a medium objectively receives messages from people who have died.

What we can observe directly, on the other hand, is the medium's subjective experience, their changes in behaviour, the structure of the ritual and the social or emotional impact of the message.

Mediumship can therefore be lived spiritually as real communication, studied psychologically as an experience of consciousness or examined anthropologically as a social function.

These three levels are not incompatible, as long as we don't confuse them.

5How to tell who does what — and, above all, whom to trust

To understand a practitioner's role, the title displayed on their website ultimately matters much less than a few concrete questions.

Which tradition do they claim? Who trained them? Which community recognises their role? What exactly are they promising? Can they tell the difference between spiritual practice and medical treatment? Are they willing to say "I don't know"?

A genuine traditional specialist may also use vocabulary that fits none of our three categories. The best reflex is therefore to ask what the role means in their own culture rather than imposing our definition on them.

For a contemporary practitioner, transparency is essential. Someone trained in Core Shamanism can honestly present themselves as a practitioner of that method. Someone who has studied mediumship can talk about their mediumship practice. There is no need to invent an Indigenous lineage to become interesting.

Finally, be wary of absolute promises. A person claiming they can cure every illness, communicate with any deceased person on demand or guarantee the outcome of a ritual is clearly going beyond what the available evidence allows.

Shaman, healer and medium therefore belong to three families of functions that can overlap without becoming identical.

The shaman is often defined by an active relationship with the spirit world and ritual journeying. The healer by the function of care. The medium by the role of intermediary or channel.

And sometimes the same person can indeed fill two or three of these roles.

Humans love categories; traditions, on the other hand, have the annoying habit of not always fitting neatly into our boxes.

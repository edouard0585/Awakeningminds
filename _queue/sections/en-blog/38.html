1Qigong: Meditating Without Forcing Your Body to Stay Perfectly Still

Some people love sitting on a cushion for twenty minutes, watching their breath in absolute silence.

Others start thinking about their grocery list after eleven seconds, have a sore back after two minutes and, after four, wonder why they voluntarily decided to become a piece of furniture.

For these people, qigong for beginners can be an excellent gateway into meditation, conscious breathing, physical relaxation and awareness of inner sensations, because this Chinese practice combines precisely slow movement, posture, breath and attention.

The word qigong, sometimes written chi kung in older Western transliterations, can be understood as the work or cultivation of qi, a Chinese notion traditionally associated with breath, vitality and the processes that animate the body.

There are a huge number of qigong schools, with very different aims: health, martial arts, meditation, longevity, breathwork or spiritual practice.

To get started, there’s no need to know a hundred forms.

Five simple movements are more than enough to discover a fundamental feature of qigong: when movement slows down enough, sensations you almost never noticed become very clear.

Warmth in the hands.

The weight of the arms.

A gentle stretch.

The pressure of the feet.

Breath moving lower.

Tingling.

A sense of space.

Tradition may speak of the flow of qi.

Physiology will rather speak of blood circulation, proprioception, sensory attention, muscle tone, thermoregulation and interoception.

You don’t need to pick a side before you begin.

Move first.

Observe afterward.

2Before You Begin: The Wuji Posture, or How to Do Nothing Correctly

Stand with your feet roughly parallel, hip-width apart.

Keep your knees slightly unlocked.

Your pelvis is neither strongly arched nor exaggeratedly tucked.

Your shoulders drop naturally.

Your arms hang.

Your chin draws back very slightly.

Imagine the crown of your head being drawn upward and the weight of your pelvis sinking toward the floor.

In some schools, this starting posture is called Wuji.

Before the very first movement, stay like this for thirty seconds.

Feel your feet.

The heel.

The ball of the foot.

The toes.

Then observe your breathing without trying to change it right away.

This step is much more important than it looks.

Qigong doesn’t start with a spectacular movement.

It starts with the ability to feel the position you’re in.

That’s almost the opposite of how we usually function, physically in one place while mentally three places further on.

3Movement 1 — Raising and Lowering the Sky

As you inhale, slowly raise your arms in front of you to chest height.

Your palms face the floor.

As you exhale, let them float gently back down.

Repeat for about two minutes.

Don’t try to synchronize your breathing with military precision.

The movement should stay comfortable.

Simply imagine your arms floating upward and becoming heavier as they come down.

What might you feel?

A gradual warmth in your hands.

A slight fatigue in your shoulders if they’re working too hard.

A pleasant heaviness in your arms.

Your breathing slowing down.

If your shoulders tense up, you’ve probably raised your arms too high or tried to “do it right” with far too much enthusiasm.

Qigong is sometimes the difficult art of doing less.

4Movement 2 — Holding the Ball Between Your Hands

Place your hands in front of your belly as if you were holding a large ball.

One palm slightly above.

The other slightly below.

Gently move them apart.

Then bring them closer together.

Continue for two minutes.

This is where many beginners start to feel what they spontaneously call a “ball of energy.”

They may perceive:

  • warmth;
  • tingling;
  • pressure;
  • a magnetic sensation;
  • slight resistance.

These perceptions are interesting.

You just need to avoid jumping too quickly to an extraordinary physical conclusion.

When you hold your hands facing each other, your brain has a great deal of tactile, proprioceptive and thermal information to work with. Focusing your attention amplifies your awareness of these signals even further.

So you can perfectly well say:

“I feel something between my hands.”

And leave it there.

You’ve already discovered something you may never have noticed before.

That’s more than enough for a first session of energy qigong.

5Movement 3 — Opening the Chest Like Two Doors

Place your hands in front of your chest, palms facing each other.

As you inhale, gently open your arms out to the sides.

Not very far.

Just enough to feel your rib cage open.

As you exhale, bring your hands back in front of you.

Repeat six to eight times.

This movement very naturally combines breathing with chest mobility.

Try to feel:

  • the slight stretch at the front of the shoulders;
  • the opening between the ribs;
  • the movement of the breastbone;
  • the gentle closing as your hands come back.

The classic mistake is to deliberately puff your chest out as far as it will go.

This isn’t a pigeon in love showing off.

Simply let your rib cage go along with your breathing.

6Movement 4 — Swinging the Arms to Release the Body

Place your feet a little wider apart.

Let your pelvis turn gently to the left, then to the right.

Your arms stay relaxed and naturally follow the movement.

They may lightly tap your belly or your back.

Continue for a minute or two.

This movement introduces something beginners often forget: letting go.

In many physical practices, we tend to believe that progress means adding strength.

In qigong, progress sometimes means removing unnecessary tension.

When your arms become truly relaxed, the movement feels almost automatic.

The pelvis turns.

The arms follow.

The mind no longer needs to manage every joint.

This feeling of movement that “almost does itself” is one of the most characteristic pleasures of slow practices.

7Movement 5 — Bringing the Qi Back to the Belly

Finish with your hands in front of your lower belly.

For men and women alike, there’s no need to look for a point down to the millimeter.

Simply place your palms a couple of inches below your navel.

Breathe calmly.

As you inhale, feel your abdomen move slightly.

As you exhale, imagine that all the sensations scattered during the session are gathering back toward the center of your belly.

In some Chinese traditions, this area corresponds to the lower dantian, a major center of energy practice.

You can use this image without claiming that an anatomical reservoir of energy is physically located there.

From a bodily point of view, focusing your attention on the belly simply helps you reconnect with your breathing, your center of gravity and a sense of stability.

Stay for a minute.

Then let your arms drop.

8What You Should Feel… and What You Absolutely Don’t Have to Feel

After ten minutes of qigong for beginners, some people immediately feel a lot:

  • warmth;
  • tingling;
  • heaviness;
  • calm;
  • fluidity;
  • pulsing in the hands.

Others feel very little.

Both reactions are normal.

The trap is reading that you absolutely must perceive “an electric ball between your palms” and then spending the whole session waiting for it to happen.

The practice then turns into an exam.

Yet the whole point of qigong is precisely to develop a finer body awareness.

A recent meta-analysis on mindfulness and interoception found a small to moderate positive effect on subjective measures of interoceptive awareness, which broadly supports the idea that repeated attention to bodily sensations can change the way we perceive them. (PubMed)

Obviously, this doesn’t prove the existence of qi.

But it does explain why practices that combine slowness, breathing and attention can gradually make the body much more “present” in awareness.

9The Four Beginner Mistakes in Qigong

First mistake: moving too fast.

Slow down by half.

Then a little more.

Second mistake: holding your breath.

Your breathing should stay comfortable.

Third mistake: looking for extraordinary sensations.

Start by recognizing ordinary ones.

Fourth mistake: tensing your muscles too much.

If you finish ten minutes of qigong with your trapezius muscles on fire, something probably needs simplifying.

A gentle practice is not an ineffective practice.

In fact, it reduces muscular noise so you can better notice subtle signals.

10Your Complete 10-Minute Session

Minute 1: Wuji posture and natural breathing.

Minutes 2–3: raising and lowering the arms.

Minutes 4–5: holding the ball between your hands.

Minutes 6–7: opening the chest.

Minutes 8–9: relaxed arm swinging.

Minute 10: hands on the belly and returning to calm.

Practice this short sequence every day for a week.

And instead of asking:

“Can I feel the qi?”

ask a more precise question:

“What am I perceiving today that I didn’t perceive a week ago?”

That’s an excellent way to begin.

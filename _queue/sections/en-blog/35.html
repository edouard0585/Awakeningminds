1Chi, or Qi, Is Everywhere in Chinese Culture… but Simply Translating It as “Energy” Already Creates a Problem

Ask ten people what is chi? and you will probably get ten answers: an invisible energy flowing through the body, a vital breath, an inner force, something you develop through tai chi, a substance the acupuncturist gets moving, or that mysterious warm ball some people say they feel between their hands after three minutes of qigong.

The Chinese word qì, often written “chi” in older Western transcriptions, is indeed central to Chinese philosophy, Taoism, the martial arts, qigong, acupuncture and traditional Chinese medicine. But automatically translating qi as “energy” can create a misunderstanding, because the historical concept is broader and more flexible than the modern scientific meaning of the word energy. Historical research on the uses of qi shows that it served to describe bodily, cosmological, medical, respiratory and philosophical phenomena, and that it has no single equivalent in the Western biomedical vocabulary.

In the Huangdi Neijing, the great classical corpus of Chinese medicine, different forms of qi are used in particular to describe vital functions, nutrition, environmental influences and the processes associated with health and illness. The notion developed in an intellectual world where the human body was not seen as a machine made of independent parts, but as a system of transformations and relationships.

That is why a rational mind can take a genuine interest in qi without immediately having to choose between two extremes:

“Chi is a scientifically proven supernatural force.”

or

“Since it can’t be measured like electricity, the whole concept is absurd.”

There is a third, much more interesting path: understanding what qi meant historically, observing what the practices said to mobilize it actually make the body feel, and then carefully separating subjective experience from scientific claims.

2Qi, Prana, Pneuma: Why Have So Many Cultures Imagined a “Vital Breath”?

The comparison between Chinese qi, Indian prana and Greek pneuma is a common one, and even the medical terminology of the National Library of Medicine presents qi as a concept of vital force or breath roughly comparable to Indian prana, Greek pneuma and Latin spiritus.

This parallel is fascinating.

In cultures very far apart geographically, we find the idea that breath, movement, vitality, consciousness and inner life are linked.

Perhaps that isn’t very surprising.

Breathe rapidly for several minutes and your inner state changes.

Hold your breath and your entire body immediately starts to care.

Go for a run, then notice your chest, your warmth, your heart rate and your muscle tone: the impression of a “force” moving through the body comes quite naturally.

Before modern physiology appeared, humans looked for models that could organize these sensations.

The vital breath is a particularly powerful metaphor because breathing sits exactly on the border between voluntary and involuntary.

You breathe without thinking about it.

Then you can decide to slow down.

It therefore belongs both to the automatic body and to conscious action.

In qigong, this relationship is even more obvious: breath, posture, attention and movement are coordinated.

The practitioner may then feel warmth, heaviness, lightness, pulsing, tingling or expansion.

The tradition readily calls some of these experiences a manifestation of qi.

A physiologist might describe them in terms of blood flow, skin temperature, muscle activity, breathing and sensory attention.

These two descriptions simply aren’t answering exactly the same question.

3Can You Really “Feel Chi”?

Here is an easy experiment.

Gently rub your hands together for a few seconds.

Then hold them about four inches apart.

Slowly bring them closer.

Move them apart.

Repeat.

You may feel:

  • warmth;
  • tingling;
  • a sense of pressure;
  • a feeling of subtle resistance;
  • pulsing.

The temptation is immediate:

“There it is, that’s qi.”

Perhaps your own tradition uses that word for this experience.

But scientifically, there are several mechanisms that are much easier to verify.

Friction raises skin temperature.

Your hands contain an enormous number of sensory receptors.

When you strongly direct your attention to one area of the body, you perceive more of the sensations that normally go unnoticed.

Your muscles constantly adjust the position of your fingers.

The brain combines proprioception, touch, temperature and anticipation to build the overall sensation of your hands in space.

This ability to sense the inside of the body is linked to interoception, while proprioception tells you about position and movement.

Does that mean “feeling chi” is false?

Not necessarily.

The sensation is very real.

What remains to be determined is how to interpret it.

Saying:

“I feel a warmth and pressure that my qigong practice calls qi”

is a perfectly reasonable statement.

Saying:

“I have scientifically detected an unknown physical energy flowing between my hands”

would require entirely different evidence.

The nuance is small in the sentence.

It is huge in the method.

4Qi in Qigong and Tai Chi: Why Do the Exercises Really Give a Feeling of Vitality?

Qigong roughly means “work,” “cultivation” or “mastery” of qi.

The exercises generally combine posture, slow movement, breathing and attention.

Consider an extremely simple exercise:

  • stand up;
  • bend your knees slightly;
  • relax your shoulders;
  • slowly raise your arms as you inhale;
  • let them float down as you exhale.

Repeat for five minutes.

Even without believing in an invisible energy, several things happen.

You slow your behavior down.

You pay more attention to sensations.

You coordinate breath and movement.

You release certain unnecessary muscle tension.

Your body temperature may change slightly.

You pay closer attention to your hands and arms.

Sensations then become richer.

A hand you barely noticed five minutes ago can suddenly feel warm, heavy or alive with tiny tingles.

This experience probably explains part of the teaching power of energy vocabulary.

Telling a beginner:

“Observe the variations in somatosensory sensitivity, peripheral thermoregulation and neuromuscular tone”

is technically impressive but not very poetic.

Saying:

“Feel the qi in your hands”

immediately gives attention a direction.

Here, traditional language acts as a sensory map.

5Are Meridians Pipes Through Which Energy Flows?

This is another common confusion.

In some popular illustrations, the meridians of Chinese medicine look so much like electrical circuits that you could picture little cables running under the skin.

That is not how they should be understood anatomically.

Modern biomedicine has not identified a network of tubes literally corresponding to the traditional meridians and carrying a substance called qi.

The historical models of Chinese medicine instead use systems of circulation and relationship between different bodily functions.

Modern research on acupuncture studies mechanisms such as nerve stimulation, local effects, the nociceptive system, central modulation and various contextual factors.

That is not the same as demonstrating that traditional qi is identical to a particular neurological mechanism.

Even the term deqi, which refers to certain sensations sought during acupuncture, can include heaviness, numbness, distension or radiating feelings around the needle; modern literature is precisely trying to distinguish these subjective perceptions and the observable physiological changes from the traditional interpretation of qi.

6A Ten-Minute Exercise to Experience Qi Without Having to Believe in It

Stand with your feet parallel.

Soften your knees.

Take one minute to feel the weight of your body.

Then lightly rub your hands together.

Stop.

Hold them facing each other.

For two minutes, slowly bring them closer and move them apart.

Don’t look for a specific sensation.

Observe.

Next, inhale as you move your hands apart.

Exhale as you bring them together.

If you like, imagine a ball between your palms.

Continue for three minutes.

Then place your hands on your belly.

Breathe slowly.

Feel the real warmth of your palms.

Finally, stay still for two minutes.

Ask yourself just one question:

What am I actually feeling?

Not:

“What should I be feeling?”

That makes all the difference.

You can call it qi, body heat, somatic awareness or simply sensation.

The experience remains.

7So, Does Chi Exist?

It all depends on what you mean by “exist.”

Qi undeniably exists as a major historical, philosophical, medical and cultural concept of Chinese civilization. Its history is documented and its role in Chinese practices is considerable.

The sensations produced during qigong, tai chi, conscious breathing or acupuncture are also real for the people who experience them.

On the other hand, the existence of a new, measurable form of physical energy called qi, distinct from the known mechanisms of physiology, has not been scientifically established.

And in the end, that conclusion doesn’t stop you from practicing at all.

You can use qi as a map of bodily experience, a traditional language for exploring breath, posture, movement, attention and vitality.

You can appreciate an old map without claiming it is a satellite photograph.

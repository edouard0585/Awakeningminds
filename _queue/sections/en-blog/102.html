1Self-induced cognitive trance: what exactly are we talking about?

The word trance drags a particularly overstuffed suitcase behind it: drum-beating shamans, mediums, stage hypnosis, ritual dance, visions, religious ecstasy and the occasional documentary where the dramatic music works much harder than the researchers. Yet when you look at self-induced cognitive trance, the subject becomes more precise and, above all, far more interesting scientifically. It refers to a deliberately initiated subjective state in which attention, body perception, your relationship to your thoughts, the experience of time, sensations and sometimes your usual sense of identity or agency can shift. So the term doesn’t necessarily mean a loss of control or some strange kind of sleep: the person can remain awake, able to communicate and aware of the experience, while sensing their mind working in a very different way.

Part of the current scientific interest comes from research on so-called cognitive trance and on people who can enter this state at will. A 2020 paper in Clinical Neurophysiology, for example, described — cautiously, as a case study — behavioral and brain responses assessed with EEG combined with transcranial magnetic stimulation. It’s intriguing, but a single case obviously doesn’t allow anyone to declare that “the trance brain” has just been mapped. It is an exploratory observation that calls for larger studies, control groups and replications.

This caution is fundamental to this entire article: there is little doubt that a subjective experience of trance exists, since humans in many cultures describe and practice related states. What remains much harder is establishing a single neurobiological definition able to cover extremely diverse experiences.

2What the brain might change during a voluntary trance

Our ordinary state of consciousness isn’t as fixed as it looks. A gripping conversation, a drive done almost on autopilot, a deep meditation, getting absorbed in a novel or a moment of flow can already change considerably what reaches the foreground of our attention. Trance potentially pushes some of these changes further: attention can narrow or, on the contrary, widen, certain bodily sensations become extraordinarily vivid while others fade, and the usual inner dialogue can change pace or lose its importance.

So neuroscientists are looking less for a hypothetical “trance button” than for a set of changes in networks linked to attention, sensory integration, body awareness, agency and self-representation. However, research is still too limited to provide a universal signature. A TMS-EEG case study by Olivia Gosseries and her colleagues is a good example of this approach: rather than interpreting the experience as supernatural or imaginary, the researchers objectively examined the brain responses associated with the reported state.

This approach allows an essential distinction. Saying that the brain shows changes during trance doesn’t mean that “trance is all in your head” in the sense of being fake. All our conscious experiences — falling in love, listening to Bach, feeling a burn or watching a sunset — necessarily have a brain dimension. Conversely, observing a change in the brain doesn’t automatically validate the spiritual interpretation someone gives to their experience. A practitioner may speak of an inner journey or a spiritual connection; the lab measures electrical activity, behavior and physiology. Both accounts can coexist, provided neither claims to be measuring the same thing.

3How can you learn to enter an unusual state voluntarily?

The idea of a self-induced trance is particularly interesting because it removes an intermediary: no one else is needed to guide the state. Practitioners gradually learn to recognize certain internal signals and to use natural breathing, attention, movement, sound, mental imagery or deliberate shifts in body perception as gateways.

We do need to distinguish, however, between culturally transmitted techniques and what has been scientifically validated. Shamanic traditions have long used drums, chanting, dance or rhythmic repetition, but their historical presence doesn’t demonstrate that a particular tempo “automatically synchronizes brain waves.” The ethnomusicologist Gilbert Rouget devoted a major book to the relationship between music and trance and rejected simplistic explanations that turn a drumbeat into some kind of universal neurophysiological switch. According to his analysis, cultural context, learning, collective representations, emotion and ritual are inseparable from the way music accompanies trance.

For careful personal exploration, a gentle approach is therefore to start from an attention practice rather than a hunt for extreme sensations: sit or lie down in a safe place, observe your bodily sensations for a few minutes, keep steady attention on a repetitive sound or a mental image, then notice, without forcing, any changes in perception. The goal isn’t to provoke dizziness, hyperventilation or loss of control. An interesting trance doesn’t need to be spectacular to be deep.

4Trance, meditation, hypnosis and dissociation: cousins, but not twins

A major difficulty comes from vocabulary. Meditation, hypnosis, absorption, dissociation and trance can share certain phenomena without being synonyms. Mindfulness meditation, for example, can strengthen observation of present experience, while a hypnotic practice may use suggestion and imagination far more explicitly. A ritual trance may involve movement, music and cultural representations absent from the first two.

Dissociation calls for even more care. In everyday language, the word is sometimes used to describe any feeling of distance from the body. In clinical settings, dissociative phenomena can become involuntary, distressing and disruptive to daily life. So deliberately seeking an unusual state is not the same as suffering from pathological dissociation.

This distinction matters especially for people with a history of trauma, significant dissociative episodes, psychosis, neurological seizures or other conditions that some intensive practices might worsen. In these situations, experimenting alone with techniques designed to strongly alter perception isn’t a good idea; appropriate medical or psychological advice is preferable.

In the end, the word “trance” describes a family of phenomena more than a single mechanism. Insisting on fitting all these experiences into one box is a bit like putting sleep, lucid dreaming and napping in the same drawer because all three may involve a pillow.

5What science is really discovering — and the huge questions that remain open

The most interesting advance may not be the discovery of some mysterious brain frequency of trance, because that is precisely where we are not. It lies rather in the fact that these experiences are becoming objects of experimental study. Researchers can compare the ordinary state with a voluntarily induced one, record EEG, analyze behavior, examine body perception and gradually build reproducible protocols.

The study by Gosseries and colleagues shows that a neurophysiological investigation is feasible, but its status as a case study calls for great restraint. Other work on altered states of consciousness, hypnosis, meditation or bodily experience offers complementary conceptual tools, without, however, justifying merging all these categories.

The next questions are fascinating: do two trained people use the same brain networks to enter trance? Does practice gradually transform the experience? Are some features shared across different traditions? How much comes from attention, movement, expectations or social context? And above all, what differences exist between a controlled voluntary trance and an involuntary dissociative experience?

For now, the best position is to resist both extremes. The first would be to dismiss all trance as pointless fantasy. The second would be to turn every EEG result into validation of objective spiritual journeys. Between these two caricatures lies a vast scientific territory: that of a human brain capable of deliberately changing its own conscious experience. That alone is extraordinary enough without needing any neurological glitter.

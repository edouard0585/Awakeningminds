1Kundalini: A Symbolic Serpent Turned Global Phenomenon

Of all the concepts that come from yoga and tantra, few carry an image as powerful as kundalini: an energy pictured as a serpent coiled at the base of the spine which, when it awakens, is said to rise through the central axis of the subtle body, pass through the chakras and lead to profoundly transformed states of consciousness.

It’s beautiful.

It’s also exactly the kind of image that can give rise to an enormous amount of exaggeration.

Online, a headache, a shiver during meditation or a particularly restless night can sometimes instantly become:

“My kundalini is awakening.”

Then, three videos later:

“It’s reached my crown chakra.”

So it’s better to first understand what kundalini means in the yogic and tantric traditions before interpreting the slightest tingle as a cosmic revolution.

In several tantric systems, kundalini is described as a latent power associated with the base of the subtle body. Its ascent is linked to practices of yoga, breathing, concentration, mantra and meditation, with an extremely rich symbolism around the ida, pingala and sushumna channels and the various subtle centers.

The modern Western version, however, has hugely simplified and standardized these traditions.

The famous diagram of “seven chakras lined up like the colors of a rainbow, with kundalini rising from red to violet” is extremely well known today, but it synthesizes and modernizes historical systems that were far more diverse.

2What Does a “Kundalini Awakening” Look Like, According to Firsthand Accounts?

Contemporary accounts of kundalini awakening describe extremely varied experiences:

  • a sensation of energy rising up the spine;
  • intense heat;
  • shivers;
  • vibrations;
  • spontaneous body movements;
  • unusual breathing;
  • powerful emotions;
  • mental images;
  • a sense of unity;
  • changes in how the body is perceived;
  • strong sensitivity to noise or light;
  • periods of euphoria or peace;
  • sometimes also fear, insomnia, confusion or emotional instability.

The scientific problem is immediately obvious.

These experiences are not specific to kundalini.

Trembling, shivers, perceptual changes, intense emotions or unusual bodily sensations can appear in very different contexts: meditation, rapid breathing, stress, sleep, hypnagogia, ritual practice, fatigue, music, physical exercise or intense psychological states.

That doesn’t mean the experience has no value.

It means a single symptom doesn’t automatically tell you where it came from.

If you suddenly feel a wave of heat during meditation, you know for certain that you’re feeling a wave of heat.

The rest is interpretation.

3What Is the Famous “Kundalini Syndrome”?

The term kundalini syndrome is used in some psychological and spiritual writing to describe difficult episodes interpreted as being linked to an intense energetic awakening.

But the term doesn’t correspond to a standard medical diagnosis recognized as such in modern psychiatric classifications.

There is, however, a much broader and scientifically documented topic: the adverse effects of meditation and contemplative practices.

A systematic review published in 2020 showed that meditation-related adverse events were reported in the literature, including anxiety, depression and cognitive or perceptual abnormalities in some practitioners. A more recent review also stresses that meditation isn’t always neutral or pleasant for everyone, and that some people may experience anxiety, re-experiencing of trauma or disrupted functioning.

This absolutely does not mean meditation is dangerous in general.

It means we need to drop the naive idea that:

“More intense = always better.”

A contemplative practice can be a form of training.

And like any training, the intensity needs to be adjusted.

4Why Can Some Practices Produce Extremely Strong Sensations?

Several techniques traditionally linked to energy practices strongly alter bodily experience.

Rapid or forced breathing, for example, can change carbon dioxide levels in the blood and cause sensations such as dizziness, tingling, muscle contractions or perceptual changes.

Long periods of stillness heighten awareness of sensations that were previously ignored.

Lack of sleep during some retreats can also alter your mental state.

Intensive repetition of mantras, chanting, rhythm, prolonged concentration and the ritual setting also help change attention and how sensations are interpreted.

From a traditional perspective, these phenomena can be described as the movement of prana or kundalini.

From a physiological perspective, they potentially involve breathing, attention, autonomic arousal, sleep, emotions and expectations.

The two accounts can coexist as interpretive frameworks.

But when symptoms become worrying, physiology and mental health must come first.

5Kundalini Yoga: Don’t Confuse the Symbolic Tradition With Modern Programs

The term Kundalini Yoga refers to a range of practices today.

Some modern protocols combine postures, breathing, meditation, chanting and repetitive movements.

Clinical research on these programs does exist. A recent systematic review of randomized controlled trials identified fifteen studies on various applications of Kundalini Yoga and reports promising results on certain psychological, cognitive and physical measures, while stressing the need for larger, better-standardized trials with longer follow-up.

This matters because it helps avoid two opposite mistakes.

First mistake:

“Kundalini Yoga has no effect at all, since kundalini energy can’t be measured.”

A program combining breathing, movement, meditation and attention can obviously produce psychological and physical effects, regardless of whether the energy model is literally true.

Second mistake:

“Since some studies show benefits from Kundalini Yoga, science has proven that kundalini exists.”

No.

Studying the effects of a practice is not the same as validating the entire cosmology that comes with it.

A tai chi class can improve balance without demonstrating the physical existence of qi.

Same logic.

6Precautions: What Is Best Avoided

⚠️ Important precautions

Avoid deliberately chasing an extreme experience by piling up forced breathing, sleep deprivation, fasting, long meditations and intensive practices without serious supervision.

If a practice causes any of the following over time:

  • significant insomnia;
  • panic;
  • confusion;
  • derealization;
  • disorganization;
  • worrying unusual perceptions;
  • severe agitation;
  • inability to function normally;
  • stop or reduce the practice and seek advice from a qualified health professional.

People with a history of psychotic, manic, dissociative or significant trauma-related disorders should be especially careful with intensive practices and should not automatically treat every mental change as an “energetic purification.”

A spiritual experience and a clinical problem are not always mutually exclusive.

You can respect the spiritual meaning someone gives to an experience while making sure their health stays protected.

7How to Explore Kundalini Without Pushing the Big Red Button

To get started, there is absolutely no need to try to “raise” anything.

Use a gentle practice.

Sit comfortably.

Breathe naturally for two minutes.

Then feel the contact between your pelvis and the seat.

Next, mentally follow your spine from the pelvis up to the top of your head.

No effort.

No tensing.

No rapid breathing.

Simply imagine a vertical line.

As you breathe in, let your attention rise slightly.

As you breathe out, come back down to the pelvis.

Five minutes.

Then finish by feeling your feet and hands.

This back-and-forth movement avoids the idea that every good energy practice should always “go up.”

In many contemplative practices, knowing how to come back down into the body, eat, walk, talk to someone and return to ordinary activity is just as important as having intense experiences.

The real sign of growth may not be seeing a thousand inner suns.

It may be managing to empty the dishwasher afterward.

1“Nature spirit”: a handy Western expression that hides very different worlds

Saying that shamanic traditions believe in nature spirits sounds simple. Yet this phrase lumps thousands of very different religious relationships under a single label.

A mountain may be regarded as a power.

A river may have a spirit master.

An animal may be understood as a non-human person.

A place may have an intention, a memory or a capacity to act.

In some cosmologies, the question isn’t even whether a stone “contains a spirit” separate from itself. The stone itself can enter into a person-to-person relationship.

This is exactly what makes the modern concept of animism interesting.

Older anthropology often presented it as the naive belief that so-called “primitive” peoples attributed a soul to objects. That view has since been seriously challenged.

In her landmark 1999 article, anthropologist Nurit Bird-David proposes understanding animism as a relational epistemology: a way of knowing the world through the relationships one maintains with human and non-human beings.

The shift seems subtle, but it’s huge.

We no longer only ask:

“Why do these people believe a tree has a spirit?”

We ask:

“What kind of relationship with the tree allows it to be treated as a partner or a person?”

This second question immediately takes us away from the folklore catalog of woodland fairies and into a genuine philosophy of relationship.

2Animism doesn’t mean “everything has a tiny invisible soul”

British anthropologist Graham Harvey has also played an important role in renewing the study of animism. His approach emphasizes worlds populated by human and other-than-human persons, only some of whom are biologically human.

This framing helps us better understand traditions in which animals, plants, ancestors, mountains or other entities can take part in relationships of communication, obligation and respect.

Among the Nayaka of India, studied by Bird-David, animism is understood through relationality and social practice rather than as a simple abstract theory claiming that “everything has a soul.”

This nuance is worth keeping when we talk about shamanism.

A ritual specialist doesn’t necessarily operate in a world divided between “dead matter” and “spirits hidden inside.”

They may live in a universe where certain entities that the modern West classifies as objects or resources already have a form of agency or personhood.

Relationship then becomes central.

You ask permission.

You offer something.

You follow certain hunting rules.

You give thanks.

You negotiate.

These practices aren’t scientific proof that a mountain really has a consciousness comparable to a human’s.

What they do show is that a society can build ethical relationships with the non-human world that are very different from those of modern Western naturalism.

And that is already a remarkable intellectual discovery.

3Animals, mountains, rivers and forests: the “spirits” change from culture to culture

It would be tempting to draw up a universal dictionary:

Wolf = protection.

Eagle = vision.

River = emotions.

Mountain = wisdom.

That is exactly what to avoid.

The meaning of an animal or a place depends on the history, ecology and mythology of a particular people.

In several Siberian traditions, animals that matter for hunting can have complex relationships with humans and ritual specialists. In some Amazonian societies, animals may be conceived of as subjects with their own perspective. In the Korean practices of Jeju, rituals are addressed to the wind goddess, the Dragon King and the mountain deities in direct connection with fishing, the seasons and the sea.

The ecological context isn’t just a backdrop.

A people that depends on the sea doesn’t necessarily build the same religious relationships as a population of reindeer herders or a society living from hunting and tropical horticulture.

Spirits therefore also reflect a way of inhabiting a territory.

That explains why importing a spiritual entity out of its context can quickly become incoherent.

Asking “the spirit of the buffalo” to guide your walk in a European forest isn’t forbidden by some metaphysical police, of course.

But truly getting to know the animals, plants, rivers and stories of the place where you live can produce a far less decorative relationship.

The sacred often begins two miles from home rather than five thousand.

4Can you communicate with a nature spirit? Tradition, inner experience and scientific evidence

In many shamanic or animist cosmologies, yes: communication with non-human beings is considered real.

The shaman may receive information in a dream, a trance, a song or a vision. An animal may be understood as a messenger. A natural phenomenon may be interpreted as an answer.

From a scientific point of view, the situation is different.

We know that humans can produce extremely convincing experiences of images, inner voices, presence or dialogue during dreams, meditation and altered states of consciousness.

We have no evidence establishing that an independent spirit of a tree or a mountain objectively communicates with the human brain.

So you can keep two different formulations:

“During my meditation, I experienced a dialogue with the spirit of this tree” honestly describes an experience.

“This tree objectively transmitted supernatural information to me” is an additional claim that would require independent evidence.

This distinction in no way prevents you from using the experience symbolically.

You can silently ask a river: “What can you teach me about change?”

Then notice the associations that come up.

Psychologically, the river then serves as a support for contemplation.

Spiritually, someone may consider it a genuine relationship.

The two readings aren’t identical, but they can coexist as long as one isn’t disguised as proof of the other.

5Reconnecting with the living world without artificially imitating an Indigenous tradition

The modern interest in nature spirits probably also reveals a dissatisfaction with our contemporary way of treating the environment as a collection of available resources.

The relational animism studied by Bird-David offers a fascinating idea here: the world can be known through the relationships we maintain with it, and not only through classification and extraction.

That doesn’t mean abandoning science.

A tree has a physiology that can be studied scientifically.

It exchanges water, carbon and nutrients.

It belongs to an ecosystem.

Nothing prevents a human from building an aesthetic, symbolic or spiritual relationship with that same tree at the same time.

To practice without usurping a particular tradition, then, start extremely simply.

Choose a natural place near you.

Go back there regularly.

Learn the real names of the species.

Observe the seasons, insects, birds, soil and weather.

For a few minutes, stay silent and listen.

If you want to personify the place in your mind, ask what a respectful relationship would require of you.

Then add the part some spiritualities curiously forget: do something concrete.

Don’t leave any litter.

Support local biodiversity.

Avoid offerings that could pollute.

Help protect the place.

A spiritual relationship with nature that physically destroys nature has a slight consistency problem.

Nature spirits, whether you see them as metaphysical realities or as symbolic ways of relating to the living world, ultimately shift the center of gravity.

Humans are no longer alone on stage.

They become one participant among many.

And perhaps the most important lesson of many animist traditions isn’t that we absolutely must believe trees talk.

It’s that it might be worth starting, for once, by learning to listen to them.

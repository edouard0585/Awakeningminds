1The Library of Alexandria was not just a building filled with scrolls: it embodied an almost excessive project

Imagine a cosmopolitan city in the Hellenistic world in which sovereigns decide that accumulating knowledge constitutes a form of power.

Poetry.

Mathematics.

Medicine.

Astronomy.

History.

Philosophy.

Geography.

The texts are copied, compared, corrected and cataloged.

Scholars live and work nearby.

Different versions of a work can be compared in order to try to establish the best possible text.

Welcome to Ptolemaic Alexandria.

The famous Library of Alexandria, closely linked to the Mouseion, developed under the first Ptolemies and helped make the city one of the great intellectual centers of the ancient Mediterranean world. (World History Encyclopedia)

Modern fascination often focuses on the question:

“How many scrolls did it contain?”

Ancient and modern figures vary enormously and their interpretation is delicate.

But the project is almost more interesting than the number.

The implicit goal seemed extraordinarily ambitious:

collect, organize and work on the largest possible set of available texts.

It is one of the first great Western dreams of an almost total library.

And that dream never really went away.

We just moved it.

Alexandria.

Imperial libraries.

Encyclopedias.

Archives.

Internet.

Then, in a completely different register:

akashic records, cosmic library, universal memory.

The media change.

The fantasy remains:

somewhere, everything is preserved.

2No, the Library of Alexandria probably did not disappear in a single gigantic Hollywood fire

The popular version is perfect for the cinema.

A gigantic building.

Thousands of scrolls.

Flames.

Julius Caesar.

All ancient knowledge goes up in smoke in a single night.

The problem is that the real story seems much more complicated.

During the battles at Alexandria in 48 BCE, Caesar set ships on fire in the port and the flames actually caused destruction; several ancient sources mention burnt scrolls, but scholars have long debated what exactly was destroyed, in particular possible warehouses near the port rather than the entire great library. (World History Encyclopedia)

Above all, the disappearance of the great Alexandrian intellectual center seems to have been much more gradual.

Loss of patronage.

Political crises.

Expulsion of scholars.

Conflicts.

Successive destruction of neighborhoods.

Transformation of institutions.

The royal district was particularly heavily affected during the conflicts of the 3rd century CE.

The Serapeum, associated in certain traditions with a secondary collection, was destroyed in 391, but we do not know with certainty what collection of books was still there at that moment. (World History Encyclopedia)

As for the story according to which the Caliph Omar ordered the burning of books in the 7th century to heat the baths of Alexandria, it appears in much later sources and is dismissed by historians as an unreliable account. (World History Encyclopedia)

The truth is therefore less spectacular:

the library seems to have declined, suffered and gradually disappeared rather than exploded in a single night.

History loses a great fire.

It gains several centuries of complexity.

3The true genius of Alexandria was not only in keeping the texts, but in organizing them

Accumulating a million documents without a system to find anything essentially amounts to inventing the badly organized hard drive.

Alexandrian scholars were therefore not content with collecting.

They classified.

Edited.

Compared.

Commented.

Callimachus, poet and scholar of the 3rd century BCE, is notably associated with the Pinakes, a vast bibliographic catalog of the library’s authors and works, often considered a major ancestor of organized bibliography.

The texts could be divided by categories:

  • tragic poetry;
  • comedy;
  • history;
  • medicine;
  • rhetoric;
  • law.

Scholars also worked on textual criticism, authenticity and the establishment of works. (World History Encyclopedia)

This seems almost banal today because we live in a world of:

  • search engines;
  • tags;
  • databases;
  • indexes.

But the idea is fundamental.

Having the information is not enough.

You need to know:

  • where it is located;
  • who produced it;
  • which version is reliable;
  • how it compares to others.

Ironically, this problem has once again become absolutely central in the Internet age.

We have almost realized the dream of a universal library.

But we have simultaneously created a new question:

how can we distinguish the true, the false, the important and the useless when we can find everything?

Alexandria wanted to accumulate.

Our time must above all learn to filter.

4Why the idea of a total library then reappears in literature and spirituality

The idea that somewhere there could be an archive containing everything has an almost metaphysical appeal.

If all the books exist, perhaps all the answers exist.

If all events are recorded, nothing is completely lost.

Literature has produced a dizzying version with Jorge Luis Borges and his short story The Library of Babel, in which the universe takes the form of a library containing all possible combinations of characters.

This potentially implies:

  • your exact biography;
  • its false version;
  • the demonstration of all theories;
  • and several billion volumes of gibberish.

Total knowledge then becomes a paradox:

if everything exists, how can we find what is true?

Modern esoteric traditions develop another version of the dream with the akashic records, conceived by different authors as a sort of subtle or cosmic archive of events and souls.

This modern idea must be distinguished from the ancient Sanskrit concept of ākāśa, which has a much more complex philosophical and religious history; the Western notion of a gigantic “cosmic file” is particularly linked to theosophical and esoteric developments of the 19th and 20th centuries.

We therefore pass:

from the material papyrus of Alexandria

to

Borges’ imaginary library

then

to

the metaphysical archive of esotericism.

Three totally different libraries.

But the same anxiety runs through them:

what if something important was lost?

5Why we find it so hard to accept the idea that knowledge can disappear

A lost text has a fascinating power precisely because we can no longer read it.

We know that many ancient works have not reached us.

Sometimes we only know a work thanks to a quotation from another author.

Then immediately imagine:

a brilliant medical treatise?

an unknown chronicle?

a revolutionary philosophical work?

Maybe.

Or maybe three hundred pages on a tax problem of the Hellenistic era.

Loss creates a perfect space for imagination.

This is one of the reasons why the Library of Alexandria symbolically became the library of all lost books, although it would be historically false to imagine that it literally contained all the knowledge of Antiquity or that all the lost texts disappeared with it.

Many works had several copies and circulated in different cities; the transmission of ancient knowledge to Byzantine, Arab, Latin and other traditions is much larger than the history of a single library. (World History Encyclopedia)

However, the symbol works.

Alexandria represents that nightmarish moment when we imagine:

“What if we had lost something irreplaceable?”

This fear also explains our contemporary obsession with:

  • digital archives;
  • backups;
  • copies;
  • cloud;
  • universal libraries.

We replaced the papyrus with the server.

The anxiety is the same.

6The total library almost exists today — and we are discovering that having all the answers is still not enough

We now carry in our pocket access to more texts, images and knowledge than a Ptolemaic ruler could probably have imagined.

And yet, we do not feel omniscient.

Why?

Because information is not knowledge.

And knowledge is not wisdom.

You can have:

a thousand articles on meditation

without meditating for ten minutes.

One hundred books on relationships

without knowing how to listen to a person.

All the encyclopedias on sleep

and still look at your phone at 2 a.m.

Perhaps the real lesson of the universal library lies there.

We have spent millennia dreaming of a place where all information would be available.

Then we almost got this world.

And we discover that the next problem is:

what should we read?

Then:

what should we believe?

Then:

what should we do with what we know?

Alexandria represents the dream of conservation.

Borges shows the nightmare of excess.

The celestial records represent the dream that nothing is ever truly lost.

And our time adds a fourth library:

the Internet, where almost everything seems accessible but where human attention remains hopelessly limited.

Perhaps the perfect library is not the one that contains all the books.

It’s the one that helps you find, at the right time, the one you really need.

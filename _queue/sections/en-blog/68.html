1Your Consciousness Already Changes Several Times a Day

When people hear the phrase altered state of consciousness, they easily picture something extraordinary: a shaman beating a drum, a yogi absorbed in deep meditation or someone going through a spectacular mystical experience.

Yet your conscious experience is changing all the time.

You wake up after a particularly realistic dream and need a few seconds to work out where you are.

You drive along a familiar road and suddenly realize that several minutes seem to have vanished.

You are so absorbed in a movie that you jump when someone speaks to you.

You go for a run and gradually slip into a state where the action feels almost automatic.

You fall asleep and, for a few seconds, see absurd images flickering behind your eyelids.

All these phenomena point to a fundamental idea: consciousness is not a switch with only two positions, awake and asleep.

It is more like a multidimensional landscape.

The following can change independently:

  • attention;
  • the perception of time;
  • body awareness;
  • mental imagery;
  • the sense of identity;
  • sensitivity to the environment;
  • voluntary control;
  • emotional intensity.

So the phrase “altered state of consciousness” is useful, but extremely broad. Researchers often prefer to describe precisely which dimensions change rather than throwing meditation, hypnosis, dreaming and trance into one giant bag.

2Sleep and Dreams: The Extraordinary State We Find Perfectly Normal

Every night, something remarkable happens.

Your body stays in your bedroom.

But your conscious experience can place you:

  • in a city;
  • in your childhood;
  • on a mountain;
  • with someone who has died;
  • or in a supermarket where your old math teacher is suddenly selling dragons.

During a dream, the brain generates an immersive experience complete with perception, emotions and a storyline.

And we consider this perfectly normal.

Lucid dreaming adds an extra layer: the dreamer realizes they are dreaming while the dream carries on.

Research using predefined eye signals has experimentally confirmed that some participants can become lucid during REM sleep and signal their lucidity from inside the dream.

Lucid dreaming thus shows, quite spectacularly, that the categories “awake” and “asleep” are not always enough.

You can be physiologically asleep while still having a degree of metacognition:

“I know this experience is a dream.”

Consciousness loves gray areas.

3Hypnagogia: The Wild Cinema of Falling Asleep

Between waking and sleep lies a particularly strange region: hypnagogia.

You are still vaguely aware.

Then an image appears.

A face.

A sentence with no context.

A sensation of falling.

A sound that may never have existed in the room.

You come back slightly.

Then drift off again.

These phenomena can include images, sounds, bodily sensations and fragmented thoughts.

They are generally normal when they occur around the time you fall asleep.

Hypnagogia is enormously interesting for understanding altered states of consciousness.

It shows how experience can gradually become less dependent on the outside environment and more internally generated.

The room disappears.

Imagination takes over.

And this can happen without any substance and without any particular spiritual technique.

Sometimes all it takes is being very tired in a comfortable bed.

4Meditation: Changing Your Relationship to Experience Rather Than Putting On a Show

Not all meditation produces a spectacular altered state.

And it doesn’t need to.

A mindfulness meditation may simply increase your awareness of thoughts and sensations.

A concentrative practice can produce far more intense absorption.

Some contemplative traditions also describe states in which:

  • the perception of the body changes;
  • time seems to slow down or disappear;
  • the boundaries between self and environment become less prominent;
  • imagery becomes very vivid;
  • a sense of unity arises.

Contemporary research on meditation shows that different practices can engage attentional, self-referential and sensory systems in different ways.

This makes the idea of a single “meditating brain” highly doubtful.

Open-awareness meditation, tantric visualization and intensive concentration on an object don’t do exactly the same thing.

The term meditation describes a family of practices.

Like “sport.”

Marathon running and archery are both sports.

Yet nobody expects the same training.

5Flow: When Action Absorbs Almost Everything Else

Flow, popularized by Mihály Csíkszentmihályi, arises when a person becomes deeply absorbed in an activity that is challenging enough to engage their abilities without completely exceeding them.

Musician.

Athlete.

Artist.

Programmer.

Surgeon.

Gamer.

Time may seem to speed up.

Self-commentary fades.

Movement becomes fluid.

Attention focuses almost entirely on the action.

This resembles certain aspects of trance.

But flow usually has a concrete goal and involves ongoing interaction with a task.

In a contemplative trance, by contrast, the content can become mainly internal.

The difference matters.

But the similarities suggest that absorption is probably one of the great natural gateways to altering conscious experience.

6Hypnosis and Trance: When Attention Meets Suggestion

In hypnosis, attention usually becomes focused and suggestion takes center stage.

One can suggest:

  • a sensation of heaviness;
  • a lighter arm;
  • a change in pain;
  • an image;
  • a distortion of time.

The person does not become a robot.

They actively take part in constructing the experience.

Shamanic trance, on the other hand, takes place in a very different cultural setting and may use rhythm, drumming, chanting, movement, cosmological storytelling and a relationship with spirits.

Both can involve absorption and altered perception.

But lumping them into the same category without specifying the context would lose exactly what makes them interesting.

A clinical hypnosis lab and a traditional ceremony are not simply two different sets of decor around the same phenomenon.

Context influences what the person expects, feels and interprets.

7Ecstasy, Prayer, Chanting and Dance: When the Body Joins In

Some states of consciousness are hard to reach just by sitting still.

For a very long time, humans have used:

  • repetitive dance;
  • chanting;
  • percussion;
  • rocking;
  • prayer;
  • breathing;
  • collective movement.

The body itself then becomes the engine of transformation.

Rhythm steadies attention.

Repetition reduces novelty.

Music shifts emotion.

Movement produces new internal sensations.

The group adds synchronization and social meaning.

After long enough, ordinary perception can change profoundly.

That’s why ritual ecstasy is not simply meditation with more decibels.

It uses a different architecture of consciousness.

9Can You Explore These States Without Substances?

Absolutely.

The most accessible methods are:

  • meditation;
  • yoga nidra;
  • self-hypnosis;
  • lucid dreaming;
  • music;
  • dance;
  • visualization;
  • gentle breathing;
  • prayer;
  • chanting;
  • creative immersion.

The healthiest rule is to build up intensity gradually.

Start with a short practice.

Observe how you react.

Come back fully.

Sleep normally.

Eat normally.

Keep up your usual activities.

You don’t need to turn every session into an expedition to the edges of reality.

The most interesting ability isn’t necessarily:

“How far can I go?”

But rather:

“How precisely can I observe what changes?”

10The Extraordinary May Lie in the Flexibility of Consciousness

Altered states of consciousness become far more interesting once you stop seeing them as a collection of paranormal phenomena.

They reveal something fundamental:

our experience of reality depends enormously on how attention, body, memory, imagination and context work together.

Time can feel different without the clock changing.

The body can feel immense without having grown.

An inner image can become almost as convincing as a perception.

The sense of self can become quieter.

That doesn’t mean everything is an illusion.

It means that the way we experience reality is far more malleable than we imagine.

And that discovery is dizzying enough without adding anything to your glass.

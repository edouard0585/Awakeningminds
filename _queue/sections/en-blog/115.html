1The mudang: a religious tradition still alive in ultra-modern Korea

In Seoul, you can travel just a few subway stops from a neighborhood bristling with digital screens and skyscrapers to a space where a mudang, a term commonly translated as “shaman,” prepares offerings, changes costume several times, sings, dances and addresses deities or ancestors. This coexistence is one of the most fascinating features of Korean shamanism: it isn’t just a folkloric relic kept behind glass, but a set of practices that keeps evolving within an industrial, technologically advanced society.

Researchers often speak of Korean shamanism, Muism or Korean folk religion, but these terms cover different regional and historical realities. Ritual specialists don’t all follow the same path, and the term mudang itself doesn’t capture the full range of local diversity. Anthropologist Laurel Kendall showed in her fieldwork how closely folk religious practice was tied to family life, everyday concerns and, historically, the experience of many women. Her book Shamans, Housewives, and Other Restless Spirits has become a major reference for understanding this dimension of Korean religion.

We should also avoid treating the mudang as exact equivalents of the Siberian specialists the word “shaman” originally referred to. The comparison is anthropological: in both cases, certain practitioners are understood as mediators between humans and non-human entities. Korean institutions, myths, techniques and social roles nonetheless remain specifically Korean.

2The gut: when music, dance, costume, divination and spirits form a single ritual

The visible heart of many practices is the gut — also romanized as kut — a ritual that may include prayers, music, songs, dance, costume changes, offerings, mythic narratives and sometimes ritual possession. A gut can be held for many reasons: prosperity, protecting a family, resolving a difficulty, accompanying the deceased or the well-being of a community.

It would be reductive to present this ritual as a mere technique for “going into trance.” It is more of a full-scale religious drama in which participants, musicians, objects, food, clothing and deities each have a defined place.

Some Korean traditions are now officially recognized as cultural heritage. The Jeju Chilmeoridang Yeongdeunggut, inscribed by UNESCO in 2009 on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, is celebrated on Jeju Island during the second lunar month. Shamans perform rites addressed in particular to the wind goddess Yeondeung, the Dragon King and the mountain deities, asking for calm seas, abundant harvests and good fishing. The ritual includes summoning the gods, offerings, divination and launching a straw boat onto the water.

The Gangneung Danoje festival, also inscribed on UNESCO’s intangible heritage list, includes shamanic rites addressed to guardian mountain deities and illustrates the historical coexistence, within a single festival, of shamanic, Buddhist and Confucian elements.

Korean tradition therefore looks less like an isolated religion than a landscape in which different religious layers have long lived side by side.

3Possession, calling and sinbyeong: when becoming a mudang isn’t presented as a simple career choice

In some lineages, becoming a mudang is traditionally interpreted as the consequence of a spiritual calling. A notion known as sinbyeong, often loosely translated as “spirit illness,” describes a set of physical or psychological ordeals that may be interpreted as a sign that a person is being called to become a shaman.

Within this religious framework, the resolution sometimes comes through a naerim-gut, an initiation ritual marking acceptance of the calling. The future practitioner then enters an apprenticeship with an experienced mudang, often framed as a relationship between a “spirit mother” and a “spirit daughter.” One study of Korean ritual possession describes this path in particular, along with different forms of possession observed during certain gut.

However, there is a crucial line to respect between cultural interpretation and clinical diagnosis. If a Korean community identifies certain symptoms as a spiritual calling, anthropology can document that understanding and study its social effects. This doesn’t mean that an episode of psychosis, severe depression, epilepsy or a dissociative disorder is scientifically caused by a spirit.

Conversely, automatically reducing every religious phenomenon to a pathology would completely miss its cultural dimension.

For a modern reader, the practical rule is simple: an unusual experience can be given a personal spiritual interpretation, but persistent, dangerous or seriously disabling symptoms also call for a medical or psychological assessment.

Spirituality can give meaning to an experience. It shouldn’t stop you from getting your health checked.

4A religion often carried by women and deeply tied to everyday problems

One of the most interesting aspects of Korean shamanism is the historical place held by women. In a social world long structured by strongly patriarchal Confucian hierarchies, folk rituals offered some women a space where their concerns, family relationships and emotions could find powerful religious expression.

Laurel Kendall’s work shows exactly this closeness between mudang, households and daily life. The people who come for consultations aren’t necessarily seeking “cosmic enlightenment.” They may be worried about marriage, money, health, children, ancestors, work or family tensions.

It’s a useful correction to the Western image of the shaman preoccupied solely with the mysteries of the universe.

Often, the supernatural also busies itself with why the family business isn’t doing well anymore.

During a gut, emotional expression can be intense and theatrical. The spirits are invited, welcomed, sometimes entertained or appeased. In possession traditions, a deity or a deceased person may be considered to speak through the mudang.

From within the religious tradition, the spiritual presence is real.

From a scientific point of view, we can study changes in voice, behavior, attention, emotion, cultural learning and dissociative states without being able to confirm that an external entity has actually taken possession of the body.

This dual reading makes it possible to respect both the tradition and the limits of what can be demonstrated.

5Mudang on YouTube: why modernity hasn’t made the spirits disappear

Korean shamanism offers an excellent counterexample to the old idea that modernization and science would inevitably make all folk religious practices disappear. Mudang continue to exist, some appear in the media and on social networks, while rituals are protected as cultural heritage.

This survival doesn’t mean, however, that the tradition has stayed unchanged.

Practices adapt to cities, tourism, new technologies, changing family structures and the social status of practitioners. Some rituals also become heritage performances accessible to a much wider audience than the community that originally gave them meaning.

UNESCO notes, for example, that the Gangneung Danoje has undergone heavy media exposure and cultural standardization, leading to the disappearance of certain traditional elements. In other words, protecting a tradition can paradoxically transform it.

For anyone interested in shamanism today, Korea offers a particularly useful lesson: a living spiritual practice is never a snapshot of the past.

Contemporary mudang may work in an environment full of smartphones, cars and apartment blocks while addressing ancestral deities. That obviously doesn’t prove these spirits objectively exist. It shows something quite different: human modernity has never been as rationally uniform as we sometimes like to claim.

Korean shamanism therefore deserves better than a simple “Asian trance” label. It is a complex religious world combining family, history, women, music, possession, divination, ritual theater, ancestors and local identity.

Above all, it reminds us that an ancient ritual doesn’t have to stay frozen to stay alive.

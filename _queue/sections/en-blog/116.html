1Michael Harner: from academic anthropology to a new Western spiritual method

Few people have shaped the way the contemporary West imagines and practices shamanism as much as Michael Harner. Born in 1929 and deceased in 2018, this American anthropologist carried out fieldwork in South America before gradually moving from the academic study of shamanism to practicing it and then teaching it.

According to the account published by the Foundation for Shamanic Studies, Harner developed his own practices after experiences in the Upper Amazon in the 1960s. He began teaching small groups in the early 1970s and in 1979 founded the Center for Shamanic Studies, which later became the Foundation for Shamanic Studies.

His most influential book, The Way of the Shaman, published in 1980, played a major role in spreading drum-based shamanic journeying throughout the West.

Harner did not, however, claim to pass on any single, specific Indigenous tradition. Instead, he built what he called Core Shamanism. The idea was to identify, by comparing different cultures, elements he considered universal or very widely shared in shamanism, then to separate them from their specific cultural contexts in order to create a system accessible to Westerners.

It is precisely this ambition that explains both the enormous success of his approach… and the criticism it draws.

Because one question comes up immediately: when you strip a tradition of its language, myths, kinship, land and history to keep only the “common techniques,” do you get its universal core, or are you building something new?

2What is Core Shamanism according to the Foundation for Shamanic Studies?

The Foundation for Shamanic Studies today defines Core Shamanism as a set of features that are universal, near-universal or common to shamanic traditions, together with journeying to other worlds.

The signature practice is the shamanic journey.

The participant lies down or settles in comfortably, closes their eyes and usually listens to a repetitive drumbeat. They imagine or experience an entrance — a tunnel, an opening in the ground, a tree, a cave — then explore what Harner calls “non-ordinary reality.”

In the language of the tradition Harner developed, they may meet power animals, spirit teachers or other allies there.

It’s important here to clearly separate the levels of interpretation.

For practitioners who embrace the cosmology of Core Shamanism, these entities may be considered genuinely real in a non-ordinary dimension.

For psychology, the experience can be examined as a combination of mental imagery, absorption, expectations, rhythm, attention and symbolization.

Science has not demonstrated the objective existence of a “non-ordinary reality” populated by spirits independent of the brain.

Harner, however, explicitly defended a realist reading of these spiritual experiences. The FSS also presents Core Shamanism not as a mere imagination technique but as a method that gives access to spirits and to a hidden reality.

This distinction matters for understanding that the system isn’t just a relaxation method: it really does contain a spiritual worldview.

3Why the system became such a huge success in the West

Harner’s practical genius was to offer a spirituality that was relatively easy to learn and required neither conversion to an established religion nor the use of psychoactive substances.

A few tools are all you need: a drum or a recording, an intention, visualization and a conceptual framework.

The person also gets an active role. They don’t have to wait for a priest to hand down a revelation: they can undertake a journey themselves, question what they identify as a guide, then come back with a message or an image.

For a Western culture that prizes autonomy and individual experience, the formula was an extremely good fit.

The FSS itself explains that its teaching was designed specifically to help Westerners regain access to a spiritual heritage that the organization considers largely lost in Europe as a result of historical religious repression.

From the 1980s onward, the trainings spread widely. Harner left his university post in 1987 to devote himself to the foundation and to teaching.

The system has also influenced a multitude of practices that don’t strictly follow the FSS: drumming circles, neo-shamanic sessions, retreats and personal development programs.

The paradox is delicious: a method designed to extract a “universal core” from shamanism has itself ended up becoming an identifiable tradition with its own history, vocabulary and institutions.

4The anthropological problem: is there really a universal shamanism hidden beneath cultures?

This question is the trickiest point.

Harner saw similarities between different traditions: soul flight, helping spirits, rhythmic techniques, healing and communication with a hidden reality. From this he inferred the existence of a cross-cultural core.

But contemporary anthropologists generally place more emphasis on historical and local differences.

An Evenki specialist from Siberia, a Korean mudang and an Amazonian healer may all be called “shamans” in Western books, even though their societies share no language, mythology, ritual or religious institution.

By isolating what looks similar, you risk erasing precisely what gives these practices their meaning.

Core Shamanism should therefore be understood as a modern synthesis developed by Michael Harner, not as a universal prehistoric tradition finally reconstructed by science.

The FSS itself states that it is a system “not bound to any specific cultural group.” That very description sets it apart from historical Indigenous traditions.

You can perfectly well find a rich spiritual practice in it without claiming that it reproduces exactly what Indigenous peoples did.

This historical honesty may be the best way to avoid the famous spiritual game of “it’s 40,000 years old” — a discipline in which birth certificates unfortunately tend to go missing.

5Practicing Core Shamanism today: spiritual experience, symbolism and critical thinking

For anyone wanting to explore this approach, the drum journey can be seen as a structured practice of spiritual imagery.

Set a simple intention.

Choose a limited duration.

Settle into a safe place.

After the experience, write down exactly what you perceived before interpreting it.

An animal you meet can be seen as a spirit ally within the cosmology of Core Shamanism, but also as a psychological symbol, a product of the imagination, or a mix of memories and expectations.

Keeping these different readings open doesn’t necessarily make the experience any less profound.

You should also stay cautious about the notion of shamanic healing. The FSS teaches methods it presents as spiritual healing practices. That doesn’t mean they replace a diagnosis, psychotherapy or validated medical treatment.

A person can work symbolically on their sense of loss, their relationship with nature or their imagination while carrying on with their usual care.

Core Shamanism is ultimately one of the most important phenomena in contemporary Western spirituality: neither a simple reproduction of an Indigenous tradition, nor an ancient universal system proven by science.

It is a modern spiritual construction, born from the work of an anthropologist turned practitioner, which has profoundly changed the way hundreds of thousands of people approach the word “shamanism” today.

Its richness may show itself more clearly when we study it for what it really is than when we try to manufacture an extra layer of antiquity for it.

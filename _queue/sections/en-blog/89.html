1You Open Your Eyes, You Are Conscious… but Your Body No Longer Responds at All

The scene is spectacular enough to be etched in your memory: you feel as though you are awake, maybe you see your room, you hear the sounds around you and you know perfectly well where you are, but when you try to raise an arm, turn your head or simply sit up, nothing happens; to make the experience even more convincing, some people simultaneously feel pressure on the chest, hear voices, see a figure near the bed or feel certain that a presence is in the room.

This is called sleep paralysis.

The phenomenon can occur when falling asleep, but it is particularly well known during an awakening in which consciousness returns before the muscular inhibition characteristic of REM sleep has completely lifted.

During REM sleep, the brain generates particularly rich dreams while a large part of the voluntary muscles is temporarily inhibited; this muscle atonia is normal and probably very useful, since it prevents us from physically acting out most of the movements of our dreams.

In sleep paralysis, two normally well-coordinated states briefly overlap:

waking consciousness returns, but certain REM sleep mechanisms are still active.

Result:

you are conscious.

Your body is still sleeping a little.

And since the human brain particularly hates incomprehensible situations, it sometimes begins to produce an explanation.

This is where things can get downright cinematic.

2Why Do So Many People See a Figure, a Presence or Something Sitting on Their Chest?

Sleep paralysis is known in many cultures, but the stories used to explain it vary enormously: nocturnal demons, witches, spirits, jinns, ghosts, supernatural visitors or creatures that come and sit on the sleeper’s chest, and this cultural diversity is fascinating because it shows how a similar physiological experience can receive extremely different interpretations.

The common thread is often the sense of presence.

Sometimes you don’t even see anything.

You simply know:

“Someone is there.”

Hallucinations associated with the transition between sleep and wakefulness may combine dream imagery, real perception of the room and anxious anticipation, creating a particularly convincing hybrid state; you actually see the wardrobe, but part of the dream production may still be active, so that a shadow becomes a person, a noise becomes a voice and the difficulty moving is interpreted as an external force holding you down.

The feeling of pressure on the chest also has a possible explanation that is much less supernatural: during REM sleep, breathing of course continues, but some accessory respiratory muscles are less available, and deliberately trying to breathe “more” while feeling paralyzed can produce an extremely distressing feeling of tightness.

Usually, you are still breathing.

But your brain, already panicked by the lack of movement, interprets every sensation as confirmation of danger.

This produces a remarkably efficient loop:

I can’t move → something is wrong → fear → heightened sensations → the impression that the danger is increasing.

Understanding this loop before the next episode often profoundly changes the experience.

3Why Does Sleep Paralysis Happen, and Who Is More Likely to Experience It?

Occasional sleep paralysis can occur in an otherwise healthy person, but various factors seem to increase its likelihood, notably sleep deprivation, irregular schedules, certain sleep disorders and probably stress in some people.

The episodes are also particularly associated with narcolepsy, although having sleep paralysis obviously does not mean you have narcolepsy.

This distinction is important.

An isolated episode after a week of chaotic sleep does not have the same meaning as a regular combination of paralysis, intense daytime sleepiness, uncontrollable bouts of falling asleep and other characteristic symptoms.

Some studies have also found an association with sleeping on the back, which matches the frequent testimony of people who say that their episodes occur mainly when they sleep on their backs; this does not mean that sleeping on your back always causes paralysis, but changing position may be a reasonable experiment for someone who notices a very clear connection.

Stress and anxiety can play an indirect role, notably by disrupting sleep and making nighttime awakenings more frequent, while the fear of having another episode can itself begin to disrupt falling asleep.

Here is a particularly unpleasant trap:

you have an episode of paralysis.

You are afraid of having a second one.

You sleep less well.

Irregular sleep may make conditions more favorable.

You have a second episode.

You say to yourself:

“I knew something was wrong.”

The work then consists as much of stabilizing sleep as of changing the interpretation of the episode.

4What to Do During Sleep Paralysis: Three Simple Actions Rather Than a War Against Your Body

The first thing to remember is:

the episode usually passes on its own.

In the moment, thirty seconds may feel like ten minutes, but episodes of paralysis are usually brief.

First action:

name what is happening.

“It’s sleep paralysis. My brain is waking up before my body. This will pass.”

This phrase can significantly reduce the catastrophic feel of the experience, especially if you have prepared it in advance.

Second action:

don’t necessarily try to move your whole body at once.

Trying to get up with all your strength and finding that nothing moves can amplify the panic.

Some people find it easier to focus their intention on a small movement:

  • a finger;
  • a toe;
  • the lips;
  • the eyes.

The literature does not allow this strategy to be presented as a guaranteed technique, but it is consistent with numerous testimonies and at least has the advantage of reducing the all-out struggle.

Third action:

look at or imagine something neutral.

If a figure seems to be standing at the foot of the bed, avoid spending the episode analyzing its intentions.

Remember:

“Part of my dream system may still be active.”

The fear may not disappear instantly, but you take away the hallucination’s status as proof.

You can also pay attention to your breathing as it already is, rather than trying to take gigantic breaths, which can make it feel even more as though your chest is “resisting.”

The key word is:

let it finish.

5How to Reduce the Likelihood of Episodes Without Organizing Your Whole Life Around Fear

When the episodes are occasional, the first strategy is particularly boring but probably more useful than many mysterious remedies:

protect your sleep.

Try to get enough of it.

Keep a relatively regular schedule.

Avoid, as much as possible, extremely short nights followed by major shifts in your schedule.

If you notice that the episodes occur almost exclusively when you are on your back, try another position.

Also reduce the anxiety around the phenomenon by learning what is really happening, because anticipatory fear can turn bedtime into a period of constant vigilance.

A mistake would be to gradually build up twenty mandatory rituals:

  • a specific light;
  • a protective object;
  • an exact position;
  • exact music;
  • the door open exactly three centimeters.

If these behaviors become necessary for you to feel safe, they may end up reinforcing the idea that paralysis is a danger that must be actively neutralized.

The best psychological antidote often remains:

“It’s very impressive, but I know this phenomenon.”

If you practice lucid dreaming or certain conscious sleep-onset techniques, you may also encounter the boundaries between wakefulness and sleep more deliberately; if these experiences become anxiety-provoking or disrupt your rest, cutting back on these practices is perfectly reasonable.

Exploring consciousness is interesting.

Being afraid to go to bed is much less so.

6When Sleep Paralysis Warrants Seeing a Doctor

Isolated sleep paralysis is usually not a cause for concern on its own.

However, it is useful to talk to a sleep professional when it becomes very frequent, causes significant fear of going to bed or is accompanied by symptoms such as excessive daytime sleepiness, uncontrollable bouts of falling asleep, muscle weakness triggered by emotions or other signs that could point to a specific disorder such as narcolepsy.

It is also necessary to distinguish an experience linked to the sleep transition from hallucinations that occur regularly in the middle of the day when you are fully awake, because the diagnostic context is obviously not the same.

The most important thing is probably to strip the experience of its aura of unknown threat.

You are not necessarily “stuck between two worlds.”

You may, much more simply, be stuck for a few moments between two physiological states of the same brain.

And this explanation, even if it is less spectacular than a demon sitting on your chest, has a considerable advantage:

the next time your room suddenly fills with an ominous presence, you already have a much less frightening hypothesis.

13 – 6 – 5: three numbers that are incredibly easy to remember

365 cardiac coherence rests on a particularly simple formula:

3 times a day.

6 breaths per minute.

5 minutes per session.

That’s where the famous number 365 comes from.

Not a date.

Not a mystical frequency.

Not a cosmic message encrypted in your smartwatch.

Three.

Six.

Five.

In practice, six breaths per minute means one full inhale-exhale cycle lasts about ten seconds.

So the simplest version is:

  • 5 seconds breathing in;
  • 5 seconds breathing out.

And repeating that rhythm for five minutes.

That’s it.

This simplicity explains a large part of the success of cardiac coherence breathing: you can do it sitting at your desk, in a parked car, at home, before a meeting, after a tense day or between two stretches of work.

But behind this apparent simplicity lies some far more interesting physiology.

2Your heart doesn’t beat like a metronome, and that’s excellent news

If your average heart rate is 70 beats per minute, your heart doesn’t necessarily produce a beat exactly every 857 milliseconds.

The interval between two beats varies slightly.

This variation is called heart rate variability, or HRV.

For example:

  • one interval might last 850 ms;
  • the next 810;
  • then 900.

These variations are normal and depend in particular on breathing and autonomic regulation.

When you breathe in, your heart rate generally tends to speed up slightly.

When you breathe out, it tends to slow down.

This phenomenon is called respiratory sinus arrhythmia.

This isn’t a pathological “arrhythmia”: it’s a normal physiological phenomenon.

When you deliberately slow your breathing to around a certain rate, the oscillations linked to breathing, heart rate and blood pressure can fall more closely into sync.

That’s where cardiac coherence gets really interesting.

3Why six breaths per minute?

Six breaths per minute corresponds to a frequency of about 0.1 Hz.

Around this frequency, many adults come close to their cardiovascular resonance frequency, even though the exact value varies from person to person.

A scientific review on slow breathing explains that breathing around this resonance frequency increases heart oscillations and can improve indices associated with vagal heart rate variability and baroreflex sensitivity. (PubMed)

The baroreflex is one of the mechanisms involved in the rapid regulation of blood pressure.

Sensors located in the arteries, among other places, detect changes in pressure and help adjust cardiovascular activity.

During slow breathing, the breathing rhythm interacts strongly with this system.

More recent work continues to study this phenomenon: a study published in 2026 describes an increase in functional connectivity within central autonomic networks during paced breathing at about 0.1 Hz, or roughly six breaths per minute. (PubMed)

So the number “6” wasn’t pulled out of a hat.

But you shouldn’t treat it as a universal, sacred biological value either.

In some people, the individual resonance frequency can be slightly different.

For a daily well-being practice, six breaths is simply an excellent approximation.

4How to practice cardiac coherence properly

Sit down comfortably.

You don’t have to be in a meditation posture.

A chair will do perfectly.

Put your feet flat on the floor.

Let your shoulders relax a little.

Then use the following rhythm:

  • breathe in for 5 seconds;
  • breathe out for 5 seconds.

Ideally, breathe without forcing.

Your belly can move naturally.

You don’t need to suck in five liters of air with each inhale.

That’s a common mistake.

When people read “deep breathing,” they sometimes think:

“I have to fill my lungs to the max!”

The result: they breathe too hard, feel slightly dizzy and conclude that cardiac coherence is turning them into an astronaut.

Good breathing is slow, not gigantic.

The depth should stay comfortable.

If five seconds feels long, start with four seconds in and four seconds out, then slow down gradually.

5Why three times a day?

The rule of three daily sessions is essentially about turning the exercise into a regulation routine rather than a technique you only use in a crisis.

A simple schedule could be:

8:00 a.m. — after waking up or breakfast

Goal: start the day with deliberate breathing and a steady rhythm.

1:00 p.m. — around your lunch break

Goal: interrupt the build-up of morning stress.

6:00 p.m. — end of the day

Goal: create a transition between work and personal time.

Obviously, you don’t have to stick to these times.

The best strategy is often to attach the practice to three things that already happen:

  • after your coffee;
  • before lunch;
  • after closing your laptop.

That way, cardiac coherence becomes a well-being habit rather than one more task on a list that’s already far too long.

And what if you can only practice once a day?

Do it once.

An imperfect practice is infinitely more real than an ideal program you never start.

6What science can really say

There’s a lot of research on slow breathing and heart rate variability.

A meta-analysis of 223 studies showed an increase in several HRV parameters associated with parasympathetic regulation during slow breathing sessions, immediately after some sessions and after some repeated interventions. (PubMed)

The long-term effects are more complex, though.

A review of the cardiovascular mechanisms of slow breathing found acute improvements in certain parameters, including baroreflex sensitivity in some studies, but stresses that long-term results remain more mixed. (PubMed)

That’s why it’s better to present 365 cardiac coherence as a simple tool for slow breathing, autonomic regulation and stress management, rather than as a method capable of “curing” a long list of diseases.

That’s already a lot.

The 365 protocol in a nutshell

Three times a day.

Sit down.

Breathe in for five seconds.

Breathe out for five seconds.

Keep going for five minutes.

Don’t force it.

If your head starts spinning, go back to normal breathing.

Then get back to your day.

Five minutes of conscious breathing probably won’t make all your problems disappear.

But for five minutes, at least, you’ll stop adding new ones in your head.

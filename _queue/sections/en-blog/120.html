1How do you become a shaman in traditional cultures?

Usually not by choice: the modern idea that you can "become a shaman" after a few training modules is historically very far removed from many of the traditions in which this role has been studied.

In Siberia, Central Asia and other regions, ethnographic accounts often describe a vocation regarded as imposed by the spirits, inherited or recognized by a community. In these accounts, shamanic initiation follows a calling rather than a free choice.

The person is not necessarily a willing candidate. On the contrary, some accounts describe resistance, fear or illness before the role is accepted.

In traditional societies, becoming a shaman generally means entering a web of relationships: with spirits, a family, ancestors, a social group, a territory and ritual rules.

The practitioner's identity is therefore not just an inner experience.

In his work on the shamanism of Northern Asia, Charles Stépanoff shows how closely the categories of shamanic people are tied to local ideas of singularity, personhood and relationships with the non-human world.

The community also plays a crucial role.

Someone who claims, on their own, to have received a spiritual mission does not necessarily gain the status of shaman automatically.

In several societies, their ability must be recognized, tested and then woven into a set of social practices.

The modern world has sometimes replaced this collective validation with a printed certificate.

Paper is certainly easier to frame and hang on the wall, but it is not quite the same institution.

2What is the initiatory illness in shamanism?

It is a period of suffering that many ethnographic accounts mention before someone takes up the shamanic role.

It may involve illness, intense dreams, visions, unusual behavior, isolation or a feeling of being called by spiritual entities.

In the traditional interpretation, these phenomena can amount to an initiatory illness.

Mircea Eliade did much to popularize the idea of the future shaman symbolically going through death, dismemberment and rebirth.

More recent research, however, has emphasized the diversity of traditions and the danger of turning this motif into a universal script.

Northern Asian specialists may be recognized in very different ways, and not every initiation involves a dramatic crisis.

Above all, this concept should not be used today to automatically relabel a serious mental health problem, or an illness affecting the brain, as a "shamanic awakening."

Periods of extreme, sleepless excitement, losing touch with reality, seizures or long spells of feeling detached from yourself can produce experiences perceived as deeply spiritual while still needing a health professional's assessment.

The two dimensions are not mutually exclusive.

A person can give spiritual meaning to a difficult period and receive care.

This principle becomes especially important when someone is isolated in a society where no traditional community has the mechanisms to hold and frame the experience.

Telling someone who is losing touch with reality that they are simply "becoming a shaman" can dangerously delay the care they need.

3What does shamanic initiation involve?

Mostly long training: in several traditions, initiation turns the candidate into a practitioner able to use, within a structured framework, experiences that were once chaotic.

The training may cover myths, chants, ritual objects, spiritual genealogy, dietary rules, instrument making, conduct during rituals and relationships with specific spirits.

It can last for years.

Initiation is therefore not just a spectacular ceremony. It is often a process of education and socialization.

This point is essential to understanding why a single mystical experience is not enough to make someone a traditional specialist.

A person can have an extraordinary vision without knowing how to conduct a funeral rite, interpret a chant, handle a request from the community or respect the taboos attached to the role.

The practitioner must learn not only how to enter certain states but also how to come out of them, how to interpret experiences within their cultural system and how to put them at the service of other people.

In mediumship traditions, ethnographic research also shows that training can consist of giving a stable cultural form to an experience that was initially unpredictable. Emmons's study of mediumship at Lily Dale, for example, describes the gradual socialization of future mediums in a community where their experiences are given rules and a shared vocabulary.

Initiation thus turns an individual experience into a social skill.

4Is neo-shamanism real shamanism?

It depends mostly on what you mean by "shaman": a modern method is not the same thing as a traditional role.

Today there are modern systems such as Core Shamanism, in which people learn drum journeying, symbolic techniques and practices for relating to what they consider to be spirits.

These methods can become genuine spiritual disciplines.

But they do not automatically make someone the equivalent of an Evenki, Tuvan, Korean or Amazonian specialist.

A more accurate way of putting it is to say:

"I practice neo-shamanism,"

"I am a Core Shamanism practitioner,"

or

"I work with shamanic-inspired techniques."

These words have an unglamorous but precious advantage: they say exactly what you do.

The problem arises when someone claims a traditional authority that doesn't exist.

Having learned to drum does not automatically give you access to the rites of a culture whose language and myths you don't know.

Modern practice can be perfectly valid without dressing itself up as ancient.

Ethics also need some thought: knowing your limits, respecting the cultures of origin, refusing to make impossible medical promises and being able to refer a participant in distress to an appropriate professional.

That is often a far better measure of a practitioner's maturity than their feather collection.

5What does serious shaman training look like today?

If you feel drawn to this path, you may wonder how to become a shaman today. It is better to see the training as a long discipline rather than a status to acquire quickly.

Start with history and anthropology.

Study at least a few specific traditions so that you understand how they differ.

Then develop a steady personal practice: meditation, journaling, body awareness, symbolic work or gentle trance techniques.

Above all, learn to stay grounded, to recognize your limits and to handle emotional reactions.

If you work with other people, basic knowledge of psychological first aid, trauma, consent and the situations in which a practice is unsafe becomes extremely useful.

The ability to say "this situation is beyond my skills" is an underrated spiritual skill.

In a traditional setting, training and responsibility go hand in hand. The specialist has obligations toward those who come to consult them.

A modern practitioner should apply the same principle.

Finally, resist the urge to chase only extraordinary experiences.

A good guide is not the one who has seen the most entities in their meditations.

It is the one who knows how to create a safe setting, listen, avoid imposing their interpretations and stay grounded enough not to turn every human problem into an energy attack.

Becoming a shaman, in the traditional sense, therefore depends on a culture and on community recognition that cannot be artificially reproduced.

Becoming a contemporary practitioner inspired by shamanism, on the other hand, is possible.

But the more honest the training, the less it probably needs to proclaim itself exceptional.

1Smoke cleansing: a practice far older and more diverse than modern "energy clearing"

Burning an aromatic material so that its smoke accompanies a prayer, a rite, an offering or the symbolic transformation of a place is an extremely widespread practice throughout human history. Resins, woods, seeds, leaves and herbs have been used in very different traditions, from the Mediterranean basin to the Americas and Asia. Yet in spiritual circles today, the term smoke cleansing covers a set of practices that are sometimes merged under a single idea: "clearing bad energy".

This phrasing can be used as symbolic or religious language, but it should not be turned into a scientific claim. No recognised physical measurement currently shows that a bundle of burnt plants removes an invisible "negative energy" from a room. On the other hand, the smoke, the scents, the ritual gesture, any music and the intention can genuinely change our psychological experience of a space. A room in which we deliberately perform a rite of transition may feel different afterwards, notably because our attention and emotional associations have shifted.

This distinction makes the practice more interesting rather than less. You don't need to prove the existence of a paranormal cloud hiding behind the sofa to decide that a ritual marks the end of a difficult period.

But not all smoke cleansing is culturally equivalent. What social media readily calls smudging has specific roots in Native North American ceremonies. Using that word for any plant burned in a bedroom erases specific religious histories.

You can perfectly well do a personal smoke cleansing without claiming to reproduce a rite that belongs to another tradition.

2White sage: why this plant deserves more respect than a quick online purchase

White sage, Salvia apiana, grows naturally mainly in Southern California and northern Baja California. It has documented food, medicinal and ceremonial importance for several Indigenous peoples of the region, including the Chumash, Cahuilla, Kumeyaay and Luiseño.

The California Native Plant Society points out that these communities have maintained relationships with this plant for countless generations, and directly criticises its recent transformation into a standardised commercial "cleansing" accessory. The organisation also stresses that some bundles sold commercially may come from illegal harvesting or from sources of doubtful traceability.

So the problem has two dimensions.

The first is cultural: certain forms of purification by smoke belong to religious traditions with their own prayers, protocols and meanings. Superficially copying the aesthetic without knowing that history can amount to cultural appropriation.

The second is ecological and commercial: global demand concentrated on a plant with a relatively limited geographical range encourages harvesting networks that are not always transparent.

This doesn't mean that every non-Indigenous person who touches a white sage leaf is automatically committing a cultural offence. The situation is more nuanced. But if your goal is simply to create a moment of symbolic purification at home in Europe, there is a remarkably reasonable question to ask: why would you necessarily need to import a sacred Californian plant when Europe has dozens of aromatic herbs of its own?

Rosemary won't ask to see your spiritual passport.

3Palo santo: between sacred wood, global marketing and traceability questions

The palo santo sold as incense often comes from Bursera graveolens, a tree of the tropical dry forests of South America. Its aromatic wood is used in various local contexts and has gone massively international in the wellness market.

You often read two contradictory claims: "palo santo is endangered" or, conversely, "no problem at all, it's completely sustainable".

The reality calls for more precision.

International trade in wild species is governed by complex monitoring and regulatory systems, and the mere fact that a species is not listed as threatened on a given list does not guarantee that every local commercial supply chain is sustainable. The CITES database itself points out that interpreting the trade in species requires looking at taxon, origin, country, purpose and type of product rather than a simple binary status.

For consumers, then, the right strategy is to look for a clearly stated provenance, a documented supply chain and a seller who can explain where the wood comes from. Be wary of vague descriptions along the lines of "harvested according to an ancient sustainable tradition in the heart of the sacred forest" when no country, producer or supply chain is mentioned.

There is also a subtler phenomenon: in the West, palo santo is often sold with a generic cosmology that mixes the Andes, the Amazon, "shamanism" and self-help. Yet the real uses of the wood vary by region and population.

You can love its scent without turning every stick into a relic of an imaginary spiritual civilisation.

4Local alternatives: rosemary, juniper, bay laurel and other plants that bring the ritual closer to home

To build a coherent personal practice, it is often more interesting to look around you.

In France and across Europe, rosemary, bay laurel, juniper, lavender, thyme and certain mugworts each have a long medicinal, culinary, symbolic or ritual history. This doesn't mean they should be burned carelessly, or that every species is suitable for inhalation. The idea is rather to choose plants that are abundant, correctly identified and legal to gather.

Rosemary is a very good example: its scent is strong, it grows easily in many regions and its Mediterranean history is rich enough that there is no need to invent a Native American ancestry for it.

A ritual can then become deeply local.

Open a window.

Place a small amount of dried plant material in a fireproof container.

Define your intention precisely: ending a difficult day, preparing for meditation, marking a house move, symbolically accompanying grief or simply changing the sensory atmosphere of a room.

Let a small amount of smoke circulate, then put the material out completely.

You can also do away with burning altogether: an aromatic infusion, a bunch of herbs, a hydrosol, suitable diffusion or simply opening the windows can serve the same symbolic function for anyone who doesn't want smoke.

Spirituality survives fresh air surprisingly well.

5"Natural" smoke is still smoke: safety should be part of the ritual

This is probably the most overlooked point. Burning a plant transforms its organic matter through combustion. The fact that it is organic, sacred or picked with a beautiful intention doesn't change the laws of atmospheric chemistry.

Studies on incense smoke show the possible presence of fine particles, carbon monoxide, volatile organic compounds, aldehydes and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. One medical review notably recommends reducing exposure and ventilating rooms when incense is burned. A more recent review of particles from incense also confirms that combustion contributes to indoor air pollution.

This doesn't mean that an occasionally burned sprig of rosemary is automatically equivalent to occupational exposure in a smoke-filled temple. Dose, duration, ventilation and individual susceptibility matter enormously.

But people with asthma or lung disease, those who are very sensitive to smells or those living with young children should be particularly careful.

Also keep smoke cleansing away from sensitive animals and never leave smouldering material unattended.

The most elegant ritual is still the one that doesn't end with the smoke alarm going off.

Smoke cleansing can therefore remain a beautiful practice: sensory, meditative, symbolic and sometimes inherited from a specific tradition. It simply benefits from being rid of two confusions: believing that all spiritual smoke comes from the same culture, and believing that "natural" automatically means "harmless to your lungs".

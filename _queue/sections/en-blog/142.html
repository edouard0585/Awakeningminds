1What are the signs of astral travel?

A heavy body, a buzzing sound, the feeling of floating. But these signs can also signal the start of a lucid dream. That is the ambiguity at the heart of what is sometimes called an astral dream. Picture the scene.

You are lying down.

Your body grows heavy.

You feel a buzzing.

You feel as if you are floating.

Then you find yourself in your bedroom, but something seems slightly different.

For some practitioners, this is clearly an astral projection.

For others, it is a lucid dream that began in bed.

The problem is that the two experiences have a lot in common.

Both can happen around sleep.

Both can bring vibrations, a sense of movement and strikingly realistic scenery. Both can include partial control and a setting that looks like the real world.

A 2024 theoretical review of the links between out-of-body experiences (OBEs), lucid dreaming and sleep paralysis highlights exactly this closeness. Its authors suggest that some sleep-related OBEs may be linked to hybrid states. Unusual transitions between waking and REM sleep may also play a part.

A phenomenological analysis published in 2025 goes even further. The researchers conclude that many features traditionally used to tell lucid dreams and out-of-body experiences apart are not reliable enough. They propose focusing above all on the sense of disembodiment as the core feature of an OBE. It is the experience of perceiving yourself as existing without your physical body.

So the boundary is far less clear than spiritual manuals sometimes like to suggest.

2How do you recognize a lucid dream?

A lucid dream has a fairly simple definition: during the dream, the person becomes aware that they are dreaming.

They do not necessarily control everything.

They may simply think:

“This is a dream.”

Lucid dreaming is a well-established phenomenon in science.

Laboratories have verified it with trained dreamers. During the dream, they make a sequence of voluntary eye movements agreed on in advance. The physical eyes reproduce these signals during REM sleep, which marks the moment the sleeper becomes lucid.

Methods for inducing lucid dreams are also being studied.

The International Lucid Dream Induction Study, published in 2020 with 355 participants, tested several techniques. It found that MILD and SSILD could help increase the chances of a lucid dream under certain conditions.

A lab study combining a nighttime awakening with the MILD technique also produced lucid dreams in a number of participants. None had been selected for any special ability, and some of these dreams were confirmed by eye signals.

So we can say with confidence that a human being can become aware during a dream.

What this ability does not automatically show is that every bedroom-like scene or out-of-body journey must be a lucid dream.

3How do you recognize an out-of-body experience?

In a classic out-of-body experience, the central element is different.

The person feels that their conscious point of view itself is located outside the physical body.

Sometimes they can watch their body from a high vantage point.

The neuroscience literature often describes three components:

  • the feeling of being located outside the body;
  • perceiving the world from that outside position;
  • sometimes, seeing your own body from a distance.

Olaf Blanke's work shows that disruptions in how the brain combines the senses can help trigger this kind of experience. The area around the temporoparietal junction is especially involved.

That is an important difference from lucid dreaming.

In a lucid dream, you know:

“I am in a dream.”

In an out-of-body experience, you may think:

“I have really left my body.”

But this criterion remains imperfect.

A person can have a lucid dream in which they feel completely disembodied.

Conversely, a person can have a sleep-related out-of-body experience without recognizing that they are dreaming.

This is why the 2025 analysis suggests focusing more on the experience of disembodiment than on the interpretation given afterward.

4Astral projection vs lucid dreaming: how do you tell the difference?

After the experience, start by writing down what happened.

Then ask yourself five questions.

First question: did I know I was dreaming?

If so, that clearly points to a lucid dream.

Second: did I feel my point of view clearly leave my body?

If so, the experience looks more like an OBE.

Third: was the setting stable?

Careful: this criterion is not decisive.

Some lucid dreams are extremely realistic and coherent.

Fourth: was I still aware of my physical body lying down?

Some out-of-body experiences involve a double feeling: the physical body is there, and the self is somewhere else.

Fifth: did the experience begin during sleep paralysis, or as I was falling asleep?

If so, it is very likely linked to the physiology of sleep. On its own, though, that does not tell you whether it was a lucid dream or an OBE.

So these questions do not work as a metaphysical detector.

They help you classify the experience more precisely.

A common mistake is to think that a realistic setting automatically proves the person was really outside their body.

Dreams can reproduce familiar places in great detail.

Likewise, noticing something odd in the bedroom does not necessarily prove that the experience was “only a dream.”

5Does a very realistic astral dream prove you left your body?

No. To demonstrate astral projection in the literal sense, you would need to go beyond the story itself.

A person would have to reliably perceive information out of reach of their senses. And that result would have to be repeated under controlled conditions.

This is where the distinction between experience and proof becomes essential.

An experience can be extremely convincing.

It can change a life.

It can feel more real than an ordinary dream.

But intensity and accuracy are not the same thing.

To date, research on out-of-body experiences strongly supports the existence of a subjective feeling of disembodiment. It also offers several plausible neurological models.

It has not firmly established that consciousness can travel outside the body and observe its surroundings independently.

That does not stop anyone from holding a spiritual interpretation.

A person can say:

“I believe this experience was an astral projection.”

That is a belief, or an interpretation.

Saying:

“Science has proven that I left my body”

would be incorrect.

The best approach is probably to document the experience precisely, without rushing to a conclusion.

And if you want to explore both phenomena, keeping a sleep journal is especially useful.

Note the time, your body position, any paralysis and the feeling of leaving. Add how lucid you were, the setting and what you remember on waking.

After a few weeks, your own patterns become much easier to see.

In the end, the “lucid dream or astral travel?” debate may have a third, interesting answer:

sometimes, we do not yet know exactly how to classify the experience.

Accepting this uncertainty is not a failure.

It is often the beginning of a smarter exploration.

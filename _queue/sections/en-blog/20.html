1Your Brain Is Not a Sculpture Finished at 25

For a long time, a particularly discouraging idea did the rounds: once you’re an adult, your brain is more or less finished, wired, fixed, wrapped up, thank you very much and good luck.

Modern neuroscience has moved well beyond that view.

The adult brain keeps an ability to adapt called neuroplasticity, meaning that its functioning, some of its connections and sometimes certain structural features can change in response to experience, learning and the environment.

Every time you learn something, repeat a movement, memorize a route, practice an instrument or train an attentional skill, you’re making use of this plasticity.

So meditation isn’t special because it has some mystical access to the nervous system.

It’s interesting because it, too, is a form of repeated brain training.

During a mindfulness meditation:

  • you deliberately direct your attention;
  • you lose it;
  • you notice the distraction;
  • you come back;
  • you observe bodily sensations;
  • you notice thoughts;
  • you sometimes hold back an immediate reaction.

In other words, you’re repeating several cognitive processes.

And what the brain practices regularly can, within certain limits, become more efficient or reorganize itself.

2“Neurons That Fire Together Wire Together”: What Hebb Really Meant

One of the most famous phrases in neuroscience is often summed up like this:

Neurons that fire together wire together.

In other words, when certain neurons or networks are repeatedly activated together, their connections can grow stronger.

The idea is associated with the Canadian psychologist Donald Hebb and his work on learning.

Beware of the modern shortcut, though:

thinking positively three times in front of the mirror does not instantly “rewire” your brain.

Neuroplasticity depends on many mechanisms:

  • repetition;
  • attention;
  • learning;
  • context;
  • sleep;
  • age;
  • stress;
  • physical activity;
  • experience.

Regular meditation can contribute to this plasticity precisely because it involves very specific repetitions.

Take focusing on the breath.

Your attention wanders off.

You notice.

You come back.

Ten seconds later:

it wanders off again.

You come back.

That’s not a failed session.

It’s probably the most interesting part of the training.

Every return is one more repetition.

3Hippocampus, Insula, Prefrontal Cortex: What Do MRI Scans Really Show?

Neuroimaging studies on meditation and the brain have drawn a lot of attention because some have reported differences in several brain regions in meditators or after mindfulness interventions.

The regions regularly studied include:

  • the hippocampus, involved in memory and various contextual processes;
  • the insula, very important for interoception, the perception of the body’s internal states;
  • the prefrontal cortex, associated with many executive functions and cognitive regulation;
  • the cingulate cortex;
  • the temporoparietal junction;
  • certain regions involved in self-referential processing.

A recent review of structural MRI studies on mindfulness interventions identified changes reported in the insula, prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, posterior cingulate cortex and temporoparietal junction, among others. But the authors stress one essential point: the number of studies is still limited, their methods vary widely, and current evidence doesn’t allow firm conclusions about whether these changes are causal or universal. (PubMed)

That’s exactly the nuance any good article on meditation and neuroplasticity should keep.

Yes, some of the data is fascinating.

No, we can’t honestly say:

“Meditate for eight weeks and your hippocampus will grow by 12%.”

The brain isn’t a muscle you measure with a tape measure after fifteen mental squats.

4What Probably Changes Before the Structure: How You Use Your Attention

Before looking for spectacular changes in gray matter, it’s probably more useful to look at functional and behavioral changes.

After several weeks of practice, some people notice:

  • that they spot a distraction more quickly;
  • that they recognize bodily tension more easily;
  • that they return to the present moment more readily;
  • that they interrupt rumination sooner;
  • that they observe their emotions more before reacting.

These changes are already a form of learning.

Let’s take an example.

Before:

anxious thought → immediate reaction → rumination.

With training:

anxious thought → noticing → bodily sensation → breath → possible choice of reaction.

The mental content hasn’t necessarily disappeared.

An extra step has simply slipped in.

And that step is precisely the kind of skill mindfulness meditation trains again and again.

5How Long Does It Take to “Change Your Brain”?

Here’s the question video titles dream about:

“21 days to reprogram your brain!”

Unfortunately, biology hasn’t read the marketing calendar.

There’s no single length of time after which the brain suddenly becomes “rewired.”

Some functional changes can appear quickly.

Other kinds of learning take weeks or months.

The structural changes seen in some meditation studies are generally associated with extended programs or substantial practice.

The recent review on brain morphology notes that the largest structural changes were reported in interventions lasting several months or in long-term meditators, while stressing the need for stronger, better-controlled studies. (PubMed)

So a good rule is to replace:

“How many days until my brain changes?”

with:

“What skill am I actually training?”

Attention?

Tolerance for discomfort?

Body awareness?

Observing thoughts?

Emotional regulation?

These goals are concrete.

And they’re far more useful than an obsession with a few MRI pixels.

6Neuroplasticity Brings Excellent News, Just Not the Kind the Internet Sells

The real good news about brain plasticity in adulthood isn’t that you can become a completely different person thanks to a miracle technique.

It’s that learning remains possible.

You can improve your ability to focus your attention.

You can change certain habits.

You can develop better emotional awareness.

You can learn new skills.

You can change certain repetitive reactions.

It usually takes time, repetition and sometimes a lot more patience than expected.

Meditation fits perfectly into this logic.

Every session is a repetition.

Every distraction you notice is a repetition.

Every return to the breath is a repetition.

Every moment when you realize:

“I’m ruminating”

before moving on to something else is a repetition.

So neuroplasticity isn’t a magic wand.

Maybe it’s something better.

It’s the biological possibility of continuing to learn.

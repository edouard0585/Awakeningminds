1Another Era, Another Name… and One Question That Is Impossible to Avoid

You are lying down with your eyes closed. A voice asks you to walk down a staircase. Your breathing slows. Then you reach a door. “Behind this door is another life.” You open it.

A street appears. Unfamiliar clothes. A stone house. Suddenly, you have the feeling that you know you are another person, living in another era, sometimes even with a name, a family, a profession, and a particularly cinematic death.

You have just experienced what is known as a past life regression.

The question immediately arises:

did you actually recover a memory from a previous existence, or did your mind just construct an extraordinarily convincing story?

The most rigorous answer today is simple: we currently have no scientific method capable of demonstrating that a scene obtained through regression hypnosis is genuinely an autobiographical memory of a life lived before your present birth.

That does not mean the experience is necessarily trivial or useless. A regression can be emotionally powerful, produce unexpected imagery, bring symbolic conflicts to the surface, and sometimes give a person a new way of thinking about their own story.

But a convincing experience is not automatically historical evidence. And that distinction is precisely what makes the subject so fascinating.

2Why Can Hypnosis Produce Such Realistic Scenes?

Hypnosis can increase absorption, imagination, and responsiveness to suggestion. When someone tells you, “Imagine a house,” you may simply create an image deliberately. But as absorption deepens, that image can gradually feel less deliberately constructed.

Details begin to appear spontaneously. A door. A smell. A person. An emotion. The experience takes on a life of its own.

The same thing happens in dreams. You do not usually tell yourself: “I shall now create a 42-year-old background character wearing a brown jacket.” He just appears.

The human brain is remarkably good at producing coherent worlds without showing us the backstage machinery that created them. Hypnotic regression makes use of this capacity.

A critical review published in 2026 on hypnotic age regression specifically emphasizes problems surrounding memory accuracy and excludes past-life regression studies from its main clinical analysis because of insufficient empirical support and methodological concerns.

In other words, even if a hypnotic technique can be useful in certain therapeutic contexts, that does not validate the idea that hypnosis functions as an autobiographical time machine.

3The Central Problem: Our Memory Is Not a Camera

We often talk about memory as though it were a library. A memory is stored somewhere. All we need to do is find the correct shelf.

Memory research shows something considerably more complicated. When we remember, we reconstruct. We combine:

  • real fragments;
  • general knowledge;
  • expectations;
  • emotions;
  • information learned later;
  • associations.

That is precisely why two people who experienced the same event can describe it very differently several years later.

Under hypnosis, this problem becomes particularly important because suggestibility can increase. An apparently innocent question such as “What color is the house you lived in?” already assumes that a house exists. The mind can then produce an answer. That answer becomes the starting point for the next question. Within a few minutes, an entire architecture can emerge.

This is why responsible professionals are extremely cautious whenever hypnosis involves the supposed recovery of memories. A memory experienced with 100% certainty can still be inaccurate.

The subjective strength of a memory does not guarantee its historical truth.

4Cryptomnesia: When a Forgotten Memory Returns Disguised as a Revelation

Here is a particularly fascinating — and sometimes confusing — phenomenon: cryptomnesia.

You read something ten years ago. You forget that you read it. Then the information returns. But you have also forgotten its source. So the content feels new.

Now imagine a regression. You visualize an old town. Eighteenth-century clothing. A shop sign. A first name. Later, you discover that some of those details are historically plausible.

Strange? Yes. Proof of a past life? Not necessarily.

You may previously have encountered those details in:

  • a movie;
  • a museum;
  • a novel;
  • a photograph;
  • a documentary;
  • a forgotten conversation.

Our memory absorbs an enormous amount of information whose origin we later lose. That obviously cannot explain every imaginable testimony. But before invoking a transpersonal memory traveling across several centuries, it is reasonable to check what the present-day brain may already have learned.

5Why Do Some Traditions Take Past Lives Seriously?

The idea of rebirth or reincarnation is extremely ancient. It appears in different forms across several Indian traditions.

In Hinduism, the cycle of rebirth is generally understood through concepts such as samsara and karma.

In Buddhism, rebirth also occupies an important place, but it is not simply the transfer of an immutable personal soul from one body to another: the Buddhist doctrine of non-self makes the mechanism considerably more subtle.

Spiritual traditions therefore did not wait for modern hypnosis before imagining continuity across several existences. However, contemporary past-life regression is a relatively modern technique combining hypnosis, spirituality, personal development, and sometimes psychotherapy.

We should avoid presenting it as an ancient method that has remained unchanged for thousands of years. A Buddhist monk meditating on rebirth and a Western hypnosis session in which someone is guided toward “ancient Egypt” are not automatically the same practice.

6Can a Regression Be Useful Even if the Scene Is Not Historically True?

Yes. And this is probably the most interesting question of all.

Imagine that during a session someone visualizes a fictional existence in which they spend their entire life trying to please an extremely authoritarian father. Afterward, they realize: “That is exactly what I am doing today with my boss.”

Does the past life absolutely need to have existed? Not necessarily.

The scene can function as:

  • a metaphor;
  • a guided dream;
  • a symbolic narrative;
  • a projection;
  • a dramatization of a current conflict.

Psychology has long used imagery, metaphors, role-playing, and narratives to explore emotions. A story can be psychologically true without being historically true.

The problem only begins when a practitioner says: “What you just saw really happened.” At that point, symbolic exploration becomes a factual claim that the method itself cannot establish. The ethical difference is enormous.

7How Can You Approach Regression Carefully?

If you want to explore this type of practice, keep a few rules in mind.

First:

treat images as experiences, not evidence.

Second: avoid leading questions. Instead of “Who murdered you?” ask “What do you notice?”

Third: do not make any major decision based exclusively on a regression. Not: “I recognized my partner as my fifteenth-century executioner, so I’m filing for divorce tomorrow morning.”

Fourth: if the session touches on a present-day trauma, choose someone who is genuinely trained in mental health rather than someone who immediately turns every emotion into ancestral karma.

Finally: after the session, write down what you clearly perceived, what was suggested to you, and what you interpreted afterward. This helps protect the boundary between the experience itself and the story reconstructed around it.

8What About Children Who Say They Remember Another Life?

There is a specific body of literature concerning children who spontaneously report memories interpreted as belonging to a previous life, particularly the research initiated by psychiatrist Ian Stevenson and later continued at the University of Virginia.

These cases are very different from hypnotic regressions because the memories are described as spontaneous rather than induced through hypnotic suggestion.

They have fascinated researchers and the public for decades, and some cases contain a remarkable amount of detail. They nevertheless remain controversial.

Methodological difficulties include:

  • unintentional transmission of information;
  • case selection;
  • cultural interpretation;
  • witness memory;
  • difficulty controlling every possible source of information.

It is therefore possible to acknowledge that certain cases are intriguing without concluding that reincarnation has been scientifically demonstrated.

Uncertainty is not a weakness. Sometimes it is the most intellectually solid position available.

9Regression: Believe It, Reject It… or Explore Without Deciding

There is a third position between “Every vision I have is an exact memory of one of my past lives” and “Every spiritual experience of this kind is ridiculous.”

You can say:

“I don’t know what this scene objectively represents, but I can observe what it produces psychologically.”

That position allows you to preserve:

  • curiosity;
  • wonder;
  • symbolic value;
  • and critical thinking.

You may leave a regression with a powerful image. A feeling. A question. An intuition about your current life.

But it is probably best to leave the official records of your supposed 1732 incarnation alone until further evidence arrives.

1A talking circle isn’t just a meeting hack invented to stop interruptions

The principle seems almost childlike: several people sit in a circle, an object is passed around and only the person holding it speaks. Yet behind this simplicity lie North American Indigenous traditions whose uses can be political, communal, educational, ceremonial or tied to conflict resolution.

First Nations sources and Canadian institutions stress one essential point: there is no single, universal Indigenous talking stick protocol.

Practices differ from nation to nation.

Queen’s University’s guide to Indigenous protocols points out that some peoples use a stick while others use an eagle feather, a wampum belt, a pipe or a sacred shell; the rules and meanings must therefore be understood within the tradition in question. Trent University also notes that the direction in which the circle moves may depend on the territory and the local Indigenous protocol.

In some Mi’kmaq descriptions, the object symbolically gives the speaker the space they need to talk without interruption, while the others listen and try to understand rather than immediately preparing a reply.

This origin deserves to be acknowledged when a contemporary version is used in a school, a company or a meditation group.

The principle can be adapted.

But it’s far more respectful to say where the inspiration comes from than to present the object as an ancient universal technique miraculously rediscovered by a management consultant.

2Why the circle radically changes the way a group listens

An ordinary conversation often favors people who are quick, self-assured or who already enjoy high status.

Someone speaks.

Someone else jumps in before they’ve finished.

A third person is busy mentally preparing their reply.

After ten minutes, everyone may have talked a lot, but nobody is sure they’ve really heard the others.

The talking circle imposes a very strong behavioral constraint: you can’t respond right away.

The Talking Circle described by Trent University specifically asks participants to practice active listening without automatically commenting on what was said before. The aim is to take in what you’ve heard and come back to your own ideas and feelings when your turn comes.

This absence of immediate reaction changes the atmosphere.

Silence is no longer a void that has to be filled.

It becomes part of the process.

The circular shape also plays a symbolic role: no seat is automatically “the head of the table.” One Mi’kmaq source describes the circle as a structure in which nobody holds a position of prominence, encouraging expression and equal contributions.

Of course, this doesn’t erase real power dynamics. A director sitting in a circle is still the director when they stand up. But the protocol can temporarily dampen certain conversational habits.

That’s probably why this structure has been adapted in education, healthcare, restorative justice and community groups.

3How to run a contemporary talking circle respectfully, without staging a fake Indigenous ritual

If you aren’t passing on a specific Indigenous tradition, the best approach is to say clearly that you’re using a dialogue practice inspired by talking circles, and then to keep the object simple.

An ordinary stone, a personal piece of wood or any other neutral object works.

There’s no need to buy an imitation “Native American” stick covered in supposedly sacred patterns.

Sit in a circle.

Set the topic.

Explain a few rules: one person speaks at a time; nobody interrupts; it’s fine to pass; confidentiality when the context calls for it; keep to a reasonable length.

Some Indigenous descriptions also emphasize that everyone may choose not to speak. Cree educational material presented in a recent publication explicitly mentions the option of passing the stick without speaking and recommends that everyone be given the chance to express themselves.

The facilitator mustn’t turn the circle into an emotional interrogation.

Nobody is obliged to share a trauma.

A very simple question can be enough: “What’s taking up most of your attention today?”

When the object returns to where it started, you can decide on a second round or close with a minute of silence.

The magic of the process comes less from the stick itself than from the group’s shared discipline of not stealing the floor.

4Talking circle, therapeutic circle and healing: words not to confuse

The expressions healing circle and talking circle sometimes appear together, particularly in certain Indigenous and healthcare settings. An article in The Permanente Journal describes the introduction of healing circles and talking circles in primary care and outlines some of the structures used in Lakota traditions.

That doesn’t mean an improvised circle among friends automatically becomes group psychotherapy.

This distinction matters enormously when traumatic topics come up.

An untrained facilitator can perfectly well host a conversation about gratitude, creativity or everyday relationships.

They should be much more cautious about inviting people to talk about violence, abuse, suicidal thoughts or severely traumatic events.

The rule “what’s said in the circle stays in the circle” can foster trust, but it replaces neither professional ethics nor the procedures required when someone is in immediate danger.

Likewise, the absence of interruption doesn’t mean every statement becomes true or that no limits can be set.

A circle has to stay safe.

Threatening or discriminatory remarks, or ones that directly encourage dangerous behavior, don’t become sacred because the person saying them is holding a piece of wood.

Listening without interrupting must be distinguished from agreeing.

You can hear someone deeply without adopting their interpretation.

5What the stick really teaches: listening all the way through before building your reply

The great value of the circle can be summed up by a very simple personal experiment.

During an ordinary conversation, try listening to someone without preparing your reply while they’re talking.

It’s surprisingly hard.

Our brain anticipates, sorts, compares and is already building a comeback.

The talking stick temporarily externalizes a rule we’re not very good at: it’s not my turn yet.

This constraint also gives more room to people whose conversational style is slower.

After a few circles, you can bring this skill into everyday life: leave two seconds after someone finishes a sentence; check that the person is done; rephrase before you respond; tolerate silence.

That may be the best way to honor a practice that Indigenous peoples have generously shared in certain contemporary contexts: not by appropriating its aesthetics, but by genuinely learning what it demands.

Acknowledging its origins matters too. Protocols differ between nations, and when you take part in a genuine Indigenous circle, you should follow local guidance rather than impose a generic version learned from a book.

In the end, the stick is nothing like a magic microphone.

Its power comes precisely from everything the others agree not to do while one person holds it: interrupt, correct, hurry or redirect the conversation toward themselves.

In an age when everyone has a way to publish their opinion instantly, learning to go a few minutes without giving one has a strange air of advanced spiritual practice.

1Saying “Trance” Actually Hides Extremely Different Experiences

Using the word trance in the singular gives the impression that there is one universal state that all humans enter in the same way, with the same sensations and the same mechanisms. Anthropology tells a much richer story. Depending on the culture and the practice, a person may experience themselves as traveling to another world, receiving an entity, temporarily losing their ordinary identity, receiving a message, dancing until they become absorbed or entering a silent contemplation accompanied by a feeling of unity.

These experiences should not be considered interchangeable. In his major work Music and Trance, Gilbert Rouget built a typology that distinguishes in particular between trance and ecstasy, shamanism and possession, as well as various collective and emotional forms of trance. His work is valuable because it shows that music does not act like a universal acoustic drug. The same drum does not automatically produce the same state in every human being: learning, ritual context, expectations, symbols and social relationships play a fundamental role.

To understand the types of trance, then, you have to accept two levels of reading. The first is cultural: what does the experience mean to the people who live it? The second is psychophysiological: what attentional, emotional, motor and bodily processes accompany the state? Reducing the first to the second would lose the richness of the phenomenon; turning the first into scientific proof of an invisible world would be the opposite mistake.

2Journeying Trance: Going Elsewhere While Staying Here

In some practices described as shamanic, trance is described as a journey. The practitioner remains physically present but reports that their consciousness, soul or subjective experience travels to another place or another level of the world. Descriptions may include landscapes, animals, ancestors, guides or symbolic encounters.

The word “journey” should be understood here first of all in its cultural and phenomenological context. Science can study the account, the mental imagery, bodily perception or the associated brain activity, but it has not established that a conscious entity objectively leaves the body and visits an invisible space.

In the Western imagination, this form of trance is often associated with repetitive drumming. Yet Rouget warned against mechanical explanations: music and trance are linked in many different ways depending on cultural structures, and he rejected the reductionism that turns rhythm into an automatic neurophysiological trigger. A person initiated into a particular cosmology isn’t just listening to a series of beats; they recognize a framework, an intention, codes and sometimes years of training.

For a contemporary practice inspired by journeying, it is therefore more honest to speak of guided imagery or an inner journey when you are not genuinely part of a given tradition. You can symbolically explore a forest, a cave or an imaginary landscape without claiming to reproduce a complex cultural ceremony.

3Possession Trance: When Ordinary Identity Takes a Back Seat

Possession works according to a very different logic. Here, the person doesn’t necessarily picture themselves traveling elsewhere; in some traditions, it is instead a deity, a spirit, an ancestor or another presence that is understood to be expressing itself through them.

Behavior can change: voice, posture, gestures, dance, emotional expression or the way of interacting with others. Depending on the culture, this state may be sought, valued, ritualized and interpreted as a legitimate social or religious function. It would therefore be deeply misleading to automatically regard all ritual possession as a psychiatric disorder.

Rouget describes in particular how, in some possession contexts, music helps the practitioner identify with the deity and allows that identification to be expressed through dance. Music is then not simply sound stimulation: it can signal a particular entity, recall a ritual repertoire and organize the group’s participation.

Cultural psychiatry calls for essential caution here: a behavior is not assessed independently of its context, of whether it is voluntary or involuntary, of the distress it causes and of its impact on functioning. A ritualized experience that is expected and socially integrated is not automatically equivalent to a pathological episode. Conversely, a person suffering from involuntary states, memory loss, significant fear or impaired day-to-day functioning deserves a professional assessment rather than an exclusively spiritual explanation.

4Ecstasy: Stillness, Absorption and a Sense of Going Beyond

In everyday language, ecstasy suggests overflowing joy. Historically and anthropologically, the concept can cover much more complex experiences: contemplative absorption, a feeling of unity, a temporary loss of the usual distinction between self and world, a sense of sacred presence or a transformation in the perception of time.

Rouget contrasts certain features of ecstasy with those of trance: the former may be more associated with stillness, silence and solitude, while the latter may involve movement, noise, stimulation and interaction. However, this typology should not be turned into an absolute biological rule. Human practices readily spill over the boxes we build to understand them.

A deep contemplative meditation, for example, can produce an experience of intense absorption without any dancing or drumming. A collective ritual, on the other hand, can combine movement, emotion and a feeling of unity. The notion of mystical experience is also studied in psychology, independently of the question of whether its metaphysical content is objectively true.

So the most useful distinction is descriptive: what does the person feel? Is their ordinary identity reinforced, diminished or replaced? Are they still or active? Is the experience voluntary? What role does the group play? What does it mean culturally? These questions shed more light than the label “trance.”

5Why No Classification Should Erase Culture, Safety and Personal Experience

Journeying, possession and ecstasy are useful landmarks, but reality forms a much more complicated continuum. Music, dance, repetition, prayer, breathing, fasting, sleeplessness, meditation and a collective setting can alter conscious experience in different ways. Dunja Njaradi’s article on the dialogue between anthropology, ethnomusicology, dance and neuroscience points out precisely that the study of trance spans several disciplines and that the relationship between movement, music, kinesthesia and affect cannot be reduced to a single explanation.

For today’s practitioner, this diversity calls for humility. Using a shamanism-inspired visualization doesn’t entitle you to call yourself a shaman. Dancing until you feel strong absorption doesn’t mean you are reproducing a possession ceremony. Nor does feeling a sense of unity during meditation oblige you to conclude that you have just proven a cosmology.

It’s also important to keep one simple criterion: the ability to come back, to function and to stay safe. A voluntary experience that is limited in time and integrated into your life is not the same as involuntary episodes accompanied by confusion, distress or lasting impairment of functioning. When the latter appear, a medical or psychological consultation is appropriate.

The richness of human trances lies precisely in their plurality. What they teach us is less that there is an identical secret door in every brain than something more fascinating: culture, body, music, belief, attention and social relationships can intertwine to profoundly transform the way a person experiences themselves.

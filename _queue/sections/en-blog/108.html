1What Is Flow State in Psychology?

It’s a state of deep absorption in an activity, so complete that you stop watching yourself and lose track of time. Flow state examples are easy to find. A musician plays and stops consciously thinking about their fingers. A climber sees nothing but the next hold. A writer looks up and is stunned to discover that three hours have just vanished. All of them may describe a similar feeling: the action seems to unfold almost by itself.

The psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi popularized the term flow to describe this experience of deep engagement in an activity, characterized in particular by intense concentration, reduced self-consciousness, an altered perception of time and a sense of seamlessness between action and awareness.

The resemblance to certain forms of trance is obvious. In both cases, ordinary mental functioning seems temporarily reorganized.

But the concepts are not interchangeable.

Flow is generally studied in the context of a goal-directed activity: playing, creating, working, doing a sport. Trance, by contrast, can be sought for its own sake, in meditation, a ritual or an inner journey, and can involve much more pronounced imagery or changes in identity.

So the right question isn’t “Is flow a trance?” but rather: what processes might these experiences share?

Absorption, selective attention, a quieter inner commentary and a subjective transformation of time are four particularly interesting candidates.

2Why Does Flow Feel So Effortless?

Probably because self-monitoring quiets down while your brain keeps working hard. Flow has a paradoxical quality: a person can accomplish something technically difficult while feeling less conscious mental effort.

That doesn’t mean their brain “switches off.” On the contrary, the systems needed for the activity have to work with remarkable precision.

A systematic review published in Cortex in 2022 examined 25 studies and 471 participants using EEG, fMRI, fNIRS or brain stimulation. The research pointed to the involvement of regions linked to attention, executive functions and reward systems. But the authors also stressed that the results were heterogeneous and still insufficient to establish a definitive neural model of flow.

This matters, because a popular theory called transient hypofrontality has sometimes been summed up online as: “during flow, the prefrontal cortex shuts down.”

Reality is much more subtle.

Contemporary work suggests instead a dynamic reorganization of different networks. A 2026 review of nine imaging studies points in particular to a reduction in certain self-referential processes associated with the default mode network, alongside the engagement of attentional control networks.

In short: less “how do I look right now?”, but certainly not less brain.

3What’s the Difference Between Flow and Trance?

Both rest on absorption, but flow usually needs a task matched to your skill, while trance can happen when you’re completely still. In a rhythmic, meditative or cognitive trance, something comparable to flow can happen: attention gradually stops jumping between dozens of objects.

The practitioner is no longer checking their phone, their social image, the time, their grocery list and the meaning of life every eleven seconds. One channel becomes dominant.

This absorption reduces the competition between certain mental contents. A repetitive action (dancing, drumming, reciting, breathing or visualizing) can then seem to become automatic.

The difference is that flow usually requires ongoing interaction between skill and task. An activity that’s too easy breeds boredom; one that’s too hard risks producing anxiety and breaking engagement. Classic models of flow therefore emphasize a balance between challenge and skill, along with fairly clear goals and feedback that lets you adjust what you’re doing.

A contemplative trance doesn’t necessarily have these features. You can stay completely still without “succeeding” at any task.

So their kinship lies more in the phenomenology of absorption than in a shared mechanism.

4How Do You Get Into a Flow State?

Mostly by setting up the conditions that let it emerge, and mindfulness may help. That’s surprising, because mindfulness and flow seem both close and opposed. Mindfulness encourages conscious observation of present experience; flow can give the impression that the observer has almost disappeared.

Yet a 2025 meta-analysis of randomized trials on mindfulness-based interventions and flow concludes that these interventions can promote certain aspects of flow, while noting the limitations and heterogeneity of the literature.

One possible explanation is that attention training makes it easier to return to the task when distractions arise.

So if you’re wondering how to enter flow state, work on the conditions that let it emerge. Simply ordering “FLOW, NOW!” rarely works.

Choose a task demanding enough to engage your abilities without completely exceeding them. Set a clear goal. Cut down on interruptions. Give yourself a long enough stretch of time to really get into the activity.

Then stop checking every two minutes whether you’re in flow.

Obsessively monitoring the optimal state is probably an excellent way to break it.

5What Does Being “in the Zone” Really Mean?

It’s a wonderful way to describe flow, but not a scientific mechanism. Athletes and artists sometimes talk about being in a state of grace, or in the zone. The movement feels right even before it is thought through. The pianist seems to follow the music rather than produce it. The athlete anticipates the action perfectly.

In a spiritual reading, these moments can be experienced as a connection with something larger. That interpretation belongs to personal experience and can be deeply meaningful.

Scientifically, we can study attention, automaticity, reward, performance, the perception of time and self-awareness. We cannot conclude from these measurements that a “universal energy” has temporarily taken the controls.

Flow is fascinating precisely because it shows that a subjectively extraordinary state can emerge from the interplay of skill, challenge, attention and motivation.

Trance adds another possibility to this family of experiences: deliberately using certain conditions to change the usual organization of consciousness.

Ultimately, both remind us that our ordinary way of being conscious is not the only one available. Our attention has configurations. Some are scattered, others extremely focused; some are saturated with inner commentary, others surprisingly silent.

And when the right conditions come together, human beings can experience something that looks like magic without science having to say the magic word.

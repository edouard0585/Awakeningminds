1NSDR: A Modern Term for a Family of Practices That Already Existed Under Other Names

The acronym NSDR, for Non-Sleep Deep Rest, has become hugely popular in recent years, especially in content about performance, sleep and recovery, to the point where you might sometimes think a new neurological technology had just been discovered.

In reality, NSDR isn’t a single, fully standardized clinical technique.

The term is more of a modern category covering various practices in which a person stays broadly awake while entering a state of deep relaxation and reduced attention to their surroundings.

This can include exercises similar to:

  • yoga nidra;
  • body scan;
  • guided relaxation;
  • certain forms of hypnosis;
  • lying-down meditations.

The word is handy.

But we shouldn’t turn it into a new ancient tradition.

Yoga nidra, for example, has its own historical context and various structured modern forms, notably those popularized in the 20th century by Swami Satyananda Saraswati.

NSDR is more of a contemporary label.

So the important question isn’t:

“Is this really NSDR?”

but:

“What happens when I spend twenty minutes lying still, eyes closed, with less stimulation and gradually loosening attention?”

And above all:

“Can it replace sleep?”

Short answer:

no.

But it can still be very interesting.

2Resting the System Doesn’t Mean Reproducing a Night’s Sleep

During sleep, the brain goes through different stages, each with its own physiological characteristics:

  • light NREM;
  • deep slow-wave sleep;
  • REM.

These phases contribute to very diverse functions:

  • metabolic regulation;
  • memory;
  • plasticity;
  • immunity;
  • emotions;
  • physical recovery.

A non-sleep deep rest session doesn’t simply reproduce these processes because you’re very relaxed.

You may feel:

  • heaviness;
  • a loss of your sense of time;
  • a quieter inner dialogue;
  • hypnagogic images;
  • a sense of having slept.

But if you stayed awake, you haven’t necessarily gone through the physiological architecture of a normal sleep cycle.

That’s where you need to be especially careful with slogans like:

“20 minutes of NSDR replaces two hours of sleep.”

Or:

“30 minutes of yoga nidra equals four hours of sleep.”

These equivalences are extremely popular.

They aren’t backed by robust data that would let you convert one minute of practice directly into a precise number of hours of biological sleep.

Deep rest can be useful.

But sleep remains irreplaceable.

If you sleep five hours every night and use NSDR to “make up for it,” your body probably won’t sign that contract.

3So Why Can a Session Feel So Spectacularly Restorative?

Picture a very packed workday.

You’ve spent four hours switching between:

  • emails;
  • meetings;
  • notifications;
  • documents;
  • messages;
  • decisions.

Your brain isn’t necessarily worn out only by the cognitive effort itself.

It’s also saturated with shifts of attention.

You lie down for twenty minutes.

Phone off.

Eyes closed.

A voice guides you through a body scan.

For twenty minutes, almost no new decisions are required.

No messages.

No tasks.

No choices.

This massive reduction in stimulation can feel extremely restorative.

Breathing sometimes slows down naturally.

Muscle tension eases.

The attention system stops jumping from one object to another.

You may not be sleeping.

But you’ve stopped constantly consuming cognitive resources.

That’s exactly why an NSDR session can sometimes feel more restful than twenty minutes spent “doing nothing” on your phone.

On your phone, you’re not doing nothing.

You’re reading.

Evaluating.

Reacting.

Comparing.

Deciding.

Deep rest, on the other hand, creates a genuine sensory and cognitive pause.

That’s probably one of its main practical benefits.

4NSDR, Memory and Learning: What We Actually Know

Part of NSDR’s popularity comes from the idea that it might improve memory or speed up learning.

Research on various resting states, yoga nidra, sleep and periods of wakeful rest does suggest that the brain keeps processing and consolidating some information when we stop a task, and the concept of wakeful rest has been studied in cognitive science for several years.

Studies show in particular that quiet periods after learning can sometimes improve retention compared with immediately starting a competing cognitive activity.

But we need to distinguish between:

  • wakeful rest;
  • meditation;
  • yoga nidra;
  • NSDR;
  • napping;
  • sleep.

They’re not interchangeable.

If you learn twenty words and then close your eyes for ten minutes in a quiet room, the potential benefit may simply come from not immediately overloading your memory with forty new pieces of information.

That doesn’t necessarily mean you’ve entered some mysterious brain state that automatically optimizes neuroplasticity.

The practical rule is still an excellent one:

after a period of intense learning, a stimulation-free rest break may be more worthwhile than diving straight into social media.

Your brain has just received some information.

Sometimes, give it a few minutes before throwing a whole other suitcase at it.

5The 15-Minute NSDR Protocol: The Less You Try to Reach a Special State, the Better It Works

Lie down or settle into a comfortable position.

Close your eyes if that suits you.

For the first two minutes, simply feel the points of contact:

  • heels;
  • pelvis;
  • back;
  • arms;
  • head.

Then slowly move your attention to different parts of the body without trying to change them.

Feet.

Calves.

Thighs.

Belly.

Chest.

Hands.

Face.

Then let your breathing unfold naturally.

You can notice the out-breath.

Then the sounds.

Then come back to the body.

If images appear, let them pass.

If you start to doze off, that’s not a failure.

If you stay perfectly awake, that’s not a failure either.

This absence of performance is essential.

NSDR isn’t a contest where you have to reach “87% deep relaxation.”

You’re simply creating a period of minimal stimulation.

At the end, gradually open your eyes, move your fingers and get back to what you were doing.

Use this session:

  • after lunch;
  • after a period of intense work;
  • before a second learning session;
  • in the late afternoon if you need a break without necessarily sleeping.

If it regularly puts you to sleep for forty-five minutes and disrupts your bedtime, obviously adjust the timing.

Rest should serve your sleep.

Not sabotage it.

6NSDR or Nap: Choose Based on What Your Body Really Needs

You’re tired.

Should you take a nap or do an NSDR session?

If you’re genuinely short on sleep and can take a short nap without disrupting your night, sleeping may be more directly restorative because you actually enter a state of sleep.

If you mainly need a mental break but want to avoid falling asleep, non-sleep deep rest can be an excellent option.

If you suffer from insomnia, be more careful with long naps, as they can reduce your sleep pressure in the evening.

And if you’re chronically exhausted despite spending enough time in bed, don’t automatically assume that all you’re missing is a better recovery technique.

Persistent fatigue and excessive sleepiness can be linked to many factors:

  • insufficient sleep;
  • sleep apnea;
  • medications;
  • medical conditions;
  • depression;
  • stress;
  • irregular schedules.

NSDR can be a tool.

Not a diagnosis.

And certainly not a permanent substitute for sleep.

In the end, the best way to use it is much less spectacular than the promises that sometimes circulate: fifteen or twenty minutes during which you deliberately stop making demands on your brain, let your body relax, then return to your day feeling a little less saturated.

No need to pretend you’ve just slept four hours in fifteen minutes.

A real break is already rare enough to be precious.

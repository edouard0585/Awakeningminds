1Ancient “Celtic shamanism” is a modern hypothesis, not an established historical fact

The phrase Celtic shamanism has tremendous evocative power. It conjures up druids traveling in spirit to the beat of a drum, bards receiving the awen beneath ancient oaks, and initiates crossing through the mists into the Otherworld.

The problem is that we have no ancient source describing an institution the Celts themselves would have called “shamanism.”

The word shaman actually comes from Siberia. Applying it to Celtic peoples is therefore a modern comparison.

The sources on ancient Celtic religions are fragmentary. For the Gauls, we largely depend on Greek and Roman authors, who looked at these societies from the outside. For Ireland and Wales, much of the mythological material was handed down in medieval manuscripts written in societies that were already Christianized.

That doesn’t make these texts useless. They preserve an enormous amount of ancient cultural material.

But it becomes difficult to claim: “Here is exactly how a druid of the first century BCE entered a trance.”

Contemporary “Celtic shamanism” movements have therefore generally built their practices by bringing together motifs from Celtic mythologies and shamanic models identified elsewhere.

Even a source from within contemporary Druidry, the Order of Bards, Ovates & Druids, explicitly acknowledges that the existence of an ancient Celtic shamanic system equivalent to Siberian or Indigenous traditions remains controversial and hard to establish.

This distinction doesn’t diminish the value of a modern practice. It simply avoids presenting a recent reconstruction as a tradition documented over two thousand years.

2The druids: priests, keepers of knowledge… but were they shamans?

Ancient authors describe the druids as a prestigious class associated with religion, teaching, justice, divination and ritual knowledge. Caesar, in The Gallic War, notably states that they studied for many years and passed on much of their teaching orally.

Other authors mention seers and poets, sometimes distinguishing between several categories.

So ancient Celtic societies did have religious and intellectual specialists.

However, that isn’t enough to call them shamans.

To use the term in a reasonably strict anthropological sense, we would generally look for evidence of a specialized practice involving, for example, voluntary trance with soul flight, a structured relationship with helping spirits and a corresponding role in the community.

The ancient sources provide no clear, systematic description of this kind for the druids.

The parallels come mostly from mythological and medieval motifs: animal transformation, divination, ecstatic inspiration, journeys to the Otherworld and encounters with supernatural beings.

They are fascinating.

But a narrative motif is not automatically a technical account of a historical ritual.

A story about a hero crossing over to a marvelous island may preserve profound religious ideas without being a manual for shamanic journeying.

Here, caution becomes our best magic cauldron.

3Awen and the awenyddion: when inspiration genuinely resembles trance

One of the most interesting pieces of evidence comes from medieval Wales.

The Welsh word awen is generally associated with poetic inspiration, creative breath or spiritual inspiration. It became central to modern Neo-Druidry, where it can be understood as a force of inspiration connecting the bard to the sacred world.

A particularly intriguing medieval source is Gerald of Wales, or Giraldus Cambrensis, who in the twelfth century describes people called awenyddion. According to his account, when asked to reveal information, they appear to enter an unusual state, utter words that are hard to understand and seem to lose their ordinary state of consciousness for a time.

These descriptions have sometimes been compared to trance or possession. One overview of Welsh traditions reproduces the very passage in which Gerald describes these people entering a deeply altered state when consulted.

So here we have interesting historical evidence of a phenomenon resembling divinatory trance in medieval Wales.

But it still doesn’t prove the existence of a full-fledged “Celtic shamanism.”

First, because Gerald was writing in the twelfth century, long after pre-Christian Celtic antiquity.

Second, because his perspective is Christian, and he interprets behavior through the demonological categories of his time.

Finally, because prophetic trance and shamanism are not synonyms.

The awen is therefore an excellent gateway into the inspired experiences of Celtic cultures, as long as we don’t hand it a Siberian passport it never applied for.

4The Otherworld: Annwn, marvelous islands and voyages that resemble shamanic tales

Irish and Welsh literature is full of passages into an Otherworld.

In the Welsh tradition, Annwn refers to a supernatural realm that appears in several texts. In Irish tales, heroes reach marvelous islands or realms associated with the sídhe. The immrama, or voyage tales, depict journeys to extraordinary islands, sometimes inhabited by supernatural creatures or figures.

The Voyage of Máel Dúin is a famous example.

These stories have striking parallels with motifs described as shamanic elsewhere: crossing a boundary between worlds, meeting entities, gaining knowledge or undergoing transformation, then returning to the human world.

It is precisely on these similarities that several modern authors built the idea of a Celtic shamanism.

The methodological problem is simple: resemblance is not historical identity.

A Greek epic, a medieval Christian vision and a Siberian tale can all feature a descent into the afterlife without belonging to a single tradition.

The Order of Bards, Ovates & Druids’ article on the subject itself acknowledges that Otherworld journey motifs are plentiful, but that the difficulty lies in demonstrating that they once belonged to an institutional system that could rigorously be called shamanic.

The term can therefore serve as an interpretive comparison, not an archaeological certainty.

5How to practice today without inventing fake ancestors

It is entirely possible today to build a spiritual practice inspired by Celtic mythologies: meditating on a story, working symbolically with the awen, walking through a landscape, studying the old texts, using poetry and music, or practicing an Otherworld visualization.

The problem begins when a modern reconstruction is presented as “the exact method of the ancient druids.”

We simply don’t have that information.

An honest contemporary practice can say: “I draw inspiration from medieval Celtic sources and modern contemplative techniques.”

That sentence may sound less mysterious than “a 3,000-year-old secret druidic initiation.” But it has one considerable advantage: it’s probably true.

It also makes it possible to respect the real, living Celtic cultures — Welsh, Irish, Scottish Gaelic, Breton, Cornish, Manx — instead of reducing “the Celts” to an aesthetic of forests and spirals.

Studying the languages, texts and history often brings far more depth than artificially adding a drum to every legend.

Contemporary Celtic shamanism can therefore be a legitimate modern spiritual path, like many forms of Neo-Druidry. It only becomes historically problematic when it blurs the difference between inspiration and demonstrated transmission.

Yet the ancient Celtic texts don’t need to be turned into a shamanic instruction manual to be extraordinary.

The awen, the medieval prophets, the shapeshifting, Annwn and the voyages to marvelous islands are already enough to open up a spiritual universe of dizzying richness.

Sometimes, true respect for mystery means accepting that we don’t know exactly how the ancients practiced it.

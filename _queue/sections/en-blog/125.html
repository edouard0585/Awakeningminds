1What does “extraction” mean in shamanic language?

In some shamanic cosmologies, not every problem is explained by the loss of something. Certain illnesses or forms of suffering may instead be interpreted as the presence of something that shouldn’t be there: a spiritual object, an influence, an intrusion or an invisible substance.

The specialist’s role is then to locate that presence and remove it.

In Michael Harner’s Core Shamanism, this idea was systematized under the name shamanic extraction. Today, the Foundation for Shamanic Studies describes the practice as identifying and then removing what it calls “localized spiritual intrusions,” with the help of helping spirits. It teaches a method that involves no direct physical contact.

These terms need to be put back in their context.

“Spiritual intrusion” is a category within the cosmology of Core Shamanism, not a biomedical diagnosis.

No scanner, blood test or validated instrument can currently detect an invisible energetic arrow lodged in someone’s shoulder.

That doesn’t stop the ritual from carrying strong subjective meaning.

A person may experience anger, a memory, a toxic relationship or a bodily sensation as something “foreign” that they want to expel symbolically.

The language of extraction then gives that experience a vivid, image-based form.

The danger begins when the symbol gets mistaken for a medical certainty: “your stomach hurts because an entity has moved in there.”

Abdominal pain deserves the appropriate medical tests first.

The spirit world can wait for your blood work.

2How does extraction work in Core Shamanism?

Methods vary from one tradition to another. In the system developed by Harner, the practitioner generally works with helping spirits and uses a shamanic state of consciousness to “perceive” where the intrusion is located.

The FSS describes its teaching as a way to detect the presumed spiritual factors and then remove and dispose of them in a ritualized way. It nevertheless specifies that its trainings are offered for educational purposes, not as medical or psychological treatment.

That caveat is absolutely essential.

Within the spiritual framework, the practitioner may sense a mass, see an imaginary shape or receive an inner cue.

From a psychological point of view, these phenomena can be understood as imagery, intuition, expectation or symbolic construction.

There is currently no validated scientific way to establish that the perceived shape corresponds to a spiritual substance objectively present in the client’s body.

This should lead to a simple ethical rule: never present a spiritual interpretation as a certain diagnosis.

Saying “during the ritual, I had an image of heaviness here” is very different from saying “a hostile entity is living in your liver.”

The first sentence reports an experience.

The second creates a frightening reality for the person and can have considerable psychological consequences.

A responsible practitioner knows the difference.

3Why the symbol of “removing something” can be psychologically powerful

Even if you don’t take the cosmology of intrusions literally, the gesture of extraction has an obvious symbolic logic.

Humans readily think about their problems in spatial terms.

“I’ve got a weight on my shoulders.”

“This anger is eating me up.”

“I want to get this out of me.”

These expressions aren’t anatomically literal, but they shape how we experience things.

A ritual in which a burden is symbolically located, removed and then set down somewhere else can therefore produce a very concrete sense of transition.

That doesn’t prove an invisible substance exists.

It simply shows how closely thought, body and mental representation are intertwined.

Someone who symbolically sets down a stone representing an old guilt may genuinely feel relief.

But the ritual becomes far more useful when it’s followed by a question: what needs to change in my life so that this burden doesn’t come back in another form?

If the “intrusion” stood for an abusive relationship, you may need to set a boundary.

If it stood for work overload, you’ll need to change how things are organized.

If it stood for trauma, psychological support may be necessary.

Extraction without any real change risks turning into a spiritual vacuum cleaner you run over the same problem every week.

4A symbolic letting-go practice with no energetic diagnosis

It’s possible to create a very sober version of the extraction principle.

Sit down for a few minutes and identify a specific sensation or concern, without attributing any supernatural cause to it.

For example: “I’m still holding on to the tension from that argument.”

Notice where that tension seems to show up in your body: a tight throat, a clenched stomach, heavy shoulders.

Then imagine a shape that matches the sensation.

Maybe a stone, a cloud, a rope or a color.

With each natural exhale, imagine that shape becoming slightly less dense.

You can symbolically move it out of your personal space and let it dissolve somewhere suitable in your imagination.

Then come back to your real surroundings.

The goal isn’t to convince your brain that a metaphysical parasite has just been removed.

It’s about using imagery as a language of transformation.

After the session, ask yourself what you can actually do in the next twenty-four hours.

An email to send?

A boundary to set?

A conversation to prepare for?

A good night’s rest?

That last step turns the ritual into a starting point rather than a magic solution.

5Ethics and safety: don’t manufacture fear in the name of the invisible

Extraction practices carry a particular risk: they can plant disturbing explanations in vulnerable people’s minds.

Telling someone they’re surrounded by “parasitic entities,” that another person has sent an attack their way or that a spiritual object has been implanted in their body can reinforce paranoia, anxiety or delusional thinking.

Anyone with psychotic symptoms or an obsessive fear of invisible influences should therefore avoid practitioners who systematically validate those beliefs.

In that situation, a mental health professional is more appropriate than an ever-growing series of extraction rituals.

Medical promises should also be firmly refused.

The FSS’s own official website takes care to present its trainings as educational rather than as a treatment for illness. That distinction should be maintained wherever the practice is offered.

Shamanic extraction can therefore be approached in three clearly distinct ways.

As a spiritual reality, for those who embrace the cosmology.

As a symbolic ritual, for those who prefer a psychological reading.

As an anthropological subject, for those who study healing systems.

All three approaches are legitimate, as long as they don’t claim to be the same thing.

“Letting go of what no longer serves you” is, in the end, an excellent image.

Real progress begins when we can also tell the difference between what we need to let go of symbolically… and what calls instead for a doctor, a psychologist, a lawyer, some sleep or simply an honest conversation.

1“Remember that you are going to die”: hard to imagine a worse slogan for a wellness app

Memento mori literally means “remember that you are going to die”, and it has to be said that the phrase doesn’t exactly have the commercial optimism of “become the best version of yourself in seven days.”

However, the idea runs through a large part of Western philosophy, Christian art and numerous contemplative practices: remembering death not to glorify sadness, but to prevent human beings from living as if they had an unlimited number of tomorrows.

The Stoics frequently returned to the brevity of life.

Marcus Aurelius reflected on the impermanence of reputations, bodies, empires and those who once seemed indispensable to the world.

Seneca, in On the Brevity of Life, defends an idea that has become almost strangely modern: the problem is not just that life is short, but that we waste an impressive amount of it.

In Buddhism, the contemplation of death appears in particular under the term maraṇasati, “mindfulness or remembrance of death”, but in a doctrinal and meditative framework different from Stoicism; its objective is not simply to repeat a macabre phrase to oneself, but to develop a more direct perception of impermanence and of the urgency of practice.

These traditions are therefore not identical.

But they share a disturbing intuition:

we often organize our lives as if we were immortal.

We postpone a conversation for six months.

A project for five years.

A visit until “later”.

Then we fill forty minutes with videos that we didn’t even want to watch.

Memento mori does not necessarily say:

“Death is coming, panic.”

Rather, it asks:

“Since time is limited, is this really how you want to spend it?”

2Thinking about death does not automatically make you wiser — sometimes it just makes you anxious

Popular literature on Stoicism sometimes turns mortality into a magic psychological button:

think you are going to die → instantly become grateful and courageous.

Scientific psychology presents a much more complicated picture.

Terror Management Theory has notably studied what happens when mortality is made more salient, that is, when people are led to think about their own death; hundreds of experiments have explored the hypothesis that this awareness can strengthen certain psychological defenses, cultural identities or social reactions.

But the literature is now being seriously reevaluated. A large systematic review published in 2025, covering several hundred studies of mortality salience, concluded that the literature presented important issues of publication bias and robustness, which invites much more caution than standard summaries of the theory suggest. (PubMed)

A 2026 meta-analysis on psychological symptoms also concludes that mortality salience can have measurable effects in certain contexts, but highlights strong heterogeneity and concerns about the reproducibility of results. (PubMed)

Here is an essential nuance:

thinking about death does not necessarily produce gratitude, wisdom and universal love.

In some people it can produce:

  • anxiety;
  • avoidance;
  • anguish;
  • obsessions;
  • a feeling of unreality.

The practice of memento mori must therefore be proportionate.

The goal is not to spend breakfast imagining your funeral with an Excel spreadsheet of available coffins.

3The Stoics: death as a sorting tool rather than an obsession

The practical interest of the Stoic memento mori appears when the awareness of finitude serves to prioritize concerns.

Imagine two problems.

First:

you need to have an important conversation with someone you love.

Second:

a stranger responded to you in a slightly unpleasant tone on the Internet.

Your brain can devote surprisingly comparable energy to both.

Now add a question:

“If my time were more limited than I imagine, which of these two events would really deserve my attention?”

Death then acts as a filter.

Not as a threat.

This logic can also apply to decisions that we put off indefinitely.

Ask:

“If I knew I didn’t have ten years to make this decision, what would I consider important?”

Please note: the exercise does not mean quitting your job, buying a ticket to Bali and immediately adopting three llamas because “YOLO”.

Awareness of mortality does not eliminate consequences.

It simply puts time back into the equation.

An excellent memento mori does not necessarily push you to live more intensely.

It can push you to live more deliberately.

Saying no to certain things.

Saying yes to others.

Repairing a relationship.

Giving up a useless battle.

Spending time with a person rather than a screen.

This all seems banal.

Death has this rather brutal talent for making certain banalities suddenly very clear.

4Maraṇasati: looking at impermanence without making a disaster movie

The Buddhist contemplation of death must be approached with more caution than simplified formats such as the “three-minute memento mori meditation.”

In several texts and traditions, maraṇasati aims to develop a repeated awareness of mortality in order to reduce complacency and encourage practice, within the broader framework of impermanence.

The logic is not:

“I’m going to die, so it’s all useless.”

It is almost the opposite:

“Because conditions are constantly changing, what I do now has a particular importance.”

A gentle way to draw inspiration from it is to contemplate not one’s own death in detail, but the finitude of a pleasant experience.

You are having coffee with someone.

Notice:

“This moment will never happen again exactly like this.”

Not:

“this person is going to die.”

Simply:

“this is temporary.”

See what this changes.

Maybe you listen more.

You put the phone away.

You look at the face in front of you.

Impermanence sometimes increases value because it removes the illusion of infinite repetition.

A plastic flower can remain the same for a very long time.

A flower that is about to fade attracts attention precisely because its current form will not last.

Our brain theoretically knows that everything passes.

Sometimes the practice is to actually feel it.

5The paradox: accepting death more can help you stop wasting so much life fearing it

The fear of death is obviously not a psychological defect to be completely eliminated. It is quite logical that a living organism prefers to continue living.

But for some people, anxiety about death becomes overwhelming and can contribute to different forms of psychological distress; a meta-analysis published in 2024 found a significant association between death anxiety and numerous psychopathological symptoms, which supports the idea that it can play a significant transdiagnostic role. (PubMed)

In this context, a solitary practice of intense contemplation of death is not always a good idea.

If you suffer from:

  • panic attacks;
  • significant hypochondria;
  • obsessions around death;
  • traumatic bereavement;
  • suicidal thoughts;
  • or very debilitating existential anxiety,

the approach must be adapted, and therapeutic work can be much more relevant than a philosophical exercise found on the Internet.

For others, death can be introduced much more lightly.

Once a week, ask:

“If this week couldn’t be replayed, what would I want to have done?”

Not a bucket list of 400 adventures.

One thing.

Call someone.

Walk.

Create.

Say thank you.

Finish something.

Start something.

Finitude transforms abstract priority into decision.

6The five-minute memento mori ritual: long enough to put things in their place, not to ruin your Sunday

Take a sheet of paper.

Write:

1. Something I treat as urgent but probably isn’t important.

Then:

2. Something important that I put off because it’s not urgent.

Then:

3. A person to whom I want to devote more presence.

Finally:

4. If this week really mattered, what concrete action would I take?

Choose one action.

That’s all.

No need to visualize your grave.

No need to listen to cello in the dark.

The most useful memento mori may not be the one that makes you constantly think about death.

It’s the one that, for five minutes, reminds you enough of its possibility that you then stop thinking about it and go back to living.

Because the trap would be magnificent:

spending your life meditating on its brevity.

The goal is not to obsess over the ending.

The goal is to become a little harder to distract from what matters before it arrives.

1What does ho’oponopono mean?

Ho’oponopono is a Hawaiian practice of reconciliation, best known today for four short phrases: “I’m sorry,” “Please forgive me,” “Thank you” and “I love you.” Long before those phrases, it was a way for a family to sit together and set a damaged relationship right.

The ho’oponopono meaning starts with pono, a rich Hawaiian word for what is good, right and in its proper place. Doubled, ponopono means neat, in order, cared for. The prefix ho’o turns it into an action: to put things right.

The standard Hawaiian dictionary, by Mary Kawena Pukui and Samuel Elbert, lists many shades of that action. Among them: to correct, to adjust, to tidy up. It also offers a lovely image: to make ready, like canoe paddlers preparing to catch a wave.

In Hawaiian it is written hoʻoponopono. The small mark ʻ, called the ʻokina, is a brief catch in the voice, as in “uh-oh.” So you say it in six syllables: ho-o-po-no-po-no.

Ho’oponopono is not about deciding who was right. It is about making the relationship right again.

2How was ho’oponopono practiced in traditional Hawaiʻi?

Traditionally, ho’oponopono gathered a whole family around a problem that weighed on everyone: a quarrel, a grudge, a wrong nobody had named. An elder of the family usually led it. When the family could not settle things alone, they asked a respected outsider to guide them.

Pukui and Elbert describe family meetings where broken relationships were set right. People went through prayer, discussion and confession. Then came repentance, followed by amends and forgiveness, offered on both sides.

The book Nānā i ke Kumu (1972), co-written by Mary Kawena Pukui, walks through the process. The gathering opened with a prayer. Each person spoke in turn, and the group paused in silence when tempers rose. The closing gesture was kala: to untie, to release the other person and yourself. A shared meal often closed the day.

This ho’oponopono is not a thing of the past. Some Hawaiian families still practice it. In 1985, Victoria Shook documented how social workers were adapting it to support families.

3Who brought ho’oponopono to the rest of the world?

The first was Morrnah Nalamaku Simeona (1913–1992), a Hawaiian kahuna, or expert in traditional knowledge. From the 1970s, she reshaped ho’oponopono for modern life. Her method, Self I-Dentity through Hoʻoponopono, can be done alone. Instead of gathering the whole family, each person does their part inwardly, through prayers addressed to the Divine.

In 1983 she was named a Living Treasure of Hawaiʻi. The honor is given each year by a Buddhist temple, the Honpa Hongwanji Mission of Hawaiʻi. She taught her method in many countries and died in 1992, near Munich, Germany.

Then came Ihaleakalā Hew Len (1939–2022). He met Morrnah Simeona in 1982 and took her classes. He trained as an instructor, taught at her side, and carried on after her death. In 2007 he co-wrote Zero Limits with the American author Joe Vitale. The book traveled the world, and the four phrases traveled with it.

Keep the difference in mind: the Hawaiian tradition is a long, guided process shared by a group. The four phrases are a modern, much simpler form.

Saying the phrases means entering an old culture through a recent door. Respect starts with knowing where they come from, and with not mistaking the door for the house.

4What do the four ho’oponopono phrases really say?

Each phrase is a different inner gesture. Here is how the MeditaDream session Heart-Centered Ho’oponopono approaches them.

“I’m sorry”

“This phrase does not mean: I am bad.” It means “something caused pain, and I acknowledge it.” It is a recognition, not a verdict.

“Please forgive me”

Nothing is forced here. “Do not decide for the other person,” the session says. You ask, and you accept that the answer is not up to you.

“Thank you”

No need for a huge wave of gratitude. Look for “something small”: the air entering your lungs, the floor supporting you, a warm cup in your hands.

“I love you”

Sometimes this is the easiest phrase, sometimes the hardest. “You do not need to believe it perfectly.” Say it, then notice what it does to you.

The order changes from one teacher to another. Some begin with “I love you,” others with “I’m sorry.” There is no required order.

5How do you do a ho’oponopono meditation?

A simple ho’oponopono meditation takes three steps:

  • sit or lie down comfortably and let your breathing slow;
  • choose who you are speaking to, starting with yourself, which is often the gentlest;
  • repeat the four phrases silently, in rhythm with your breath.

After each phrase, do nothing. Notice your body. Your throat may tighten a little. Your chest may open, or close. Sometimes nothing happens at all, and that is fine too.

If one phrase meets resistance, don’t hammer it in. Make it more honest. The session offers two alternatives:

  • “I can learn to support myself,” if “I love you” feels impossible;
  • “I can judge myself less harshly,” if “Please forgive me” feels too big.

“A sincere thank you is worth more than a forced I love you.”

A few minutes are enough to begin. You can come back to the phrases at night, or after an argument, when something still feels stuck.

7Is there any risk in practicing ho’oponopono?

Repeating four gentle phrases is not risky in itself. The real traps lie elsewhere, and they are easy to avoid.

The first is using the phrases to silence your anger. If someone hurt you, you do not owe them a forced “I love you.” The session is clear: “Choose only a relationship you can bring to mind without becoming overwhelmed.”

The second is replacing a real conversation. Phrases said in silence tell the other person nothing. Sometimes the right next step is to talk.

The third is promises. Ho’oponopono is sometimes sold as a way to attract money or miracles. No practice can guarantee that, so be wary of any offer that does.

If an emotion becomes too heavy, stop. Talk to someone you trust, or to a professional.

As the session puts it at the very end: “Forgiveness happens in steps, one phrase at a time.”

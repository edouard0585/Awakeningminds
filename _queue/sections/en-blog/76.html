1“I Feel Bad”: The Sentence That Gives You Almost No Information

Imagine your car’s warning light simply said:

SOMETHING’S WRONG.

Very helpful.

Thanks so much.

That is exactly what we sometimes do with our emotions.

“I feel bad.”

“I’m stressed.”

“I’m upset.”

But behind “upset” there may be:

  • frustration;
  • humiliation;
  • helplessness;
  • jealousy;
  • disappointment;
  • fear;
  • a sense of injustice;
  • fatigue.

And each one potentially calls for a different response.

If you are tired, maybe you need rest.

If you are frustrated, maybe there is an obstacle to solve.

If you are hurt, a conversation may be needed.

If you are jealous, you can explore a fear or a desire.

Naming an emotion precisely doesn’t make it disappear.

It turns a vague mass into more usable information.

In psychology, this practice is often studied under the name affect labeling: putting an emotional experience into words.

A classic study by Matthew Lieberman and his colleagues showed that verbally labeling the emotions associated with negative images was linked to decreased activity in limbic regions such as the amygdala and increased activity in the right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex. (PubMed)

In other words, saying:

“I’m feeling fear”

is not quite the same as staying stuck in:

“AAAAAAAAAH.”

Vocabulary changes the way the experience is organized.

2Why “Anger” Sometimes Isn’t Enough

You come home furious.

You say:

“I’m angry.”

Fine.

But let’s ask:

what kind of anger?

Are you irritated because a train is late?

Do you feel humiliated by a comment?

Are you frustrated because a project is failing?

Are you afraid of losing something?

Do you feel looked down on?

One primary emotion can be covered up by another.

For example:

  • fear → anger;
  • shame → aggression;
  • sadness → irritation.

It isn’t a universal rule.

But looking for more precision can reveal the real need.

“I’m furious with my friend” might become:

“I’m hurt because I felt left out.”

The conversation that follows is likely to go a little differently.

Compare:

“You’re driving me crazy!”

with:

“I felt left out when I found out that everyone had been invited except me.”

The second sentence provides information.

The first mostly provides volume.

3Affect Labeling: Why Putting Feelings Into Words Can Reduce Reactivity

In the study by Lieberman and colleagues, participants viewed, among other things, emotional faces and were sometimes asked to choose the word matching the emotion shown.

This affect labeling task was associated with a reduced amygdala response to negative stimuli and greater involvement of prefrontal regions. (PubMed)

A later series of experiments also examined the subjective effect of putting an emotion into words, compared with other strategies such as distraction or cognitive reappraisal. (PubMed)

It would be far too simplistic, however, to write:

“Naming an emotion automatically switches off the amygdala and eliminates the emotion.”

The brain doesn’t have a “fear OFF” button.

The effect depends on:

  • the context;
  • the type of emotion;
  • the task;
  • the person.

But this research supports an intuition that has become important in several therapeutic approaches:

putting an experience into words can change our relationship to that experience.

That’s especially interesting because the technique is ridiculously simple.

No equipment.

No candles.

No premium subscription.

Just a more precise word.

4Emotional Granularity: Going From Eight Crayons to a Box of 120 Colors

Imagine two people.

The first has three categories:

  • good;
  • bad;
  • upset.

The second can tell apart:

  • irritation;
  • frustration;
  • impatience;
  • disappointment;
  • envy;
  • embarrassment;
  • loneliness;
  • nostalgia;
  • apprehension;
  • relief.

The second person has what emotion research calls greater emotional granularity, or emotional differentiation: the ability to distinguish more finely between different affective states.

Why would that be useful?

Because a precise emotion potentially allows for a more precise response.

“I feel bad”:

solution unknown.

“I’m worried because I don’t know what to expect tomorrow”:

possible solution → look for information or prepare for several scenarios.

“I’m disappointed because I was hoping to be recognized”:

possible solution → accept the disappointment, clarify an expectation or ask for feedback.

Vocabulary doesn’t automatically solve the problem.

It improves the definition of the problem.

And in psychology as in plumbing, fixing something gets a little easier once you know where the leak is.

5The Emotion Wheel: Useful If You Don’t Know Where to Start

Emotion wheels have become very popular because they let you move from a general term to a more specific one.

One frequently cited version draws on the work of Robert Plutchik, who proposed a model organized around several basic emotions and their combinations.

The tools used today in therapy, coaching or emotional education may, however, differ from the original scientific model.

So the wheel is best seen above all as:

an organized vocabulary.

Start in the center.

Fear.

Anger.

Sadness.

Joy.

Then get more specific.

“Fear” might become:

apprehension.

Insecurity.

Panic.

“Anger”:

annoyance.

Frustration.

Rage.

“Sadness”:

disappointment.

Loneliness.

Grief.

You’re not looking for the “officially correct” word.

You’re looking for the one that best describes your experience.

And you can choose several.

Emotions often refuse to fill out a form with only one box.

6The Two-Minute Exercise: Emotion, Intensity, Need

Once a day, stop for two minutes.

Write just three lines.

1. What am I feeling?

Not:

“fine.”

Look for two or three words.

Example:

  • frustrated;
  • worried;
  • tired.

2. Intensity from 0 to 10

Frustration: 6.

Worry: 4.

Fatigue: 8.

This step lets you notice something essential:

emotions are not binary.

You are not:

anxious / not anxious.

The intensity changes.

3. What do I probably need?

Fatigue → rest.

Frustration → solve an obstacle.

Worry → clarification or tolerating uncertainty.

Careful:

a need is not always an order addressed to someone else.

“I need security” doesn’t automatically mean:

“My partner has to send me their GPS location every ten minutes.”

The need then has to be translated into a realistic, respectful response.

7Using Emotional Labeling During Meditation

Sit for a few minutes.

Observe your breathing.

Then, when an experience becomes clear enough, use a short word:

thinking.

worry.

impatience.

sadness.

tension.

Then come back to the breath or the body.

This technique avoids two extremes.

The first extreme:

being completely swept away by the emotion.

The second:

launching into a twenty-minute analysis in the middle of your meditation.

You’re not asking yet:

“Why am I sad, since when, is it because of my relationship with my mother in 2004?”

You simply note:

sadness.

It’s like labeling a folder before putting it away.

You know what’s there.

You don’t have to open everything right away.

8Careful: Naming Doesn’t Mean Intellectualizing Everything You Feel

Some people are already excellent at analyzing their emotions.

Too excellent.

They can explain perfectly why they are sad without actually feeling a single second of sadness.

“This emotional activation most likely stems from an abandonment schema formed in childhood, later reinforced by…”

Meanwhile, the body is completely cut off from the experience.

Emotional vocabulary therefore needs to stay connected to sensations.

After naming:

“anxiety,”

ask:

“How do I know it’s anxiety?”

A tight chest?

A churning stomach?

Your jaw?

Shallow breathing?

An urge to move?

This way you connect:

  • word;
  • body;
  • context.

That’s far richer than the label alone.

The word should shed light on the experience.

Not replace it.

9The Big Benefit: A Precise Emotion Makes a Precise Action Easier

Let’s take three situations.

“I’m stressed”

Too vague.

“I’m anxious because I don’t know what’s expected of me”

Possible action:

ask for clear criteria.

“I’m overwhelmed because I have too many tasks at once”

Action:

prioritize.

“I’m intimidated because this presentation matters a lot to me”

Action:

rehearse.

Same general feeling.

Three different problems.

Three different responses.

That’s why identifying your emotions is much more than a vocabulary exercise.

It’s a problem-solving step.

The more precise your emotional diagnosis, the better your chances of avoiding the one-size-fits-all remedies:

  • eating;
  • scrolling;
  • running away;
  • ruminating.

You start responding to what is really there.

10Three Lines a Night to Become Fluent in Emotions

Every evening, write:

Today I felt…

Three emotions.

It happened when…

One sentence.

What I would have needed was…

One sentence.

Example:

“Disappointment, jealousy, motivation.

I saw that a coworker had landed the project I wanted.

What I would have needed was to acknowledge that this project really matters to me and to ask how I could put myself forward for the next one.”

Jealousy has just gone:

  • from a shameful emotion;
  • to a piece of information.

You haven’t made the emotion disappear.

You’ve translated it.

And a translated emotion is often far easier to use than one screaming in a language you refuse to understand.

1The great myth of the trance you never come back from

It’s probably one of the most persistent fears around hypnosis and trance states: “What if I couldn’t come back?”

The scenario is easy to picture. You close your eyes, go deep into the experience, then something goes wrong and you’re doomed to wander mentally down a mysterious corridor while your body waits patiently on the couch.

This scenario doesn’t match what we know about how clinical hypnosis works.

Hypnosis isn’t a switch that locks the brain into another universe. It involves a particular configuration of attention, absorption and responsiveness to suggestions. The person isn’t anesthetized in the medical sense of the term, and the state doesn’t need a “secret word” spoken by the hypnotist to end.

The fact that hypnosis is used in real medical settings is actually an excellent antidote to the myths. A systematic review published in 2026 examined 19 randomized trials involving 3,699 patients in various anesthesia settings and reports encouraging results for certain uses, notably anxiety in pediatrics and dentistry. Another, larger review published in 2025 analyzed 142 studies including more than 9,000 patients and assessed pain, anxiety and adverse events, among other outcomes.

If hypnosis regularly left patients mysteriously “stuck,” its controlled clinical use would be hard to square with these thousands of studied procedures.

2So why do some people say they had trouble “coming back”?

Because several different phenomena can be confused with the idea of being stuck.

First possibility: the person is simply very relaxed or drowsy. After a long session, they may prefer to keep their eyes closed for a few moments. That’s not an inability to come out of trance.

Second possibility: the experience was emotionally intense and the person needs time to get back to their usual state. A deep meditation, a breathwork session or a long ritual can leave a floaty feeling.

Third possibility, and a much more important one: what’s described as a “trance that won’t end” may actually be another phenomenon, such as derealization, depersonalization, dissociation, a panic attack, a sleep disorder, the effect of a substance or a neurological problem.

In that case, answering “don’t worry, you’re just still in trance” can be a very bad idea.

So the useful criterion is less the label than how long it lasts and how much it affects you. An unusual sensation for a few minutes after a session doesn’t mean the same thing as someone staying disoriented for hours or days, no longer recognizing their surroundings or having the persistent feeling that the world isn’t real.

3Safe hypnosis doesn’t mean every trance practice is risk-free

We should avoid the opposite line of reasoning. Saying you don’t get “locked” in hypnosis doesn’t mean that every technique producing altered states is harmless.

Deliberately excessive breathing can cause hypocapnia, dizziness or faintness. Sleep deprivation can profoundly alter cognition and perception. Some psychoactive substances can trigger prolonged experiences. Intensive contemplative practices can also become destabilizing for some vulnerable people.

Even for medical hypnosis, scientific reviews don’t say “no conceivable risk.” A review on hypnosis and sleep reported a low incidence of adverse effects in the available studies, while highlighting the limited methodological quality of much of the research and the need to report these events better.

So context is decisive.

A light relaxation hypnosis led by a professional isn’t the same as three hours of hyperventilation, a ceremony involving a psychoactive substance or several days of sleep deprivation.

The word trance can cover such different practices that asking “is trance dangerous?” is almost like asking “is sport dangerous?” A stroll and climbing Everest both belong to the category of movement; their risk profiles differ slightly.

4What should you do if you still feel “somewhere else” after a practice?

Start by stopping the technique that keeps the state going: repetitive music, a particular breathing pattern, visualization or movement.

Then gently bring your attention back to your surroundings. Open your eyes if they were closed. Feel your feet in contact with the floor. Look at a few objects and name them. Identify where you are, the approximate date and what you were doing before the session.

Sensory grounding approaches are commonly used in clinical practice to support this reorientation, although a 2025 critical review points out that their specific effectiveness still lacks rigorous experimental validation.

Drink a little water, eat something if appropriate, walk calmly and avoid driving until your perception feels completely normal again.

Don’t try to “fight” the state with a second, even more intense technique.

If you’ve done extreme breathing and feel dizziness or tingling, go back to comfortable, spontaneous breathing rather than adding breath holds or other manipulations.

And if the experience becomes truly distressing, the calm presence of another person can help restore your bearings: name, place, time, immediate surroundings.

5The real warning signs: when to stop talking about spirituality and ask for help

So the important line isn’t “am I still in trance?” but “is my state still compatible with my safety and my normal functioning?”

Urgent medical help is appropriate in the event of unexplained loss of consciousness, seizures, chest pain, persistent difficulty breathing, unusual neurological weakness, significant confusion or dangerous behavior.

Psychologically, seeing a professional becomes particularly important when any of the following persist: hallucinations outside the deliberate context of the practice, paranoia, delusional ideas, a manic episode, very marked derealization or depersonalization, unusual memory loss, inability to function normally or suicidal thoughts.

In these situations, calling the episode a “spiritual awakening” must never stand in the way of a medical or psychiatric assessment.

That doesn’t stop the person from also giving a spiritual meaning to what they’re going through. The two approaches can coexist. But safety must stay the priority.

In the end, the myth of the permanent trance mixes up two realities: on one side, a largely unfounded fear of staying hypnotized forever; on the other, the very real existence of prolonged psychological or neurological states that can arise for various reasons and sometimes need proper care.

So the answer to the question “can you get stuck in a trance?” is more interesting than a simple yes or no: there’s no known mechanism by which ordinary hypnosis would permanently lock someone in the hypnotic state, but persistent symptoms after a practice should never be automatically put down to a trance that refuses to end.

It’s a less spectacular nuance than the legend. But when it comes to human consciousness, nuances are generally more useful than legends.

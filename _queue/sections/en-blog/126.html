1What is a helping spirit in shamanic traditions?

The image of the lone shaman journeying purely on personal power doesn’t fit many traditions very well. The ritual specialist is often described as someone who works with auxiliary, guardian or allied spirits.

Their nature varies enormously from one society to another.

An animal.

An ancestor.

A human figure.

The spirit of a place.

A being associated with a plant.

An entity with a specific name in the local cosmology.

Among some peoples, relationships with these beings are considered fundamental to the practitioner’s ritual power.

In the ethnography of the Tlingit, for example, religious specialists are described as seeking out or working with particular helping spirits. In other Siberian cultures, the shaman’s objects, songs and costume also represent their ties to various powers.

Michael Harner took up this logic and systematized it in Core Shamanism, notably through the notions of the power animal and helping spirits. His book The Way of the Shaman devotes several chapters to power animals, the restoration of power and helping spirits; the book’s official table of contents clearly reflects that structure.

Still, we should avoid turning this comparison into a universal equivalence.

A power animal in Harner’s system isn’t automatically the same thing as an animal spirit in a specific Indigenous tradition.

The term is a particular concept of contemporary neo-shamanism.

2Meeting an ally: spiritual experience or product of the imagination?

In modern shamanic journeying, a person may use drumming, meditation or guided imagery to meet what they interpret as a helping spirit.

A shape appears.

A wolf.

A raven.

An elderly person.

A light.

Sometimes the experience has a surprising autonomy: the entity seems to act in a way the practitioner hadn’t consciously anticipated.

That unexpected quality can strengthen the feeling that it exists independently of them.

Science, however, doesn’t allow us to conclude that this subjective autonomy proves the objective existence of a supernatural being outside the brain.

Our dreams already show that the mind can create characters who surprise us, answer back and seem to have a will of their own.

That doesn’t cancel out their symbolic value.

A mature approach therefore means keeping several hypotheses open.

Maybe you see this ally as a genuine spiritual entity.

Maybe as a personification of a psychological resource.

Maybe you simply don’t need to decide.

The experience can remain useful in all three cases.

The problem arises when someone declares that every message received from a spirit must necessarily be followed as infallible information.

An inner vision doesn’t automatically earn a certificate of omniscience just because it has feathers.

3Building a relationship: regularity, observation and no rushing

A lasting spiritual relationship shouldn’t look like a cosmic hotline you only call when you want an urgent answer.

If you work with an ally figure, return to it regularly in a calm setting, without always asking for something.

Pay attention to the details.

In what environment does it appear?

Does its behavior change?

What emotions come with it?

Are the messages consistent over several months, or do they mostly resemble whatever is on your mind that day?

Keep a journal that separates experience from interpretation.

Write: “the fox sat down by the river,” and only then: “I interpreted this as an invitation to wait.”

The distinction looks tiny, but it protects you against the gradual reshaping of memories.

From the perspective of Core Shamanism, the relationship with helping spirits is considered fundamental. The FSS presents the practitioner as someone who works with compassionate entities and asks them for knowledge or assistance.

But even within a spiritual reading, a relationship requires discernment.

A trustworthy guide shouldn’t become an excuse for dodging all personal responsibility.

“My spirit ally told me to quit my job right away” should never replace a look at your finances, careful thought and a conversation with the people involved.

The invisible can inspire.

The visible still pays the rent.

4Power animal, totem animal and guide: three terms best kept apart

The internet often uses power animal, totem animal, spirit animal and helping spirit as synonyms.

Historically and culturally, this mix-up is problematic.

The term power animal is especially associated with the system popularized by Michael Harner.

The word totem, on the other hand, has a different anthropological history and refers to specific social or religious systems in various Indigenous cultures. It doesn’t simply mean “my favorite animal that represents my personality.”

Likewise, a human or ancestral guide doesn’t necessarily serve the same function as a power animal.

For a contemporary practice not tied to any specific tradition, the term ally figure or symbolic ally is sometimes more honest.

You can perfectly well work with the image of a deer representing watchfulness and gentleness without claiming it’s a “Native American totem.”

This precision improves the quality of the practice.

It forces you to discover why the animal moves you personally, rather than looking it up on some universal list online.

Your experience of a snake may be linked to fear, healing, renewal or simply a childhood memory.

Personal context matters.

And in a particular cultural tradition, the snake might mean something else entirely.

Symbols love to travel; their historical passports still deserve to be checked.

5True reciprocity: turning the spiritual relationship into real-world behavior

Many shamanic traditions don’t describe the relationship with non-human beings as simply consuming advice.

It involves obligations, respect, taboos or reciprocity.

In Core Shamanism, helping spirits are also described as essential partners in spiritual work rather than as imaginary scenery.

In a personal practice, reciprocity can be expressed without copying any particular ceremony.

If your ally appears as a real animal, learn something about the species.

Find out about its habitat.

Perhaps support an organization that protects it.

If your spiritual relationship with the wolf leaves you completely indifferent to the fact that real wolves exist, a step may be missing somewhere.

You can also mark the relationship symbolically through gratitude, drawing, meditation or regular time in nature.

The key is to avoid dependence.

An ally figure should enrich your ability to make decisions, not replace it.

If you start asking for supernatural approval for every choice — which shirt to wear, which email to send, what to eat — it may help to pause the consultations for a while and get back to your own judgment.

And if voices or presences become involuntary, threatening, constant or seriously disrupt your daily functioning, the situation is no longer simply a voluntary spiritual exercise. Medical or psychological advice may then be necessary.

A relationship with a helping spirit can therefore be lived as spirituality, as a symbolic practice or as an imaginal dialogue.

Its value doesn’t necessarily depend on our ability to settle the metaphysical question once and for all.

Perhaps the sign of a good relationship is ultimately much simpler: after practicing it for a long time, are you becoming more attentive, more responsible and more able to fully inhabit your life?

If so, the alliance is producing at least something very real.

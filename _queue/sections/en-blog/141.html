1How does astral travel work?

Through imagery techniques, most often practiced lying down. Books on astral projection, another name for astral travel, offer an impressive catalog of them. You might pull on an invisible rope, roll out of bed or float up to the ceiling. You might sway, picture a distant target, stay aware as you fall asleep or wait for vibrations to appear.

For all their variety, most of these astral projection techniques have one thing in common.

They ask the real body to stay fairly still while the mind produces a feeling of movement or travel.

This logic becomes especially interesting in light of what neuroscience knows about bodily awareness. The feeling of being located in your body depends on several senses working together. They include vision, touch, your sense of body position and your inner sense of balance. When that integration is disturbed, it can produce sensations of moving, or of leaving the body.

In the classic spiritual reading, the techniques are said to help the astral body separate from the physical body.

From a scientific point of view, they may change how you picture your body and how you sense your balance. They may also affect the transitions between waking and sleep.

These two interpretations should not be confused.

The experience can be very realistic.

The technique can indeed produce unusual sensations.

But that does not show that anything objectively leaves the body.

So it is wiser to approach these methods as techniques for inducing a subjective experience. Their metaphysical meaning is another question.

2How do you do the rope technique?

The rope technique is mostly associated with Robert Bruce and his book Astral Dynamics.

The idea is simple: lying down and relaxed, the practitioner imagines a rope hanging above them.

It is not just a matter of picturing it.

Bruce stresses what he calls a form of tactile imagery. You imagine the feel of gripping the rope and pulling yourself up with non-physical hands. Meanwhile, your real body stays still.

Its publisher presents Astral Dynamics explicitly as a guide to out-of-body experiences, covering exit methods, movement and return.

To try this method carefully, you can set aside any supernatural assumption beforehand.

Lie down comfortably.

Gradually release your jaw, shoulders, belly and legs.

Then imagine a rope hanging about a foot above your chest.

Without moving your hands, imagine the texture of the rope in your palms.

Then imagine pulling.

Your real torso stays still.

This contrast between physical stillness and imagined movement can become surprisingly intense.

Some people feel swaying, lightness or movement.

Science does not allow us to say that these sensations signal a “second body” breaking away.

They may reasonably be linked to kinesthetic imagery, the felt sense of an imagined movement. A shift in how the brain represents the body may also play a part.

If dizziness or anxiety appears, stop and come back to the real sensations of the bed.

3How do you do the roll-out technique?

By imagining a movement while the physical body stays still. You might roll onto your side or float up to the ceiling. The roll-out technique is often associated with Robert Monroe and the modern literature on out-of-body experiences.

Monroe did much to popularize the deliberate exploration of out-of-body experiences. His book Journeys Out of the Body was first published in 1971. The publisher describes it as an account of his experiences and of practical techniques for producing them.

The principle of the roll-out is intuitive.

When you are deeply relaxed, imagine your felt body gently turning onto its side. Your physical body stays perfectly still.

Don't tense your shoulders.

Don't actually try to roll.

You are only imagining a change in your body's perspective.

Another variant is to float up to the ceiling.

Imagine a light pull at your breastbone.

Then a feeling that your weight is decreasing.

Finally, imagine a few inches of space between your felt back and the mattress.

All these techniques play on the same contrast. The brain receives real information telling it “the body is still,” while inner imagery says “I am moving.”

Research on out-of-body experiences suggests that conflicting signals can feed the illusion of moving outside the body. These signals come from your sense of balance, your body position and your eyes.

This offers a plausible explanation for why rolling, swaying and floating come up so often in the literature.

4What is the “body asleep, mind awake” method?

Another family of techniques is less about imagining an exit than about staying somewhat aware while falling asleep.

The practitioner lies down, stays still and watches the sensations of the transition into sleep as they unfold.

Spontaneous images.

Inner sounds.

A feeling of heaviness.

Vibrations.

A sensation of falling.

In spiritual literature, these “vibrations” are often read as the start of astral separation.

Scientifically, caution is needed. Various unusual sensations can occur in the hypnagogic phase, just before sleep: feelings of movement, muscle twitches, sounds or images.

A 2024 review offers one explanation. Some sleep-related out-of-body experiences may occur when awareness stays on during certain transitions into REM sleep.

This closeness explains why some techniques, astral travel on one side and lucid dreaming on the other, look so alike.

In lucid dreaming, however, the goal is better defined scientifically. The sleeper becomes aware, during the dream, that they are dreaming.

Methods such as MILD and SSILD have been studied experimentally. The International Lucid Dream Induction Study found that both methods could increase the frequency of lucid dreams under certain conditions.

Astral projection techniques have much less specific experimental support.

That does not mean they produce no experience at all.

5What precautions should you take with an astral projection technique?

To begin with, a careful practice lasts twenty to thirty minutes at most.

Avoid regularly sacrificing your sleep in the hope of an out-of-body experience.

Lack of sleep can itself increase strange perceptions, irritability and confusion.

Don't use fast, forced breathing to speed up the sensations either. Tingling, dizziness and a feeling of unreality may indeed appear. But they are no proof of astral projection, and they can become unpleasant or dangerous.

The simplest framework is:

  • relaxation;
  • natural breathing;
  • body imagery;
  • observation;
  • return.

If an experience begins, stay curious.

If you feel yourself floating, observe the sensation.

If nothing happens, don't add another hour of mental strain.

Astral projection has a classic paradox: the harder you try to force your way “out,” the more alert you become.

Yet falling asleep and hypnagogic images rarely respond well to goals pursued with the determination of a tax auditor.

Finally, you may often feel cut off from yourself, or like a stranger in your own body. The world may feel so unreal that it causes you distress. In that case, exercises that deliberately pull your sense of self away from your body may not suit you.

It is better to choose practices that strengthen your grounding in the body. Ask a professional for advice if needed.

So the rope, the roll-out and floating can be explored as experiments in body imagery.

They can produce extraordinary sensations.

What they cannot do, on their own, is prove that the mind has left the body, physically or metaphysically.

1Why “Dreaming of a Snake = Betrayal” Is a Very Bad Place to Start

You dream about a snake.

You go online.

First website:

“Imminent betrayal.”

Second:

“Spiritual transformation.”

Third:

“Repressed sexuality.”

Fourth:

“Money on the way.”

At this point, your snake has more career paths than a particularly ambitious graduate.

The problem with dream dictionaries is simple: they often claim to assign a fixed meaning to a symbol regardless of the person dreaming, the context, the emotion and their personal history.

But a snake obviously doesn’t mean the same thing to:

  • a herpetologist;
  • someone who was bitten as a child;
  • someone who loves snakes;
  • a person who watched a documentary about cobras two hours before bed.

One might feel fascination.

Another, terror.

The third, curiosity.

The symbol is identical.

The psychological experience absolutely isn’t.

That doesn’t mean cultural symbols don’t matter. Some images carry very rich historical, religious or mythological associations, and Carl Gustav Jung notably used amplification to compare a dream image with various cultural symbolic motifs.

But the order matters.

Start with your own dream.

Only then open the books.

Otherwise, you risk replacing your experience with the first appealing interpretation you find online.

2Question #1 — What Actually Happened in the Dream?

Before any interpretation, describe the dream as if you had to hand the script to someone who knows nothing about it.

Not:

“I dreamed about my fear of failure.”

That’s already an interpretation.

Write this instead:

“I’m in a high school I don’t recognize. I have to take an exam but I don’t have a pen. Everyone starts writing. I look in my bag and find it’s empty.”

This distinction between description and meaning is fundamental.

Write down:

  • the place;
  • the people;
  • the actions;
  • the objects;
  • bizarre changes;
  • the emotion;
  • how the dream ended.

Then give it a title.

THE EXAM WITHOUT A PEN.

You now have a scene clear enough to work with.

Next, ask:

“What was the strongest emotional moment?”

Maybe not the exam.

Maybe discovering the empty bag.

Emotion:

helplessness.

Or shame.

Or anger.

That emotion often provides more information than the most spectacular symbol.

A dream with a dragon in it can matter less than a mundane detail that suddenly shook you.

The mind loves special effects.

The analysis has to look at the storyline too.

3Question #2 — What Does Each Element Mean to Me Personally?

Take the main elements and use a very simple free association technique.

Write:

high school → ?

You answer:

  • pressure;
  • adolescence;
  • comparison;
  • fear of being judged.

Then:

exam → ?

  • evaluation;
  • performance;
  • having to prove something.

Then:

empty bag → ?

  • not being prepared;
  • not having the resources;
  • having forgotten something.

You start to see a theme emerging.

But don’t force it.

Now suppose that, for you, high school mostly brings to mind your best friend and a happy time.

The reading changes completely.

That’s exactly why personal context has to come before universal symbols.

A good question to ask is:

“If this element were a person in a room, what would it spontaneously represent for me?”

Or:

“Where have I recently felt something that resembles the emotion in this dream?”

Maybe you have a presentation tomorrow.

Your brain used the vocabulary of an old exam to represent a current situation of evaluation.

No need to assume your unconscious is mysteriously announcing your failure.

It may simply be reusing an old emotional architecture.

4Question #3 — Where Does This Dynamic Exist in My Life Right Now?

This is probably the most useful question:

“What in my current life resembles the dynamic of this dream?”

Not the object.

The dynamic.

You dream that you’re looking for a house and never find it.

The subject may have nothing to do with real estate.

The dynamic is:

  • searching;
  • not finding;
  • being rushed;
  • feeling lost.

Where does it exist right now?

Work?

A relationship?

A decision?

You dream that a train leaves without you.

Dynamic:

  • opportunity;
  • being late;
  • helplessness.

You dream of a giant wave.

Dynamic:

something is becoming too big to control.

This method avoids rigid translations.

It turns the dream into a potential metaphor, without automatically claiming that it contains an intentional hidden message.

Dream science doesn’t support the idea of a universal code that can translate every image, but many models recognize that dreams reuse elements of memory, concerns, emotions and recent experiences.

So a dream can be interesting not because it predicts the future, but because it sometimes reveals the way your brain is putting your present together.

5Question #4 — What Would Change If I Rewrote the Ending of the Dream?

This fourth question is especially interesting for recurring or unpleasant dreams.

Suppose you regularly dream that someone is chasing you.

Ask:

“What would I have wanted to do in the dream?”

Hide?

Turn around?

Ask who this person is?

Find a door?

Call someone?

You can then deliberately imagine a new ending during the day.

This technique is reminiscent of some approaches used for nightmares, notably Imagery Rehearsal Therapy, in which the script of a recurring nightmare can be changed and mentally rehearsed while awake.

Careful: this doesn’t mean every dream has to be “fixed.”

An unpleasant dream can simply be an unpleasant dream.

But consciously changing its script can sometimes reduce the helplessness associated with a recurring nightmare.

In a more Jungian framework, you could also continue the dialogue with a dream figure through active imagination, but keep an exploratory attitude rather than a literal one.

If a dream character tells you:

“Quit your job tomorrow,”

that is psychological material to reflect on.

Not necessarily a legally binding cosmic order.

6The Right Interpretation Isn’t the One That Feels Magical, but the One That Deepens Your Understanding

After answering the four questions, sum it up in one sentence:

“This dream might reflect…”

Notice the word:

might.

Example:

“This dream might reflect my current feeling of not being prepared enough for a performance review at work.”

Then ask:

“Does this reading actually help me?”

If so, great.

If it only produces more fear, superstition or confusion, drop it.

A good interpretation doesn’t need to be spectacular.

Maybe you dreamed of an ocean because you saw the sea recently.

Maybe you dreamed of your ex because a memory was reactivated.

Maybe you dreamed of a childhood home because your brain simply used a setting that is deeply consolidated in memory.

Not every dream is a prophecy.

Not every dream is psychologically profound, either.

But some are excellent mirrors of your concerns, your emotions or the conflicts you haven’t yet put clearly into words.

The dream then becomes less an encrypted message sent by some mysterious postal service of the unconscious than a spontaneous creation of your own mind, strange enough to sometimes force you to look at a problem differently.

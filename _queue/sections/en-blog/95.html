1We have probably never had so many ways to avoid silence

Waiting for a train? Podcast.

Walking for ten minutes? Music.

Cooking? Video.

Taking a shower? Playlist.

Lying down before sleeping? Series, audiobook or guided meditation.

Even the few seconds spent in an elevator sometimes become uncomfortable enough to justify an urgent phone check, as if a decisive message could have turned our existence upside down since the ground floor.

Silence has obviously not disappeared from the planet, but our ability to eliminate it almost entirely from daily life is historically quite remarkable: we now carry with us a permanent source of voices, music, videos, notifications and information that fills virtually every empty slot.

Added to this is real environmental noise: road traffic, trains, planes, construction work, ventilation, neighbors, leisure activities and urban density.

The World Health Organization also considers excessive noise to be a real public health problem, and not a simple question of comfort; chronic exposure is associated in particular with sleep disorders, annoyance, cardiovascular effects, certain cognitive impairments and reduced well-being. (World Health Organization)

However, two phenomena must be distinguished.

The first is physical:

how many decibels actually reach your body?

The second is attentional:

how long does your mind go without voluntarily receiving new information?

You can be in a perfectly quiet room and still consume three hours of videos.

And you can walk through a forest full of birdsong without considering the environment “noisy.”

Useful silence is therefore not necessarily the total absence of sounds.

It may also be the absence of a permanent demand on your attention.

2Environmental noise is not only annoying: it can actually affect the body and sleep

We tend to view a noise we are accustomed to as an essentially subjective problem:

“After a while, you don’t hear it anymore.”

But no longer consciously paying attention to a noise does not necessarily mean that the body no longer responds to it.

WHO recommendations on environmental noise are based on work showing associations with sleep disturbances, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, annoyance and certain cognitive difficulties, and the WHO estimates that approximately one in five people in the European Union is exposed to unhealthy levels of road noise. (World Health Organization)

Nighttime is particularly important.

You may not remember full awakenings and still experience physiological disturbances linked to noise.

Sleep doesn’t need to be interrupted by someone using a drill at 2:14 a.m. to be affected; repeated sound events can modify sleep continuity and promote micro-arousals or autonomic reactions.

In its European recommendations, the WHO notably proposed a long-term target of 40 dB of average outdoor nighttime level, while recognizing that this value is not easily achievable everywhere. (World Health Organization)

This obviously does not mean that a sound at 41 dB instantly becomes dangerous.

Public health recommendations concern average exposures and populations.

But they remind us of something important:

calm is not just an aesthetic luxury.

It is also a component of a healthy environment.

3Does the brain really need silence to “regenerate”?

The Internet loves spectacular formulas:

“Two minutes of silence regenerate the brain.”

“Silence creates new brain cells.”

“Ten minutes without noise reset your nervous system.”

The problem is that these claims often mix animal studies, very specific experimental results and extrapolations intended for social media.

We do not have a universal therapeutic dose of silence comparable to that of a medicine.

On the other hand, temporarily reducing sensory and cognitive demands can offer an extremely concrete change of state.

Imagine a day consisting of:

  • meetings;
  • messages;
  • music;
  • conversation;
  • traffic;
  • notifications;
  • videos;
  • television.

Even when each of these elements is pleasant, the brain spends its time selecting, filtering, understanding and responding.

Ten minutes of deliberate silence removes some of these demands.

You then begin to notice what was covered:

  • breathing;
  • shoulder tension;
  • thoughts;
  • faint environmental sounds;
  • fatigue.

Silence does not necessarily “empty” the mind.

It often reveals how full it was.

This is why the first few minutes of silent meditation can be surprisingly noisy internally.

You sit down to find peace.

Your brain responds:

“Great, now here’s everything we haven’t thought about since Tuesday.”

It’s not a failure.

It’s almost the diagnosis.

4Why a calm environment can help attention without having to live in a cave

Noise does not affect all tasks equally.

Familiar music may feel pleasant during an automatic activity.

A conversation in the background can, on the contrary, become extremely disruptive when you try to read a complex sentence.

Noisy environments can impose an attentional cost, particularly when the brain must constantly filter biologically relevant sounds — voices, sudden changes, warning signals — even when you would like to ignore them.

In children, the WHO notes in particular data linking chronic exposure to certain environmental noise, such as aircraft noise, to adverse effects on some aspects of cognitive performance. (World Health Organization)

This does not mean that we should aim for absolute silence.

Some people work better with regular background sound.

Others find total silence anxiety-provoking.

The best environment depends on the task and the person.

But there is a major difference between:

choosing a steady sound because it actually helps your concentration

and

having become unable to endure thirty seconds without stimulation.

Try an extremely simple experiment.

Once a day, do an activity normally accompanied by audio content without adding anything.

Walk for fifteen minutes.

Or prepare dinner.

Or drink a coffee.

At first, you may feel a kind of emptiness.

Then the environment reappears.

The sound of your footsteps.

The rooms of the house.

Your breathing.

The thoughts that come and then leave.

It’s not spectacular.

That is precisely the point.

5External silence and inner silence are not the same thing

A silent retreat can be extraordinarily noisy mentally.

Conversely, a person experienced in meditation can maintain a sense of inner calm in a train station.

This makes it possible to distinguish two dimensions:

  • reducing external stimuli;
  • changing one’s relationship to inner mental activity.

Meditation works mainly on the second.

You don’t necessarily try to suppress all thoughts.

You gradually learn not to follow each of them.

A ten-minute practice can be very simple:

  • sit without music or a guiding voice;
  • listen to the sounds present;
  • do not name them immediately;
  • notice the intervals;
  • then pay attention to your thoughts in the same way.

A car passes.

Then nothing.

A thought appears:

“I need to send this email.”

Then it withdraws.

You thus discover that “inner silence” is not necessarily a space where no thought appears.

It can be a space where not every thought automatically gets an answer.

This distinction is particularly useful for people who think they are “bad at meditation” because their minds keep producing content.

The brain produces thoughts.

It’s its job.

The practice consists of gradually reducing the obligation to respond to them.

6The 20-minute silence challenge: relearning to leave part of the day unoccupied

For seven days, choose twenty minutes a day with no added content.

No podcast.

No music.

No video.

No optional conversation.

No need to stay perfectly still.

You can walk, tidy up slowly, or just sit.

In the first few days, observe above all the urge to pick up the phone.

It can arrive almost physically.

“Just checking something.”

Wait.

Then notice what happens after a few minutes.

The goal is not to become a hermit or to declare war on podcasts, which humanity can keep producing without guilt.

It is simply about restoring a capacity that our modern environment makes very easy to lose:

spending a moment without consuming content.

If you live in a very noisy area, the question obviously also becomes collective and environmental; exposure to traffic or planes is not solved by a mindfulness exercise, and noise reduction policies are truly a matter of public health. (World Health Organization)

But on an individual scale, we can at least decide not to automatically add a layer of chosen noise on top of all the noise we already endure.

Silence is unlikely to disappear.

The risk is rather that we forget how to stay in it.

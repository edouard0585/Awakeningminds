1Your inner critic may have been trying to help. It just flunked its management training

You send an email.

Three seconds later:

“That sentence was ridiculous.”

You look at a photo:

“You could at least make an effort.”

You start a new project:

“Let’s be serious, you’ll never stick with it.”

Your inner critic is extraordinarily productive.

No public holidays.

No lunch break.

Open 24 hours a day.

So the natural reflex is to want to:

silence your inner critic.

But that rarely works for very long.

Why?

Because inner criticism isn’t just a stray voice.

It can be a set of mental habits learned in order to:

  • avoid mistakes;
  • prevent rejection;
  • maintain high standards;
  • anticipate criticism from others;
  • reduce risk.

Imagine a child who is regularly praised only when they succeed perfectly.

Their brain may gradually learn:

“If I criticize myself before others do, I’ll be better prepared.”

The strategy has a logic to it.

The problem is that it sometimes carries on thirty years later, even when it no longer helps at all.

So the goal isn’t necessarily to get rid of this voice.

It’s to learn to stop automatically handing it the CEO job.

2Useful self-criticism or personal attack: learn to tell the difference

Not all self-criticism is harmful.

You finish a project and think:

“I should have prepared that part better.”

Fine.

That’s an assessment of behavior.

It points to:

  • a problem;
  • a possible cause;
  • an improvement.

Compare it with:

“I’m truly useless.”

That sentence contains no usable instruction.

It attacks your identity.

So a good test is to ask:

“Does this thought tell me something I can do differently?”

If so, it can become useful.

If it only says:

  • hopeless;
  • lazy;
  • ridiculous;
  • incapable;
  • not good enough,

you’re probably no longer in constructive assessment.

You’re in attack mode.

And constant attack isn’t necessarily a good engine for performance.

High self-criticism is associated with various psychological difficulties, while interventions centered on self-compassion show encouraging results in reducing it. A meta-analysis of 20 randomized trials found a significant average reduction in self-criticism with self-compassion interventions, although the heterogeneity and moderate quality of the studies call for caution.

3Technique #1 — Turn the accusation into information

Take a recent criticism.

“I’m lazy.”

Ask:

“What concrete behavior is it trying to describe?”

A possible answer:

“I’ve put off this file three times.”

Fine.

Now:

“Why?”

Is the file:

too vague?

boring?

stressful?

too big?

poorly defined?

Your inner critic has just gone from:

“You’re lazy”

to:

“This task triggers a lot of avoidance because it hasn’t been broken down properly.”

Suddenly, a solution appears.

Break the file down.

Start with ten minutes.

Ask for clarification.

Schedule a time slot.

Shame said:

“Change your personality.”

Analysis says:

“Change your strategy.”

The second instruction is slightly more practical.

4Technique #2 — Cognitive defusion: “I’m having the thought that…”

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, or ACT, uses cognitive defusion, among other tools, to change your relationship with your thoughts.

Suppose your brain says:

“I’m going to fail.”

Simply add:

“I’m having the thought that I’m going to fail.”

Then:

“I notice that my mind is producing the thought that I’m going to fail.”

The content hasn’t changed.

But your position in relation to the content has.

First version:

I’m going to fail.

It’s a reality.

Second version:

I’m having the thought that I’m going to fail.

It’s a mental event.

This distance is fundamental.

The aim of mindfulness and defusion isn’t to replace every negative thought with:

“I’m extraordinary and the universe adores me.”

Some positive affirmations are so far removed from how we feel that they immediately trigger an internal counterargument.

Defusion uses a subtler strategy:

a thought can exist without becoming an instruction.

A 2026 systematic review on ACT, self-compassion and self-criticism concludes that ACT interventions can foster certain aspects of psychological flexibility and self-compassion, while noting that the strength of the evidence varies across outcomes and studies.

5Technique #3 — Talk to yourself as you would to someone you’re genuinely responsible for

Here’s a simple experiment.

Imagine a friend tells you:

“I completely blew my presentation.”

Answer the way you would actually speak to them.

Probably:

“Yes, it wasn’t as good as you wanted, but you can look at what didn’t work.”

Now, replace your friend with yourself.

Strangely, the sentence often becomes:

“You’re a disaster. Why can you never do anything right?”

Why do we sometimes have an internal management style that would get any company shut down for creating a toxic workplace?

Self-compassion, popularized notably by Kristin Neff, isn’t about convincing yourself that everything is wonderful.

It generally combines:

  • kindness toward yourself;
  • recognition of our common humanity;
  • mindful attention to your experience.

So a self-compassionate phrasing might be:

“That was genuinely disappointing. I notice I’m judging myself very harshly. Mistakes are part of learning. What’s the most useful response now?”

No self-indulgence.

No mandatory rainbows.

Just a change of tone.

Compassion Focused Therapy interventions also show favorable results in reducing self-criticism and increasing the capacity for self-reassurance in the studies pooled in a meta-analysis.

6Technique #4 — Negotiate with the critic instead of declaring war on it

Try personifying your inner critic for five minutes.

Not because it is literally another person.

But because it helps you observe its function.

Ask:

“What are you trying to protect me from?”

Possible answers:

  • ridicule;
  • failure;
  • rejection;
  • poverty;
  • disappointment;
  • loss of control.

Then ask:

“Does your strategy really work?”

Suppose it answers:

“I criticize you so that you’ll work harder.”

Check:

does the criticism actually produce work?

Or:

  • anxiety;
  • procrastination;
  • paralysis;
  • exhaustion?

You can then negotiate:

“I accept your goal: preparing this project properly. But I refuse the method: insulting me for six hours. Give me three specific problems to solve instead.”

This technique echoes certain therapeutic approaches that treat different inner reactions as modes or strategies that serve a function.

The critic then stops being a monster to eliminate.

It becomes an anxious advisor whose megaphone you take away.

7A five-minute protocol for when the judging machine starts up

Minute 1 — Write down the exact sentence

Not:

“I feel bad.”

Write:

“I’m not good enough to do this job.”

Minute 2 — Add some distance

“I notice my mind is telling me I’m not good enough.”

Minute 3 — Identify the fear

“If this thought were trying to protect me, what would it be afraid of?”

Example:

being humiliated.

Minute 4 — Rephrase it usefully

Instead of:

“You’re going to fail,”

turn it into:

“You’re worried because this presentation matters. Let’s prepare the three difficult points.”

Minute 5 — Action

Do one action that takes less than five minutes.

Open the document.

Write the title.

Send the message.

Prepare a question.

This matters.

Because the inner critic loves abstract courtrooms.

Concrete action gives it much less to work with.

8Why compassion isn’t complacency

The most common pushback is:

“If I stop criticizing myself, I’ll become lazy.”

That’s understandable.

Many people have used fear and self-criticism as their engine for years.

But imagine two coaches.

The first one yells:

“You’re useless, get moving!”

The second one says:

“This attempt isn’t at the expected level. Here’s what we’re going to fix.”

Which one would you want to keep for twenty years?

Compassion doesn’t rule out high expectations.

It distinguishes between:

high standards

and

inner humiliation.

The available data suggest precisely that self-compassion interventions can reduce self-criticism without the aim being to remove all personal responsibility.

You can perfectly well say:

“I did a poor job.”

Without adding:

“So I’m a bad person.”

That small separation changes an enormous amount.

9When your inner critic calls for real support

Self-criticism can sometimes become extremely harsh.

It can go hand in hand with:

  • depression;
  • eating disorders;
  • anxiety;
  • severe perfectionism;
  • trauma;
  • chronic shame;
  • strong feelings of worthlessness.

If your inner dialogue becomes constantly humiliating, if you feel you can never relax without feeling guilty, or if your thoughts turn toward ideas of harming yourself, personal development exercises may not be enough.

A psychologist or another qualified professional can help you understand the mechanisms that keep this system going.

The goal isn’t to end up with an empty head where no critical thought exists.

The goal is much more realistic:

hearing a criticism without automatically handing it the keys to your identity.

Your inner critic can stay in the meeting.

It just doesn’t need to chair the board anymore.

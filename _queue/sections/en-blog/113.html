1There is no single “Amazonian shamanism,” but dozens of different religious worlds

Talking about Amazonian shamanism is convenient, but it becomes dangerous the moment the phrase suggests that a vast region stretching from Peru to Brazil, by way of Colombia, Ecuador, Bolivia and other territories, shares a single religion.

The Amazon is home to hundreds of peoples and languages. Their ritual specialists, cosmologies, plants, songs, myths and forms of healing can differ profoundly.

The West has nonetheless popularized a fairly standardized image: a healer, ayahuasca, songs called icaros, a forest inhabited by spirits and “plant teachers.” This image does match certain traditions, notably in the Peruvian Amazon and the mestizo vegetalismo studied by the anthropologist Luis Eduardo Luna, but it shouldn't become a universal model.

His book Vegetalismo: Shamanism Among the Mestizo Population of the Peruvian Amazon, published in 1986 on the basis of fieldwork, remains a foundational reference for understanding how mestizo healers in the Iquitos region conceive of teacher plants. The Smithsonian and Wellcome Collection catalogs confirm the publication and its grounding in the study of traditional medicine, ayahuasca and Peruvian shamanism.

The word shaman itself comes from Siberia, by the way, not the Amazon.

Using it for Amazonian specialists is therefore a matter of comparative anthropological category, often convenient but sometimes too broad. Depending on the region, you'll come across local terms such as onánya, curandero, vegetalista, ayahuasquero and many others.

Starting with this diversity keeps us from turning the largest tropical forest in the world into one big spiritual retreat center.

2Icaros: songs that are not just background music

In several traditions of the western Amazon, icaros play an essential role. This regional Spanish term refers to various ritual songs used by healers, especially in the context of Peruvian vegetalismo.

Among the Shipibo-Konibo, there are also local categories of medicinal and ritual songs. Contemporary sources describe icaros as associated with healing, protection, guiding ceremonies and relationships with plants or spiritual entities. An article in ReVista, the publication of Harvard's David Rockefeller Center for Latin American Studies, for example, describes a Shipibo ícaro as a song learned through a ritual relationship with plants and used to enlist their help in different areas.

From the traditional perspective, then, the song isn't simply a pretty melody that helps you relax.

It has ritual agency.

Some practitioners believe the songs are learned from the plants during periods of diet, isolation and discipline. The healer doesn't necessarily see himself as the composer in the Western sense: he may consider that the plant teaches him the song.

Scientifically, it is possible to study rhythm, timbre, emotion, expectations, attention and the effects of music on experience. That does not make it possible to show that the plant literally transmits a melody through spiritual communication.

The two levels need to stay clearly separate.

The first describes a cultural conception of knowledge.

The second looks for observable mechanisms.

Reducing an icaro to a therapeutic frequency would be as impoverishing as reducing Mozart's Requiem to variations in air pressure.

3“Plant teachers” and the dieta: when a plant is seen as a teacher

Luna's work has documented in particular the notion of teacher plants, or plant teachers. In vegetalismo, certain plants are not conceived merely as substances containing useful molecules. They can be regarded as beings capable of teaching, communicating and passing knowledge on to the practitioner.

Training can include a dieta: a period during which certain dietary, social and sexual restrictions are observed and during which the practitioner maintains an intensive relationship with one particular plant.

This conception is profoundly different from the Western biomedical model.

For pharmacology, a plant acts through its molecules and their interactions with the body.

For some Amazonian cosmologies, it can also act as a non-human person or spiritual agent.

No current scientific experiment demonstrates the objective existence of a “plant spirit” directly teaching a song. Anthropology, on the other hand, can document very rigorously that people organize their learning, their identity and their medicine around this conception.

This difference is crucial.

Studying a belief is neither ridiculing it nor proving it.

Likewise, the presence of psychoactive substances doesn't allow us to reduce the whole experience to the chemical compound alone. A ceremony includes setting, sounds, social relationships, expectations, cultural narrative, learning and interpretation.

Ayahuasca itself is not used in the same way by all Amazonian peoples, and some so-called shamanic traditions don't use it at all.

The recent globalization of psychedelic tourism unfortunately tends to flatten these differences: everything becomes an “ayahuasca ceremony,” even when the practice offered far from the Amazon combines elements drawn from several peoples.

4The living forest: an environment that is not just a backdrop

The modern Western view tends to draw a clear line between humans, animals, plants and landscape. Many Amazonian cosmologies organize these relationships differently.

Anthropological research, especially work associated with Amazonian animism and perspectivism, has shown that some societies attribute intentionality, subjectivity or personhood to beings that the West classifies as non-human.

That obviously doesn't mean that all Amazonian peoples share an identical philosophical doctrine.

But it does help explain why the forest can be experienced as a web of relationships rather than a stock of plant resources surrounding humans.

A tree can have a spirit master.

An animal can have a point of view.

A river can be associated with specific powers or beings.

The healer then acts in a world full of invisible diplomatic relations: alliances, protections, dangers and obligations.

This logic also explains the place of song. In the traditional interpretation, an icaro doesn't act only “on the patient's brain”; it can be conceived as a way of addressing, summoning, organizing or keeping away non-human agents.

Among the Shipibo-Konibo, the relationships between songs, visions and kené geometric patterns have also inspired a great deal of anthropological and artistic work. Recent research specifically discusses the links between Shipibo aesthetics, kené patterns and contemporary representations of icaros.

The living forest is thus as much a social universe as a biological environment.

5Ayahuasca, tourism and respect: what the West risks losing by exporting the practice

The international boom in interest in ayahuasca has created a paradoxical situation.

It has given worldwide visibility to some Amazonian knowledge, provided income to some communities and encouraged new scientific research. But it has also created an industry in which songs, patterns, clothing and ritual titles are sometimes pulled out of their context and then recombined for a foreign audience.

A participant can thus hear an icaro, see a kené pattern and receive a brew called ayahuasca without understanding the particular history of the people these elements belong to.

The problem can't be reduced to a question of cultural “purity.” Cultures have always exchanged and evolved. It's more about power, attribution and responsibility.

Who is teaching?

Who gets paid?

Who can present themselves as a healer?

Which communities benefit economically from the globalization of their practices?

On top of that, there is a safety issue. Ayahuasca is psychoactive and can interact with certain medical conditions, certain medications and certain psychiatric vulnerabilities. A tourist ceremony is therefore not the equivalent of a simple guided meditation.

In the end, Amazonian shamanism becomes far more interesting once you stop looking for a single “secret of the jungle.”

Icaros, diets, relationships with plants and the perception of an inhabited forest then become windows onto radically different ways of understanding knowledge, health and the living world.

The big lesson may not be that Amazonian peoples hold some universal technique the West has forgotten.

It is more demanding than that: they remind us that a human being can live in a forest without assuming they are the only true subject inside it.

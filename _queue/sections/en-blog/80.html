1Thinking Looks for an Exit; Ruminating Drives Around the Same Roundabout Fifteen Times

You’re lying in bed going back over a conversation from the afternoon, then you replay the sentence you said, imagine the one you should have said, anticipate what the other person is thinking now, build three future conversations to fix the problem, go back to the original conversation to check a tone of voice and, forty-five minutes later, you’re in exactly the same place mentally, except it’s 1:17 a.m. and you’ve now added exhaustion to the list of problems: welcome to mental rumination.

The difference between reflection and rumination doesn’t depend only on how long you spend thinking, because complex reflection can be long and extremely productive; the fundamental difference lies rather in what happens as the process unfolds, since useful reflection gradually moves toward information, a decision, a new perspective or an action, whereas rumination recycles the same questions without reaching any real resolution.

Take the question:

“Why did I mess up that presentation?”

Constructive reflection might produce:

“I hadn’t prepared the last two slides well enough, so next time I’ll rehearse them more.”

The end.

Rumination adds:

“Why am I always like this? Why do other people seem so much more at ease? Did they notice? Why did I say that? What if my manager thinks I’m incompetent?”

None of these questions necessarily leads to a new action, but each one keeps the event emotionally active.

Rumination has been studied extensively in depression and anxiety, and the models developed in particular by Susan Nolen-Hoeksema have shown how important this repetitive response style is in keeping certain negative emotional states going.

So the first goal is not to silence your brain, a goal about as realistic as asking your stomach to stop digesting for good, but to learn to recognize the moment when a thought stops producing information and simply starts going round in circles.

2Techniques 1 and 2: Separate the Real Problem From the Loop, Then Give It an Appointment

The first technique seems almost too simple: take a sheet of paper and write down the question that keeps spinning in your head, then ask yourself “Is there a concrete action I can take in the next twenty-four hours?”

If you’re ruminating because a bill hasn’t been paid, the action might be sending an email tomorrow morning; if you’re ruminating because you don’t know whether someone still likes you, there may be a conversation to have; but if you’re ruminating about what someone thought of you for seven seconds at a dinner in 2023, you’re probably facing an uncertainty that your brain is trying to resolve with a level of investigation slightly above that of a forensics lab.

Then write:

Possible action: none right now.

This wording matters, because it helps you separate having no immediate solution from an urgent need to keep thinking.

The second technique is to set up a scheduled worry period, sometimes called worry time or stimulus control for worry in cognitive behavioral approaches: rather than negotiating with each worry as it appears, you set aside, say, fifteen to twenty minutes in the late afternoon to examine them deliberately, and when a thought pops up at 11:30 a.m., you quickly jot it down and tell yourself, “I’ll look at that during my scheduled slot.”

This strategy may seem odd (scheduling your worries like a Zoom meeting with your anxiety), but it teaches something essential: a thought can be postponed without being denied, which gradually weakens the idea that every anxious scenario deserves immediate analysis.

3Techniques 3 and 4: Leave Your Head Through Your Body, Then Learn to See Thoughts as Events

The third technique is deliberately physical, because rumination doesn’t always need to be fought with a better thought; when you’ve been stuck in a mental loop for thirty minutes, switching channels can be far more effective than searching for the perfect argument that will defeat all the previous thoughts.

Walk for fifteen minutes without your phone, do a few movements, tidy a room, take a shower or do an activity that requires real physical coordination, because the goal isn’t to “run away from your emotions” but to shift your attention enough to interrupt the repetition for a moment and let your mind return to a broader perspective.

Physical activity is also associated with many psychological benefits, and some experimental research suggests it can influence rumination processes, although the effect obviously depends on the population, the intensity and the context; so it would be an exaggeration to claim that a walk automatically cures rumination, but it is a very low-cost strategy that is often available right away.

The fourth technique comes in particular from ACT and cognitive defusion: instead of thinking “I’ve ruined everything,” put it as “I notice my mind is producing the thought that I’ve ruined everything,” then observe what changes.

You’re not claiming the thought is false.

You’re not replacing it with:

“I am absolutely perfect and every decision I make is wonderful.”

You’re simply changing its status.

It goes from:

reality

to:

mental event.

The difference looks grammatical, but it can become psychologically huge, because we are often much more able to choose our behavior when we recognize that a thought is a suggestion from our mind rather than an official order signed by reality.

4Technique 5: Mindfulness Without Turning Meditation Into a Mental Boxing Ring

A classic mistake is to start meditation for rumination with the following goal:

“For ten minutes, I’m going to think about nothing.”

Forty seconds later:

a thought.

Failure.

A new thought:

“I’m bad at meditation.”

Then:

“Why can I never calm my mind?”

Congratulations: you’ve just used meditation to ruminate about your rumination.

A much more realistic practice is to sit down, choose your breath or bodily sensations as your point of reference and, when a loop appears, simply acknowledge it:

  • “planning”;
  • “remembering”;
  • “worrying”;
  • “repeating”.

Then come back to your body.

Ten seconds later, the thought comes back?

You start again.

Success isn’t measured by the number of thoughts.

It’s measured by the number of times you notice your attention has wandered and consciously choose to come back.

This practice develops precisely a metacognitive skill that helps against repetitive thinking: seeing that you are thinking instead of being completely absorbed in the content of the thought.

Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy, developed notably by Zindel Segal, Mark Williams and John Teasdale, uses skills of exactly this kind to prevent depressive relapse, with the aim of helping people change their relationship to automatic negative thoughts rather than solving each one as if it were an urgent problem.

5Technique 6: Move From “Why?” to “What Now?”

Rumination loves the word:

why?

Why am I like this?

Why did I say that?

Why did this person leave me?

Why is it always me?

Some “why” questions are obviously useful, but when they become global and abstract, they can easily fuel endless analysis; so try deliberately switching to questions focused on the present and on action:

“What do I actually know?”

“What am I assuming?”

“What part is still up to me?”

“What is the smallest useful action right now?”

Suppose you’ve been turned down for a job.

Rumination:

“Why does nobody ever recognize my worth?”

Concrete question:

“Can I ask for feedback on my application and improve something for the next one?”

The second question doesn’t erase the disappointment, and that matters, because getting out of rumination doesn’t mean getting rid of the emotion; sometimes you simply have to accept being sad, disappointed or angry without turning that emotion into a six-hour intellectual investigation.

You can feel a loss without immediately finding a lesson in it.

You can be hurt without immediately understanding why.

Part of getting out of rumination is precisely tolerating a question that stays unanswered for a while.

6The Ten-Minute Anti-Loop Protocol, and When to Look for More Help

When you realize you’re ruminating, take ten minutes: write down the subject of the loop in one sentence, check whether there is a concrete action, note that action if there is one, then do five minutes of physical activity or walking and finish with two minutes of attention to bodily sensations, letting thoughts appear without deliberately restarting the analysis.

If the loop comes back afterward, tell yourself:

“I’ve already looked at this question today.”

It’s not a magic formula.

The loop may come back.

But you’re gradually training your brain to understand that a repeated thought doesn’t automatically earn more airtime.

If, on the other hand, rumination takes up several hours a day, seriously disrupts your sleep, or comes with depression, significant anxiety, compulsions or thoughts of harming yourself, the problem probably goes beyond a simple “bad mental habit” and deserves a professional assessment, because repetitive thoughts can appear in different conditions that are not all treated the same way.

So the real goal is not to have a silent brain.

It’s to get your choice back:

to think when a thought deserves your attention, then go back to living when it has nothing new to tell you.

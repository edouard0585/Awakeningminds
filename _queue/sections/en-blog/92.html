1Why looking up at a mountain so easily makes it feel like it belongs to another world

Sometimes you just need to stand at the foot of a mountain to understand why so many societies attributed a spiritual meaning to it: while most elements of the landscape are found at our height or below our line of sight, a mountain immediately imposes a vertical movement, forces the gaze to rise, reduces the human being to a small silhouette and creates a very concrete border between the familiar world of the valley and a higher space that seems more difficult, quieter, colder and closer to the sky.

It would obviously be wrong to claim that all peoples prayed at altitude, or that all sacred mountains have exactly the same meaning, because religious and cosmological traditions differ enormously; some mountains are considered divine homes, others places of revelation, still others ancestors, axes of the world, spaces of purification or territories that should precisely not be climbed.

But the vertical analogy has an almost irresistible symbolic power.

Below:

everyday life.

Above:

difficulty, rarity, distance.

Going up easily becomes a metaphor for:

  • purification;
  • transformation;
  • asceticism;
  • elevation;
  • encounter with the sacred.

Mircea Eliade contributed greatly to popularizing the notion of axis mundi, the symbolic axis connecting different levels of the cosmos, even if contemporary anthropology invites us to avoid applying a single interpretive grid to all cultures; a mountain can work symbolically as a center or axis in a tradition without this meaning that a universal theory explains all the sacred mountains on the planet.

What is much more certain is that the very architecture of the landscape creates a particular bodily experience.

Even before theology, you have to go up.

And going up already changes the person who climbs.

2Mount Kailash: the summit whose symbolic power is also due to the fact that we cannot conquer it

Mount Kailash, located in the west of the Tibetan plateau, probably represents one of the most extraordinary examples of a mountain shared by several religious traditions, notably Hinduism, Tibetan Buddhism, Jainism and Bön, even if each naturally attributes to it its own set of narratives, meanings and practices.

What makes Kailash particularly interesting for our contemporary relationship to the mountain is that traditional religious practice is not fundamentally built around the conquest of the summit.

We walk around it.

Circumambulation, often called kora in the Tibetan context, makes moving around the mountain a ritual practice in itself.

This difference is immense.

Modern mountaineering asks:

“How do I reach the top?”

The pilgrim instead asks:

“How do I move correctly around what is sacred?”

The landscape ceases to be an obstacle to overcome and becomes a center around which the body organizes itself.

It’s almost the opposite of our “summit reached” culture, of photographs with raised arms and of the performance counter.

The mountain remains inaccessible in the symbolic sense precisely because we do not necessarily seek to reduce it to a trophy.

This illustrates a very important principle when talking about spiritual travel: not all sacred landscapes are meant to be climbed, touched or transformed into a personal experience.

Sometimes the practice is the limit.

And respecting the limit is part of the pilgrimage.

3Mount Fuji and Sinai: two mountains, two stories, the same transformative power of movement

Mount Fuji, in Japan, shows how a mountain can simultaneously be a natural landscape, a place of pilgrimage, an artistic object and a cultural symbol; UNESCO notes that Fujisan has for centuries been associated with religious practices that historically combine Shinto and Buddhist elements, and that the pilgrimage routes, shrines, inns and purification practices have gradually structured an entire sacred landscape around the mountain. (UNESCO World Heritage Centre)

The movement itself takes on a symbolic dimension: the pilgrims crossed different spaces of the volcano, performed ablutions and completed ritual circuits, while the ascent and descent could be understood in terms of purification, death and symbolic rebirth. (UNESCO World Heritage Centre)

Mount Sinai, in the biblical tradition, embodies another form of sacred verticality: the mountain becomes a place of revelation, a space where the distance between human and divine seems temporarily reorganized.

Altitude, however, does not mean the same thing in both cases.

It is a common error in articles on comparative spirituality to take five sacred mountains, mix them up and then conclude that they represent “exactly the same cosmic energy.”

No.

Their history differs.

Their traditions differ.

Their stories differ.

But they all use a particularly effective property of the mountain:

it transforms a physical movement into a symbolic experience.

You don’t just receive an idea.

You climb it.

Your breathing quickens.

The ground becomes difficult.

The temperature changes.

The noise from the houses disappears.

The symbolism is literally inscribed in the body.

4Why effort, silence and immensity actually change our mental state

It is not necessary to assume the existence of a specific summit energy to understand why a mountain can leave a deeply spiritual impression.

Imagine the contrast between your daily life and a climb.

In ordinary life:

  • notifications;
  • cars;
  • conversations;
  • screens;
  • schedules;
  • constant micro-decisions.

On a trail:

  • one foot in front of the other;
  • breathing;
  • slope;
  • wind;
  • distance.

This attentional simplification can produce a very powerful feeling of clarity.

Added to this is what contemporary psychology often calls awe, which could be described as wonder mixed with amazement: the experience occurs in particular before something so vast that it momentarily goes beyond our usual frameworks of understanding.

A giant mountain produces exactly this type of disproportion.

Your Tuesday morning work problem doesn’t go away.

But it suddenly changes scale.

This is perhaps one of the most interesting psychological mechanisms of meditation in nature: nature does not necessarily solve our problems, but it can temporarily reduce the place they occupy in our perceptual field.

The mountain also adds a physical constraint.

When your breathing is busy negotiating a fifteen percent incline, your brain has slightly less room to mentally replay an office discussion.

This is a particularly convincing form of anchoring.

5The sacred mountain is not just a summit: it is often a whole ritual landscape

We easily imagine that a sacred mountain means:

summit = sacred.

But many traditions instead structure a complete whole:

  • spring;
  • path;
  • forest;
  • shrine;
  • cave;
  • lake;
  • gate;
  • village;
  • forbidden area.

In the case of Fuji, for example, the World Heritage property includes different sites associated with religious practices and the symbolic landscape, not simply the crater at the summit. (UNESCO World Heritage Centre)

This idea is extremely interesting for modern contemplative practice, because it reminds us that transformation does not necessarily begin when we reach the highest point.

It sometimes starts at the moment of leaving.

The pilgrimage has precisely this structure:

  • preparation;
  • departure;
  • effort;
  • successive thresholds;
  • arrival;
  • return.

And the return counts.

A spiritual experience that changes nothing once you are back home can simply become a very nice interlude.

The real question is therefore not only:

“What did I feel up there?”

But:

“What do I bring back down?”

This is a question that mountains seem to have been asking for a very long time.

6Create your own “spiritual ascent” without going to Tibet

You obviously don’t need a plane ticket to reproduce certain mechanisms that make the mountain spiritually interesting.

Choose a hill, a trail, a sloping forest or even a long natural staircase, then set a very simple practice.

During the climb:

no phone.

Only one intention.

For example:

“What do I want to stop carrying needlessly?”

Each time the mind returns to a concern, come back to:

  • breathing;
  • feet;
  • slope;
  • sounds.

At the highest point, do nothing for five minutes.

No photo right away.

Look.

Then, before the descent, formulate a single concrete action to bring back into your daily life.

The descent then becomes important:

it symbolizes the return.

This practice does not belong to any particular tradition and does not claim to reproduce the rites of Kailash, Fuji or Sinai; it simply uses an extremely old and remarkably intuitive principle:

moving physically to temporarily change the way we think.

And that’s perhaps what mountains do best.

They don’t all ask us to believe the same thing.

Almost all of them ask us, in one way or another, to look up.

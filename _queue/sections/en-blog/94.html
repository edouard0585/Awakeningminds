1Shinrin-yoku is not a bath, and luckily you don't need to bring your towel

The Japanese term shinrin-yoku is generally translated as “forest bathing,” a fairly poetic phrase which can lead to confusion since no one is asking you to immerse yourself in a pond under the pines or to build a bathtub between two oaks; the idea is rather to spend time in a forest environment by intentionally slowing down, using several senses and momentarily leaving aside the usual logic of performance associated with hiking.

You are not necessarily looking for:

  • distance;
  • speed;
  • elevation;
  • records.

You are looking for:

  • presence;
  • sounds;
  • smells;
  • textures;
  • light;
  • breathing;
  • quiet movement.

The term was popularized in Japan in the early 1980s in the context of health policies and the promotion of forest environments, then it gradually gave birth to a whole field of research around forest therapy.

Since then, the claims have multiplied:

  • shinrin-yoku would reduce cortisol;
  • would lower blood pressure;
  • would strengthen immunity;
  • would improve mood;
  • would reduce anxiety;
  • would help you sleep;
  • would increase certain immune cells;
  • would probably fix your printer if the Internet is left to work on it for a few more years.

As is often the case in well-being, there is an interesting scientific base, then an immense zone of commercial hype built on top of it.

The right question is therefore:

what benefits are actually supported by the studies, and with what degree of certainty?

And the answer is encouraging, but much more nuanced than “trees heal everything.”

2Stress and mood: this is probably where the data are the most interesting

Several systematic reviews have found favorable effects of forest interventions on psychological measures such as anxiety, stress, mood or depressive symptoms.

A meta-analysis published in 2023 and covering 36 articles and 3,554 participants concluded that forest bathing was associated with reductions in anxiety and depression symptoms, while the physiological effects appeared less systematic. (PubMed)

Another 2021 meta-analysis covering twenty studies also found favorable effects on depression and anxiety, but the authors highlighted important methodological problems: several studies presented a risk of bias and the large majority were conducted in Korea, which limits generalization. (PubMed)

More recently, a meta-analysis published in 2026 included 25 studies and 1,876 participants and observed reductions in psychological stress, depressive and anxiety symptoms; however, heterogeneity between studies was significant and the quality of evidence was only moderate for stress and low for some emotional outcomes. (PubMed)

Translation into plain English:

yes, there is an interesting signal.

But:

no, we cannot yet speak of a green medicine whose effect would be perfectly measured.

The protocols differ greatly.

A twenty-minute walk.

Two hours in the forest.

An organized therapeutic program.

A stay of several days.

A group of patients.

Healthy adults.

All of these elements are sometimes grouped under the same word.

This explains part of the variability of the results.

3Cortisol, blood pressure and heart rate: possible effects, but not a universal prescription

Cortisol is probably the most famous molecule on the entire Internet when it comes to content about stress, to the point that it sometimes seems responsible for every human problem, ranging from fatigue to having lost your keys.

Concerning the forest, a systematic review and meta-analysis analyzed 22 studies and retained eight for quantitative synthesis; the authors observed salivary cortisol levels that were overall lower in forest environments than in urban environments, while emphasizing the limited number of studies, significant heterogeneity and the possibility of expectancy or placebo effects. (PubMed)

Concerning blood pressure, a meta-analysis of twenty trials totaling 732 participants found lower systolic and diastolic values in forest conditions than in non-forest conditions. (PubMed)

But the summaries are not perfectly consistent: a review of randomized trials published in 2022 did not find an overall significant effect on blood pressure in its analysis, while observing an effect on depression and emphasizing the low number of studies and the heterogeneity of interventions. (PubMed)

The 2026 meta-analysis on short-term cardiovascular responses reported an average decrease in heart rate of about four beats per minute in the studies included, with effects also observed on certain psychological scores. (PubMed)

The correct conclusion is therefore not:

“Walking for two hours among the fir trees treats hypertension.”

But:

“forest exposure can produce modest short-term physiological changes in some people, in a still heterogeneous data set.”

It's much less spectacular.

It's also much more serious.

4And the famous phytoncides? Interesting, but no need to imagine that each tree diffuses an invisible medicine

Phytoncides are volatile organic compounds emitted by plants, often mentioned to explain certain physiological effects associated with forest environments.

The idea is attractive:

  • you breathe the air of the forest;
  • trees release molecules;
  • these molecules produce biological effects.

There is indeed experimental work on different plant volatile compounds and on immune or physiological parameters, but the problem begins when this relatively specialized research is transformed into a general statement:

“breathing near trees boosts your immunity for X days.”

The reality depends in particular on:

  • the tree species;
  • the concentrations;
  • the season;
  • the temperature;
  • the exposure time;
  • the experimental context.

A large review of reviews published in 2021 concluded that forest interventions appeared promising for certain cardiovascular and mental health parameters, but it also showed that the quality of existing reviews varied greatly, ranging from a moderate to a very low level of confidence. (PubMed)

We must therefore resist the well-being reflex of transforming a possible mechanism into a therapeutic certainty.

Perhaps certain plant molecules contribute to certain effects.

But the forest simultaneously provides:

  • less noise;
  • less visual pollution;
  • physical activity;
  • natural light;
  • distance from work;
  • gentle attention;
  • a different temperature;
  • an aesthetic experience.

Isolating the “active ingredient” is much less easy than with a tablet.

And that's not necessarily a problem.

A forest has every right to be a complex environment.

5How to do a real forest bath without turning a walk into a military protocol

An interesting session can last between forty-five minutes and two hours, but there is no demonstrated universal magic duration that would automatically transform an ordinary walk into scientific shinrin-yoku.

Choose a safe place where you can walk slowly.

For the first few minutes, don't look for anything.

Walk.

Then choose several senses in succession.

Hearing:

identify the closest sound, then the farthest.

Sight:

look at the variations in light and depth.

Touch:

feel the temperature of the air or the texture of a bark, when it does not damage anything.

Smell:

just notice the smells of the ground, the wood, the humidity.

Then stop for five minutes.

No photos.

No talking.

No particular objective.

Let your attention shift.

This practice is a lot like mindfulness meditation in nature, but with an interesting difference: instead of gradually reducing the stimuli, you use a rich but relatively undemanding environment.

The goal is not to cover twelve kilometers.

You can walk one kilometer in an hour.

Which, for some people used to Strava, is probably the most difficult part of the whole experience.

6What the forest can really offer you — and what you had better not ask of it

Forest bathing has an extraordinary advantage over many well-being practices: even if its specific benefits were ultimately more modest than certain commercial claims, you will still have spent an hour walking peacefully in a natural space rather than looking at your phone.

The current data are sufficiently interesting to consider the forest as a potentially favorable environment for reducing stress and improving certain aspects of psychological well-being, but they do not allow shinrin-yoku to be presented as a universal treatment for anxiety, depression, hypertension or immune diseases. (PubMed)

If you suffer from severe depression, a disabling anxiety disorder or significant high blood pressure:

go to the forest if it makes you feel good.

But also see a doctor.

Trees have never asked to replace your doctor.

Their strength may lie elsewhere.

A forest forces you to change your time scale.

A tree has no notifications.

No Excel spreadsheets.

No urgency to respond.

It just stays there.

You walk in the middle.

And for a moment, your attentional system may stop believing that every minute must produce something.

In a society that turns even relaxation into a performance goal, this already constitutes a fairly radical form of therapy.

1The rattle: much more than a little drum you shake

In the Western imagination of shamanism, the drum takes centre stage. Yet the rattle — whether a maraca or some other shaken idiophone — also plays a major role in many traditions.

And contrary to a common belief, there is no single, universal "shamanic rattle".

The shapes vary enormously.

A gourd filled with seeds.

A carved wooden object.

A container holding stones or crystals.

An instrument decorated with feathers or animal figures.

Each tradition can give it its own function and symbolism.

The Metropolitan Museum, for instance, holds a Hiwi tsiitsiito from Colombia or Venezuela dating from the late 19th century. The museum describes it as the main instrument used by Hiwi healers for healing and divination. The object consists of an incised gourd, seeds, stones regarded as magical and feathers; in Hiwi ritual understanding, it helps the healer in trance call on spirits that can take the form of birds.

This is an excellent illustration of the difference between a material object and a ritual object.

Scientifically, we can analyse the wood, the seeds, the sound frequency and the movement.

Culturally, the rattle is invested with agency, history and spiritual relationships.

Reducing the instrument to "a sound that puts the brain into trance" would therefore erase half of what it means.

2An instrument for relating to spirits in several traditions

On the Northwest Coast of North America, some rattles feature particularly elaborate iconography.

The Metropolitan Museum, for example, holds raven rattles associated with the shamanic practices of Northwest Coast peoples. According to the museum's catalogue entry, these objects could be used during healing ceremonies and were associated with the relationship with a spirit guide. Their iconography could depict the transfer of power between different beings.

The Smithsonian also holds several rattles identified as Tlingit shamanic objects, including one dated to around 1850.

In the Sugpiaq/Alutiiq traditions of Alaska, a rattle documented by the Smithsonian even contained a small ritual bundle of quartz, mica, wood and hair. According to the ethnographic information associated with the object, it could be used to summon helping spirits and to accompany healing or divination.

These examples show something important: ritual power is not considered to come from the sound alone.

The shape, the materials, the figures depicted, the owner and the object's history also matter.

In some traditions, the rattle is practically a small cosmology you hold in your hand.

3Why repetitive sound works so well to support attention

A rattle generally creates a much richer and more irregular sound than a drum.

Each shake produces a multitude of tiny, closely spaced impacts.

This sound texture can easily capture attention and mask some of the surrounding noise.

In contemporary practice, this effect alone is enough to make the rattle worth exploring.

Simply try shaking an instrument slowly for a few minutes while listening carefully to the beginning and end of each sound.

Your attention starts to follow the texture.

The gesture becomes repetitive.

Breathing and movement can gradually fall into sync without any need to force anything.

This resembles certain forms of sound meditation: the auditory object provides a point of focus complex enough to stop the mind from drifting off too easily into other thoughts.

We should, however, avoid a common pseudo-scientific shortcut.

No particular type of rattle has been shown to "automatically raise the brain's vibration", objectively cleanse an energy field or open a neurological portal.

You can use these expressions symbolically or within a spiritual framework.

They simply shouldn't be presented as laboratory findings.

Sound works first and foremost as auditory stimulation, rhythm and an anchor for attention.

What the practitioner builds around it then depends on their belief system and experience.

4How to use a rattle in a personal practice without copying a specific ritual

For a contemporary session, start sitting or standing in a safe place.

Hold the rattle without gripping the handle too tightly.

For one minute, make a steady, moderate movement.

Listen to the sound the way you would listen to your breath during meditation.

Then, if balance isn't an issue, you can close your eyes and let the rhythm become slightly more continuous.

Set a very simple intention: calming your attention, accompanying a visualisation, marking the start of a personal ritual or supporting a chant.

You can also move the rattle around your body while keeping a clearly symbolic reading: you might imagine that you are "clearing the space", for example, while bearing in mind that no scientific measurement shows that an invisible energy field has just been removed.

This blend of symbolism and clear-headedness works very well.

A session can last five to fifteen minutes.

Finish by gradually slowing down until you reach silence.

Then stay still for a few moments.

The transition from sound to silence can itself become an excellent exercise in presence.

And unlike the drum, the rattle requires almost no musical technique.

Good news for anyone who has already managed to make a metronome lose the beat.

5Choosing or making your rattle: the relationship matters more than the decoration

A contemporary rattle can be extremely simple.

A small gourd, some wood or a well-balanced handmade instrument is more than enough.

The weight should feel comfortable.

Test the sound before buying whenever possible: some instruments produce a soft, sandy sound, others a much sharper attack.

For long sessions, wrist comfort matters more than lots of decoration.

Above all, avoid objects marketed as "sacred" replicas from specific cultures with no clear provenance.

Old Tlingit, Hiwi or Ye'kuana rattles belong to particular symbolic systems; buying an imitation and inventing the meaning of its figures is not the same as inheriting that tradition.

If you make your own instrument, you are perfectly free to give it a personal meaning.

Put seeds or small items inside that suit the sound you want.

Decorate it with symbols that belong to your own story.

You can even note each use in a journal so that the object gradually builds up a personal memory rather than a fake antiquity.

In Ye'kuana traditions, ritual maracas can be associated with mythical figures and a specific cosmology; an example held by the National Museum in Szczecin clearly shows how closely spiritual function, iconography and initiation are intertwined.

That is precisely the most interesting lesson.

An instrument does not become spiritual because a shop added the word "shamanic" to its description.

It becomes spiritual within a system of relationship, practice, story and intention.

The rattle really is the "other instrument" of shamanism, then, but not a miniature version of the drum.

Its rhythm, mobility and physical closeness create a very different quality.

Sometimes subtle, sometimes sharp, sometimes almost hypnotic, it can become an excellent tool for sound meditation and contemporary ritual.

And if you want to use it respectfully, one rule almost covers it all: know the history of traditional objects without automatically claiming to inherit their power.

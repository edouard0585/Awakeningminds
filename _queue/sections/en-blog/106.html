1Holotropic Breathwork: A Method Born From Transpersonal Psychology

Breathing seems like the least exotic activity in the world. We do it roughly twenty-four hours a day without booking a workshop, buying a drum or even thinking about it. Yet deliberately changing the speed and depth of your breathing can produce significant physical and subjective changes. That ability is the foundation of Holotropic Breathwork, or holotropic breathing, a practice developed in the 1970s by psychiatrist Stanislav Grof and Christina Grof.

The method belongs to the history of transpersonal psychology. Stanislav Grof had previously worked with LSD in an experimental psychiatric setting. After psychedelic research was gradually restricted, the Grofs developed a substance-free approach combining deliberately accelerated and deepened breathing, evocative music, a supervised setting and integration work after the experience. The word holotropic comes from Greek and, in their view, refers to a movement “toward wholeness.”

In Grof’s theoretical model, these sessions would give access to biographical, perinatal and transpersonal experiences. This is where a distinction becomes essential: these categories belong to the theoretical framework of transpersonal psychology, and not all of them are phenomena established by contemporary neuroscience. Someone can sincerely experience a scene they interpret as a birth memory, an archetypal experience or a spiritual encounter; the subjective reality of that experience doesn’t automatically prove the interpretation given to its origin.

So holotropic breathwork occupies an unusual position: it can generate powerful experiences and benefits from growing scientific interest in breathing techniques, but its most ambitious therapeutic claims are far less established than its marketing sometimes suggests.

2Why Breathing Much Faster Can Really Change How You Feel

To understand what’s happening, you have to drop a common idea: when someone hyperventilates, their main immediate issue is usually not “taking in huge amounts of oxygen.” In a healthy person, hemoglobin is already highly saturated with oxygen under normal conditions. Excessive breathing, however, gets rid of more carbon dioxide (CO₂) than the body produces.

The CO₂ concentration in the blood then drops: this is hypocapnia. Blood pH rises, producing respiratory alkalosis, and blood flow to the brain can decrease because of vasoconstriction. Sensations such as dizziness, tingling around the mouth or in the fingers, lightheadedness, a sense of unreality or cramping of the hands can appear.

A 2023 scientific review on the relationships between breathing, brain activity and mental states stresses that different breathing protocols — spontaneous breathing, slow breathing, hyperventilation or resonance breathing — don’t produce the same effects. The changes work in particular through CO₂, pH, cerebral blood flow, heart rate variability and the activity of neural networks.

This helps explain why intensive breathing can genuinely alter consciousness without immediately having to invoke a supernatural mechanism.

But the physiological explanation doesn’t cover the whole experience. A session also involves music, expectations, sustained attention to the body, social context, emotions, memories and sometimes several hours devoted to an unusual inner exploration. So it would be just as reductive to say: “it’s just CO₂.”

The phenomenon is probably multidimensional: respiratory physiology, cognition, emotion, context and interpretation all meet.

3Trance, Visions and Emotions: What Can Really Be Attributed to the Breath?

Accounts of holotropic breathwork vary enormously. Some participants describe intense emotions, memories, complex images, changes in how they perceive their body or time, feelings of unity, or experiences they interpret as spiritual. Others have a much less spectacular session.

The mistake would be to treat intensity as a measure of success.

An impressive experience isn’t necessarily therapeutic; a calm experience isn’t a failure. Likewise, an extremely vivid mental image isn’t automatically a historically accurate memory. Our brain is capable of producing convincing experiences out of memory, imagination, expectations and emotions.

Studies specifically on holotropic breathwork remain far fewer and methodologically weaker than research on some other psychological interventions. An assessment by NAFKAM, the Norwegian center for complementary medicine, highlights the uncertainty surrounding its therapeutic effectiveness and points out that an alternative practice should never delay necessary medical treatment.

So we need to distinguish four things: an intense experience, a feeling of improvement, a measurable therapeutic effect and a medical cure. They are not synonyms.

You can come out of a session deeply shaken and find immense personal meaning in it. That matters. But evidence-based medicine then asks whether that improvement is reproducible, better than an appropriate control, lasting and safe enough.

4The Risks Are Real: Hyperventilation Is Not Ordinary Relaxing Breathing

Holotropic breathwork should never be confused with a few minutes of slow breathing used to relax. It is an intensive practice that can cause significant physiological changes.

The contraindications usually mentioned include certain cardiovascular diseases, a history of aneurysm or stroke, epilepsy or seizure disorders, glaucoma, retinal detachment, recent surgery, pregnancy and certain severe psychiatric conditions. NAFKAM also advises particular caution regarding asthma, osteoporosis and significant medical treatments.

The psychological side deserves just as much attention. An intense experience can bring up fear, traumatic memories or a sense of losing control. For someone with a history of psychosis, mania, severe dissociation or complex trauma, deliberately seeking out an extremely destabilizing state without appropriate supervision can be a very bad strategy.

And above all: never practice an intense hyperventilation technique in water, while driving, standing in a situation where a fall would be dangerous, or in any context where losing consciousness could cause an accident.

The breath is free, of course. That doesn’t mean every way of breathing is harmless.

5How to Explore the Link Between Breath and Consciousness Without Turning Your Living Room Into a Lab

Someone curious about the relationship between breathing and mental state absolutely doesn’t need to start with the most intense form.

You can begin by observing five minutes of natural breathing. Then experiment with slightly slower, comfortable breathing, with no forced breath-holding and no hyperventilation. That already makes it possible to study something fascinating: how simply paying attention to the breath changes bodily perception.

For holotropic breathwork itself, guidance from a serious, trained facilitator who is transparent about contraindications and able to refer you to a health professional when needed is far preferable to improvised experimentation.

After an intense experience, integration matters too. Rather than immediately turning every image into absolute truth, you can write down what happened, distinguish sensation, memory, symbol and interpretation, then see what still feels meaningful a few days later.

In the end, holotropic breathwork occupies fascinating territory between physiology, transpersonal psychology and spirituality. Science confirms that breathing can profoundly influence brain activity, physiology and mental experience. It does not, however, confirm every transpersonal map built around these states.

The breath can open a door onto an unusual inner experience. But as with any door leading into a room you don’t know well, walking in gently is usually smarter than breaking it down with your shoulder.

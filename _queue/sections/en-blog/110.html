1Why Writing Right After a Trance Completely Changes the Quality of the Memory

A trance experience can feel extraordinarily clear in the moment. You tell yourself you will never forget that forest you traveled through in your mind, that feeling of warmth in your chest, that sentence you heard inwardly or that animal that appeared around a bend in an imaginary landscape. Then the next morning comes, and the once-vivid scene already feels like a dream recounted in the rain: a few fragments remain, the rest dissolves. That is precisely why a trance journal can become one of the most useful tools in a regular practice.

Writing soon after the experience creates an external record of what was recalled at that precise moment. It then lets you compare sessions without relying entirely on retrospective memory. That matters, because human memory is not a video recording. Contemporary models of autobiographical memory describe it as a constructive and reconstructive process: when we remember, we assemble various elements connected to the event, to our personal knowledge, to our current goals and to how we interpret ourselves. Conway and Pleydell-Pearce’s Self-Memory System model is one of the major references on this question.

That doesn’t mean our memories are false. It means they are not perfectly frozen archives.

For a trance practitioner, this nuance is precious. An image experienced very intensely can remain meaningful without being treated as objective testimony of an external event. The notebook then becomes a personal laboratory: it preserves the experience as it was recalled, before several days of reflection, reading or conversations potentially reshape it.

2The Golden Rule: Separate What You Experienced From What You Think It Means

A good journal page ideally has two clearly separated levels.

The first is descriptive.

“I saw a large white stag. It was to the left of a river. I felt strong warmth in my belly. I had the impression of hearing the word ‘wait.’”

The second is interpretive.

“For me, the stag might symbolize patience. I wonder whether the river represents a transition.”

The difference seems slight, but it offers enormous protection against later reconstructions.

Immediately writing “My spirit guide ordered me to wait three months before changing jobs” already turns an inner experience into a factual conclusion. Yet what actually happened may be far more modest: a word was heard or imagined in an unusual state of consciousness, then interpreted.

This caution is especially important because emotional memories can be very vivid without being perfectly accurate. Research on autobiographical memory shows that the confidence we feel and the vividness of a memory do not guarantee its accuracy. A review on emotion and autobiographical memory notes in particular that even memories of striking events can change over time or become inconsistent with accounts recorded earlier.

A journal is exactly what lets you see these transformations.

If, three weeks later, you are convinced the stag spoke to you for five minutes, you can go back to the note written right afterward: “I had the impression of hearing a word.”

This small safeguard keeps a symbolic scene from gradually turning into an epic officially certified by your own memory.

3A Simple Structure That Makes the Journal Truly Useful

A trance journal page can start with very concrete information: date, time, length of the session, place, fatigue, mood, method used and any particular factors such as music, prior meditation or a recent meal.

Next, note how you entered the experience. Was it quick or gradual? Did you use a visualization, a repetitive sound, normal breathing, movement or another technique? Were you very focused or constantly distracted?

Then comes the main account. Write freely, but try to include the following five categories: images, bodily sensations, emotions, thoughts or words, and perception of time.

Finish with three very simple questions: “What surprised me?”, “What keeps coming back compared with previous sessions?” and “What don’t I understand yet?”

That last question is deliberately important.

A good journal doesn’t need to resolve everything that appears right away.

Modern spirituality sometimes suffers from a strange instant-subtitles syndrome: no sooner does an owl appear in a visualization than the internet has to explain exactly what it “means.” Yet a personal symbol can vary enormously depending on memories, culture and context.

So leave some elements untranslated.

After ten or twenty sessions, reread the whole thing. You will then be able to spot patterns far more interesting than anything drawn from a single experience: a recurring landscape, an emotion that always appears at the start, a gradual improvement in concentration, or, on the contrary, a technique that mostly produces agitation.

That is when the journal becomes a tool for progress rather than a collection of fantastic tales.

4Does Writing Help Psychologically? Sometimes, but Not in the Simplistic Way Often Claimed

Introspective writing has long been the subject of research, notably through the expressive writing paradigm associated with James Pennebaker. In these experiments, participants generally write over several sessions about significant emotional experiences.

The early research produced encouraging results. Later meta-analyses, however, strongly tempered the enthusiasm. An analysis of thirty randomized trials of expressive writing about stressful or traumatic experiences found no significant overall benefits on psychological or physical health outcomes, apart from a few very small effects in some exploratory analyses. Another systematic review had also concluded that there was no clear, general improvement that would justify treating emotional writing as some kind of universal treatment.

So it would be going too far to claim that a trance journal “heals” or automatically improves mental health.

Its main value lies elsewhere: documenting, structuring, comparing and reflecting.

For some people, writing also helps put an emotional experience into words. For others, it can instead increase rumination. If you notice that you are obsessively rereading every session looking for a hidden message, or that the journal is increasing your anxiety, cut back on how often you write and return to more factual descriptions.

A spiritual tool should help you live more clearly, not turn every Tuesday evening into a forty-seven-page paranormal investigation.

5How to Use Your Notes to Progress Without Becoming a Prisoner of Your Own Stories

After several weeks, the goal is not to count how many extraordinary visions you’ve had. A practice can progress even as the experiences become less spectacular.

Look instead for observable indicators: do you find it easier to sustain your attention? Do you come back more calmly? Do some techniques produce more confusion than others? Do the experiences remain integrated into your everyday life?

You can also give a short subjective rating from zero to ten on a few dimensions: felt depth, stability, emotion, clarity of the memory and quality of the return. These numbers are not scientific measurements of trance; they only serve to compare your own impressions over time.

Also keep a section called “alternative hypotheses.” An encounter with a luminous figure can be interpreted spiritually, symbolically, psychologically or as a mental imagery phenomenon. Nothing forces you to choose right away.

This stance is particularly important if traumatic memories surface. Recent work on hypnosis and memory reminds us that guided imagery and certain forms of regression can increase the risk of false memories or mistaken reconstructions, especially when the context strongly encourages a specific interpretation. A personal notebook should therefore never be used as proof that a scene experienced in trance really happened in childhood.

If significant traumatic material emerges, it’s better to talk about it with a trauma-trained professional than to try on your own to “recover” more memories.

In the end, a good trance journal isn’t meant to turn inner experience into certainty. It does almost the opposite: it preserves enough detail to let your curiosity stay open.

And after several months, the real treasure sometimes isn’t the spectacular revelation written in capital letters. It’s being able to look over a hundred pages and see how your attention, your symbols, your fears and your way of making meaning have evolved.

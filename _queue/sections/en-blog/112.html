1The word “shaman” comes from Siberia, but that doesn't mean all of Siberia shares a single religion

The word shaman is now used so widely that, in a single conversation, it can refer to a Siberian ritual specialist, an Amazonian healer, a Korean practitioner, a prehistoric figure or someone running a weekend workshop in the mountains. Historically, though, the term has a much more precise origin.

Linguists generally trace it back to the Tungusic languages, especially Evenki. The form šamán is attested in this language family and was picked up by Russians in contact with the Indigenous peoples of Siberia. Western Europeans then came across the term in travel and exploration accounts from the 17th and 18th centuries. Historical sources notably mention the Dutch traveler Nicolaes Witsen, whose Noord en Oost Tartarye, published at the end of the 17th century, helped spread descriptions of Siberian ritual specialists across Europe.

The popular etymology translating šaman as “one who knows” is often repeated, but it is linguistically disputed. Some analyses point out that the proposed link with a root meaning “to know” is not as phonologically straightforward as you sometimes read.

So the first lesson of Siberian shamanism is a paradox: Siberia gave us a word that the West then applied everywhere.

Yet Siberia itself is home to very different peoples: the Evenki, the Sakha or Yakuts, the Buryats, the Tuvans, the Khakas, the Nenets, the Chukchi and many others, who speak a variety of languages and have their own histories and ritual specialists.

Talking about “Siberian shamanism” is handy as a comparative category. Presenting it as a uniform religion would be historically misleading.

2What did a Siberian shaman traditionally do?

Their roles differed from one people to another, but the classic ethnographies describe several recurring themes: healing, divination, communicating with spirits, leading community rites, searching for the spiritual causes of certain illnesses and mediating between humans and non-human powers.

In some cosmologies, the universe is depicted as several levels: an upper world, the human world and a lower world. The shaman may be described as traveling between these realms during a ritual trance.

It's important to phrase this correctly: in the traditional understanding, the shaman's soul or consciousness really does travel. Anthropologists can document this belief, the structure of the narrative and the behaviors associated with it. Modern science, however, has not established that consciousness objectively travels to another, invisible world.

The drum often plays an important role. It can set the rhythm of the ritual, symbolically represent a mount or serve as an object closely tied to the practitioner's identity. But here again, diversity is key. Not every religious specialist from every Siberian people works in the same way, and the popular Western model of “drum + journey through three worlds” simplifies several centuries of ethnography.

In Shamans: Siberian Spirituality and the Western Imagination, Ronald Hutton highlights precisely how often the Western image of the shaman has been reshaped to fit European concerns: a diabolical sorcerer for some early observers, a “primitive” survival for others, and then the keeper of a universal spiritual wisdom for part of the modern counterculture.

The historical shaman sometimes vanishes behind the mirror the West holds up in front of him.

3The shaman's calling: initiation, illness and transformation

Many Siberian ethnographies describe the future shaman as someone who doesn't necessarily choose the role the way you'd pick a career path.

Some accounts speak of a calling, sometimes accompanied by illness, unusual dreams, crises or a feeling of being chosen by spirits or by a family lineage. Within the cultural system in question, these ordeals can be interpreted as an initiation.

In Shamanism: Archaic Techniques of Ecstasy, Mircea Eliade did much to popularize the motif of the future shaman's symbolic death and rebirth: visions of dismemberment, transformation of the skeleton, reconstruction of the body or acquisition of new spiritual organs.

These accounts do exist in some ethnographic sources, but two extrapolations should be avoided.

First, they are not an identical, universal experience shared by every people.

Second, a contemporary psychological crisis should not automatically be called a “shamanic initiation.” Someone experiencing psychosis, seizures or a dissociative disorder first deserves a diagnosis suited to their current medical context.

In a traditional society, interpreting the calling is part of a network of specialists, myths, rules and community recognition. That framework can't be reproduced simply by saying: “I went through a hard time, so I'm a shaman.”

What may look similar at the level of individual experience doesn't necessarily carry the same social meaning.

4Drum, costume and animals: a highly sophisticated symbolic technology

Siberian shamanic garments and instruments are among the most spectacular religious objects documented by ethnography. Some outfits feature metal, pendants, animal figures, mirrors, fringes or elements interpreted as protective.

The drum isn't simply a percussion instrument. In several traditions, it can be symbolically conceived as a horse, reindeer or vehicle that allows the shaman to travel. Images of the tree, the mountain or the vertical axis also appear in various Siberian cosmologies.

This rich iconography explains why Siberian traditions fed so heavily into the comparative theories of the 20th century.

But the danger arises when every symbol is turned into a universal code.

A bird depicted on a costume doesn't automatically serve the same function as a bird in an Amazonian or North American tradition. Similarities exist, but context determines meaning.

Religious studies therefore distinguishes comparisons from genealogies. Two cultures can have similar rituals without one having passed it on to the other or the two sharing an identical doctrine.

The drum can also contribute to absorption, movement and the regularity of the ritual, but that doesn't prove the existence of a single musical frequency capable of objectively opening a “portal.”

Symbol and physiology can meet without becoming the same thing.

5Soviet suppression, contemporary revival and the global legacy of a word that grew enormous

The 20th century profoundly upended Siberia's Indigenous religions. Under the Soviet regime, many ritual specialists were persecuted; religious practices were discouraged or banned, objects were destroyed or confiscated and communities were transformed by collectivization, forced settlement and assimilation policies. Historical overviews of the Evenki and other peoples recall this period of repression, followed by forms of religious revival after the end of the Soviet Union.

Since the 1990s, various regions have seen revivals: renewed ceremonies, religious organizations, the rebuilding of teachings and the reclaiming of identity. But here again, we should avoid the romantic image of a tradition kept perfectly intact under a glass dome for seventy years and then brought back out unchanged.

Living traditions change.

Some contemporary practices rest on family transmission; others draw on ethnographic archives; some incorporate Buddhist, Orthodox, nationalist or neo-shamanic elements.

Meanwhile, the word shaman has had a global career. Anthropologists such as Andrei Znamenski have shown how it was moved from its Siberian context into a global category, and then into Western spiritual movements.

Understanding Siberian shamanism therefore calls for a twofold attention: respecting the particular practices of the peoples who produced these traditions, while studying how the West turned their name into a universal concept.

Perhaps the most beautiful paradox is this one: to get back to the roots of the word “shaman,” you first have to stop believing it has the same roots everywhere.

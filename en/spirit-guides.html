Spirit guides: who they are and how to connect with yours

A wise presence at your side, an animal that keeps appearing, a grandmother you still feel near: many people sense they are not walking alone. Here is what spirit guides are in different traditions, and how to connect with yours in meditation.

In short

Spirit guides are presences believed to accompany, protect and advise a person on their life path. Depending on the tradition, they take the form of guardian angels, animal spirits, ancestors, ascended masters or the higher self. Some see them as beings in their own right, others as wise parts of the psyche. Both views can meet in meditation.

What are spirit guides?

The idea of a guiding presence is found in almost every culture. It answers a simple human feeling: that help, wisdom or protection can come from somewhere beyond our everyday mind.

The phrase spirit guide became common in nineteenth-century Spiritualism, where mediums spoke of guides who helped them contact the beyond. Today, it is used broadly for any presence that offers spiritual guidance, whatever its form.

The intuition itself is much older. In ancient Athens, Socrates spoke of his daimonion, an inner sign that warned him when he was about to make a mistake. Many traditions since have given this inner wisdom a face and a name.

Guardian angels, ancestors, ascended masters: guides across traditions

Each tradition gives guides a different face. Here are the main ones.

  • Guardian angel

    In Christian tradition, each person is believed to be accompanied by an angel. The Gospel of Matthew speaks of children's angels in heaven, and the Catholic Church celebrates guardian angels every October 2.

  • Ancestors

    In many African, Asian and Indigenous traditions, the family's dead continue to watch over the living, and are honored with offerings and remembrance.

  • Helping spirits

    In shamanism, the shaman works with allies met in trance, often in animal form. They protect, teach and guide the journey.

  • Ascended masters

    A notion from Theosophy and its heirs: enlightened teachers said to have lived on Earth and to guide humanity from beyond. The I AM movement spread the phrase in the 1930s.

  • Higher self

    A wiser, deeper part of yourself, described in Theosophy and in many modern spiritual paths. Here, the guide is not outside you but within.

  • Inner guide

    In psychology, a wise inner figure you meet through imagination. Carl Jung described his conversations with such a figure, whom he called Philemon.

Buddhism: bodhisattvas, Guanyin and Tara as guides

Buddhism was founded about 2,500 years ago in northern India by Siddhartha Gautama, known as Gautama Buddha. Buddha means “awakened one,” and tradition says he reached awakening beneath the Bodhi tree at Bodh Gaya. The core beliefs of Buddhism are the Four Noble Truths and the Noble Eightfold Path, a middle way between indulgence and harsh self-denial.

Theravada Buddhism, found in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia, is the home of vipassana, or insight meditation. Mahayana Buddhism spread to China, Korea, Vietnam and Japan, where Zen Buddhism grew from Chinese Chan. Vajrayana, or Tibetan Buddhism, developed in the Himalayas.

Buddhism has no spirit guides in the Western sense, yet bodhisattvas play a similar role in prayer and meditation. A bodhisattva seeks awakening for the sake of all beings. In Mahayana, this is the highest ideal.

Avalokiteśvara, the bodhisattva of compassion, is known in China as Guanyin, also written Guan Yin, Kuan Yin or Kwan Yin. Tara, revered in Tibet, is said to protect from eight great dangers. Maitreya is the Buddha to come. The Buddhist Meditations category follows them in eight sessions of 20 to 30 minutes, from The Eleven Faces of Avalokiteśvara to Tārā and the Eight Great Dangers.

Are spirit guides real? Two ways to see them

Whether spirit guides exist independently is a question of belief, and no experiment can settle it. What is certain is that many people experience them, and that these experiences can be deeply meaningful.

Psychology offers another reading. Carl Jung developed a method he called active imagination. You let an inner figure appear, then speak with it as if it were real, while staying awake and aware. In this view, a guide is a part of the psyche that knows more than your daily thoughts.

You do not have to choose. In meditation, you can welcome a guide with an open mind. Then judge it by one simple question: does this help me live with more clarity and kindness? The Keeper of the Archives, in Higher Knowledge & Beyond, trains this discernment, between what the mind invents and what seems to arise on its own.

Animal spirit guides: how to find your spirit animal

Animal spirit guides are among the most widespread. In shamanic traditions, the power animal protects and teaches. Many Indigenous peoples of North America also know totem animals, linked to a person or a clan. The word totem itself comes from Ojibwe.

The phrase spirit animal comes from these Indigenous traditions, and its casual use in pop culture can feel disrespectful to them. Many people now prefer animal guide or power animal. In the app, the guide type is called Spirit Animal.

To find your animal guide in meditation, set the intention, travel to a natural place in imagination and notice which animal approaches. Pay attention to animals that return in your dreams and journeys. The Wolf Spirit Forest is built around this kind of encounter, and our page on shamanic trance explains the tradition behind it.

Signs from spirit guides: what people describe

People who feel guided often describe the same kinds of signs. Here are the most common, with a gentle reminder to keep both your heart and your mind open. The Journal's Signs & Synchronicities notebook is made to collect them.

  • Repeated encounters: a bird, an animal or a symbol that keeps appearing.
  • Synchronicities: meaningful coincidences, like hearing the answer to a question you just asked yourself. Jung coined the word.
  • A sudden inner knowing, a calm certainty that arrives without reasoning.
  • Dreams in which a guide speaks, or a figure that returns night after night.
  • Physical sensations: warmth, a light touch, a feeling of presence.
Write signs down as they happen, then look back after a few weeks. A real pattern holds up over time; a forced one fades.

How to connect with your spirit guides in meditation

There are many ways of connecting with spirit guides: prayer, dreams, journaling, walks in nature. Meditation offers a simple, gentle structure. Here is how to meet your spirit guide, step by step. If building the safe place feels hard, our guide to safe place visualization can help. For fully guided versions, the Spirit Guides category offers five sessions of 16 to 26 minutes.

  1. Go to your safe place

    Close your eyes, breathe slowly and imagine a place where you feel completely at ease: a garden, a temple, a shore.

  2. Set an intention

    Silently state why you have come: to meet a guide who wishes you well.

  3. Welcome

    Notice who or what approaches. It may be a person, an animal, a light or simply a feeling of presence. Greet it with respect.

  4. Ask one simple question

    Keep it clear and open, such as: what do I need to know right now? Then listen, without forcing an answer.

  5. Thank

    Whatever came, even silence, thank your guide. Gratitude closes the meeting gently.

  6. Return

    Walk back the way you came, feel your body and the room, and open your eyes. Write down what you received.

If a figure feels hostile or frightening, you are free to end the meditation, ground yourself and return another day. If such experiences weigh on you or repeat, ask a professional for advice.

Keeping a record of your guides

Guides tend to reveal themselves over time. One meeting gives you an impression; several meetings give you a relationship. That is why writing things down matters.

Note their name, photo, type, appearance, purpose and symbols, along with the messages you received and the questions you asked. Log each return in Reappearances. If a guide gives you something in meditation, such as a stone, a key or a word, keep it in Gifts & objects with New gift.

Nothing is written for you: it is your own record of encounters, kept on your phone. Signs that come in daily life go into the Signs & Synchronicities notebook.

Frequently asked questions

What are spirit guides?

Spirit guides are presences believed to accompany and advise a person through life. Traditions describe them as guardian angels, ancestors, animal spirits, ascended masters or the higher self. Some people see them as independent beings, others as wise parts of the psyche. In meditation, you can meet them with an open mind, whichever view you hold.

How do I connect with my spirit guides?

Find a quiet moment, close your eyes and imagine a safe, peaceful place. Set the intention to meet a guide who wishes you well, then notice who or what approaches. Ask one simple question and listen without forcing. Thank your guide, return slowly and write down what you received. Repeating the practice helps the connection feel familiar.

How do I know what my spirit animal is?

In shamanic practice, your animal guide shows itself during a journey or a meditation, often several times. You can also notice animals that recur in your dreams, that you feel drawn to, or that cross your path in striking ways. Out of respect for the Indigenous traditions the term comes from, many people say animal guide or power animal.

What are signs from spirit guides?

People commonly describe repeated animals or symbols, meaningful coincidences, a sudden calm inner knowing, recurring dream figures, or a sense of warmth and presence. None of these can be proven, and the mind loves patterns. A good approach is to note signs as they come, then look back after a few weeks to see what really holds.

What is the higher self?

The higher self is a wiser, deeper part of your own being, described in Theosophy and many modern spiritual paths. It is said to see your life with more clarity and love than your everyday mind. Connecting with it usually means quieting down and listening inwardly. In this view, your most trusted guide is not outside you, but within.

What are ascended masters?

In Theosophy and the movements that followed it, ascended masters are enlightened teachers believed to have lived on Earth, then risen beyond it to guide humanity. The phrase was popularized by the I AM movement in the 1930s, in which Saint Germain plays a central role. It is a belief system, with its own texts and followers.

Are spirit guides real?

No scientific method can prove or disprove that spirit guides exist. Believers see them as real beings, while psychology often sees them as inner figures that express a deeper wisdom. Both views agree on one point: the experience can be meaningful. Judge a guide by its fruits. Does it leave you calmer, clearer and kinder?

What is a bodhisattva?

A bodhisattva is a being who seeks awakening not only for themselves, but for the sake of all beings. In Mahayana Buddhism, it is the highest ideal. Some bodhisattvas, such as Avalokiteśvara, Mañjuśrī or Tara, are revered as figures of compassion, wisdom and protection. In meditation, many people picture them as inner teachers, close to what others call spirit guides.

Who is Guanyin?

Guanyin is the Chinese name of Avalokiteśvara, the bodhisattva of compassion in Mahayana Buddhism. The name means “the one who perceives the cries of the world.” In East Asia, Guanyin is usually shown as a woman, often holding a vase or a willow branch. In Japan, the same figure is called Kannon, and in Tibet, Chenrezig. You will also see the spellings Guan Yin, Kuan Yin and Kwan Yin.

What is the difference between a guardian angel and an archangel?

In Christian tradition, a guardian angel is a personal protector who accompanies one person through life. Archangels hold a higher rank and carry great missions. The Bible names Michael and Gabriel, and the Book of Tobit names Raphael. Popular spirituality sometimes calls an archangel a guardian, but classic teaching keeps the two roles apart.
